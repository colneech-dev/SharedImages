## Chapter One: The Line North

The last train north was almost empty.

Mara Hallowell had chosen a seat facing backward. She liked watching places recede. It made leaving feel like a decision instead of a failure.

Rain combed the window into silver lines. Beyond it, the land opened and closed in folds of wet green: sheep behind stone walls, low cottages with smoke flattened by the wind, a stand of pines dark as ink against the sky. Every few minutes the train rounded a bend and the carriage shuddered. The sound travelled through the floor and up the bones of Mara’s legs.

She kept one hand on her rucksack.

It was canvas, olive drab, and older than the woman who had sold it to her in a second-hand shop in Inverness. Mara had stitched one strap where it had begun to split. Inside were two sweaters, a rain jacket, a torch with spare batteries, a folding knife, a first-aid kit, a tin of biscuits, a water bottle, and a leather notebook. The notebook had belonged to someone else first. Its first three pages were filled with narrow, sloping handwriting that Mara could not read. She had tried, once, to decipher it by the light of a hostel lamp. The ink had blurred and the letters seemed to change shape as she watched. She had stopped before she could convince herself the book was bewitched.

The fourth page was hers.

**HOGWARTS — OUTER APPROACH. DO NOT FOLLOW THE OLD ROAD.**

Beneath that, in smaller capitals:

**SNAPE: POTIONS ROOMS? OFFICE? SLYTHERIN SPACES? LOOK FOR ANNOTATIONS, NOT LABELS.**

She had underlined the last sentence twice.

A child across the aisle stared at the notebook. He wore a yellow raincoat and had the solemn, unblinking expression children sometimes gave strangers who looked tired. His mother slept with her head against the glass. When the train dipped through a tunnel, the child’s reflection seemed to linger in the window after the carriage had gone dark.

Mara shut the notebook.

She had not come straight from Connecticut to the Highlands. The crossing had taken her first from New York to Liverpool on a crowded passenger ship, where she spent six nights listening to the Atlantic strike the hull. She had booked the cheapest berth she could find, telling herself she wanted time to think. Mostly she had lain awake, listening to strangers cough in the dark and feeling the ship roll beneath her as if the ocean were trying to shake her loose from the decision.

In Liverpool she bought a rail ticket north. The journey had taken most of a day, changing trains twice and passing through stations whose names she had practised saying under her breath. She had chosen the railway because it was ordinary, because old timetables and local maps were easier to follow than rumors of magical routes, and because she did not trust any story that promised a shortcut to Hogwarts. If the last train stopped short, she would continue on foot. That had been the plan. Plans, she was learning, were most useful before the place began answering back.

At a station café outside Glasgow, she had overheard two elderly men speaking about the war. They sat with their coats still on, each holding a tea gone cold. One said the authorities had told people to stay indoors. The other corrected him: “They told us to leave. Then they denied there’d been anything to leave from.” Their conversation faltered when Mara looked over. One of them noticed the map folded beside her cup.

“Going up that way?” he asked.

“Visiting,” she had replied.

The old man gave a short laugh, without humour. “Nobody visits that place.”

His companion leaned closer to him. “My sister saw the lights the night the school fell. Blue ones. Up over the ridge. She said people were walking out of the air, carrying children. The papers called it a fuel depot explosion.”

“Your sister said a lot of things,” the first man muttered.

“She was there.”

“And she never went back.”

They had fallen silent after that. Mara had not asked them to say more. She had learned that people who had lived through a thing often guarded its edges more fiercely than its centre.

On the last leg north, the train passed through stretches where the land seemed to have been abandoned in a hurry. At one siding, a torn newspaper had caught in the bushes beside the track. It fluttered as the train went by, and she saw a headline printed in cracked black type: **HIGHLAND EVACUATIONS CONTINUE — OFFICIALS DENY SCHOOL SITE EXISTS**. The date was almost unreadable. A second line, partly hidden by leaves, began **FAMILIES ASK WHERE CHILDREN…** before the paper tore away.

She had seen other fragments during the journey: a sheet pasted to a wall in a closed station, its headline reduced to **MISSING PERSONS**; a yellowed clipping in the window of a pub, reporting that a “chemical incident” had forced the evacuation of three villages; a scrap in a ditch with the words **NO CASUALTIES CONFIRMED** printed above a photograph of a ruined stone arch. Each account contradicted the next. None mentioned wizards. None explained why the dates and locations matched the scraps she had collected about Hogwarts.

She had first heard the name Hogwarts in a recording with a broken beginning. The speaker, an elderly man with a voice like paper tearing, described “a school in the north” and “a night of fire.” The clip had been uploaded to a little-known archive, copied by a user whose account vanished a few weeks later. There had been no proof, only a list of names and a photograph too blurred to identify anyone. The one name she recognized was Severus Snape.

After that she searched more carefully. She learned to follow footnotes, missing pages, and the parts of official documents where a sentence had been crossed out but not quite erased. The wizarding world had been hidden from Muggles for centuries. Then governments learned enough to be afraid of it—or claimed they had. A series of leaks and unexplained events had become calls for registration. Registration had led to raids. Raids had led to resistance, and resistance to open conflict.

The war lasted longer than the public record admitted. Its dates shifted depending on the account. Some sources insisted it began in London; others pointed to a laboratory fire in the United States, where a spell had been mistaken for a weapon. The most credible fragments suggested there had been no single beginning. There had been a chain of frightened decisions: a Muggle official trying to reveal magic before a rival government could; a witch refusing arrest; a curse answered by gunfire; a family dragged from a house because someone believed their child could be used to find others.

The first time Mara read a transcript of a wizarding broadcast, she had expected the words to sound theatrical. They did not. The speaker had been trying to keep a group of children quiet while shells fell somewhere nearby. She had spoken in a low, patient voice about packing warm clothes, holding hands, and not looking through the windows.

Mara remembered the line more clearly than the rest: **When the adults say the way is safe, wait until they have gone through it first.**

Hogwarts had become a refuge for people fleeing the fighting. It was an old school, protected by enchantments that ordinary maps could not show. The castle sheltered families and students until its protections were worn down by repeated attacks—some magical, some Muggle, and some impossible to distinguish after the fact. One witness wrote of a flash that rose from the grounds without heat. Another described stones lifting into the air as if the castle had tried to walk away.

The people inside escaped by hidden passages, emergency Portkeys, and routes that had been built for a different kind of danger. Some were moved abroad. Others hid in Muggle towns under false names. A few accounts mentioned groups of witches and wizards still resisting from safe houses, but the reports stopped after the war. The surviving magical community withdrew from the region. Whether they left voluntarily, were driven out, or were carried away by a magic no Muggle could follow, Mara could not determine.

The authorities sealed the area for a time. Then the cordons thinned. The records became confused. A new road was planned, then cancelled. A village was evacuated and never officially resettled. A railway station remained open years after the last scheduled service, then closed without explanation. The castle was left alone.

The castle remained, with whatever its people had left behind.

Mara’s reflection watched her from the glass. Thirty-one years old. Dark hair tied back. A line between her eyebrows that had begun appearing in her twenties and stayed. She looked like someone who had made sensible choices and regretted each one.

The child across the aisle had stopped staring. He was asleep now, his small hand curled around the cuff of his mother’s sleeve.

Mara turned away from them.

She had not crossed the Atlantic for a ghost story. She had come because all her life she had been close to doors that opened for other people. Her mother had been practical, her father brilliant in the way that made every argument sound like a lecture. In Connecticut, their house stood on a quiet street with maples along the pavement and a narrow back step that went cold before autumn was over. There had been no great tragedy in Mara’s childhood, nothing anyone else would have called unbearable. Just a thousand small moments in which she learned that being reasonable meant accepting what she had been given.

When her father talked about the future, he meant the one he had planned for her. When her mother told her to be grateful, she meant that the world was not obliged to offer more. Mara had been good at school, careful with money, and sensible about jobs. She had learned how to make herself agreeable in rooms where nobody had asked her to speak.

At sixteen, she began going to the library after school instead of coming straight home. At nineteen, she took a job in a used-book shop and read about people who had escaped their circumstances by becoming something else: explorers, spies, inventors, witches. The witches interested her most. They did not wait for permission. They held a wand and made the world answer.

She knew that was a childish way to understand magic. She knew there were wizards who had been poor, frightened, trapped, or cruel. She knew magic did not make anyone free by itself. But knowing something and wanting it were not the same.

The train slowed. A station sign appeared through the rain, its white letters nearly erased by lichen. Mara stood, pulled down her rucksack, and waited for the doors to open.

The platform was empty.

A wind moved through the roof of the station building, stirring the dead leaves piled against its boarded windows. An old timetable case hung open. Inside it, a moth lay on its back beside a scrap of yellow paper. The ink had dissolved, leaving only a few dark strokes. Mara stepped closer but could not make out a word.

The train gave a warning chime. She stepped down onto the platform. The doors closed behind her. For a second the carriage windows framed her in warm light; then the train pulled away, gathered speed, and disappeared around a bend.

Silence settled over the station.

Mara took out her phone. No signal. Her downloaded map showed a road running west, a thin blue line for a stream, and a wide blank area where the hills rose. The railway was not marked beyond the station. She had expected that. The map she trusted most was the hand-drawn one in her notebook, copied from a page in a book about the final evacuation. Its author had called the track “a service line” and marked the last station with a small black cross.

Mara had circled the cross and written **start walking**.

She followed the rails north.

The sleepers were slick beneath her boots. Grass pushed between them, and birch saplings crowded the embankment. She had thought the walk would feel like an adventure. Instead, she was aware of each practical discomfort: wet cuffs, the strap rubbing her collarbone, the ache beginning in her right knee. She stopped twice to drink and once to check the map. The hills offered no landmarks she recognized.

After twenty minutes she found a metal post beside the line, bent nearly double. A red warning lamp had been bolted to its top, its glass long gone. Someone had scratched a mark into the metal beneath it: a circle cut by three short strokes.

Mara photographed it and copied it into her notebook.

It might have been a railway mark. It might have been a warning left by one of the people who had fled the castle. It might have been nothing. She had learned that the difference between evidence and coincidence often depended on how badly you wanted a thing to be true.

At the next bend, she found the first evidence that the place was not empty.

A pile of clothes lay beneath a stone bridge: a child’s woollen jumper, a boot with its sole missing, two lengths of curtain, and a grey coat stiff with age. They had been arranged carefully under the arch, away from the rain. Not discarded. Collected.

Mara crouched. The coat smelled of wet earth and something sharp, like old pennies. A seam had been opened along the hem. She saw a deep pocket and, tucked inside it, the corner of a folded paper.

She reached for it.

From somewhere beyond the bridge came a quick clicking sound.

Mara froze with her fingers an inch from the coat.

Click. Click-click.

Not a bird. Not the creak of a branch. The sound came again, closer this time, followed by a scrape against stone.

She rose slowly. The bridge opened onto a narrow strip of shadow. Ahead, the rails led into a cutting crowded with bracken. Nothing moved. She heard the stream below, swollen with rain, and wind passing over the hill.

Then a voice—or something that had once been a voice—whispered from under the bridge.

“Mine.”

Mara stepped back.

A pale hand appeared at the edge of the darkness. It was too long in the fingers, the knuckles swollen and crooked, the nails black with dirt. A strip of blue cloth had been tied around its wrist. The hand closed over the grey coat and drew it soundlessly into the shadows.

Mara ran.

She sprinted up the track until the stones shifted beneath her boots and her lungs burned. She did not look back. She ran because the word had been clear, because the hand had been real, and because every story she had read about the war ended the same way: somebody deciding the danger was probably nothing until it was close enough to touch.

At last she stumbled into a stand of birch and stopped behind a trunk. Her breath rasped in her throat. Rain collected at the tip of her nose and dropped onto her sleeve. She listened.

No footsteps followed. No voice called after her.

Only the rain, the stream, and a faint metallic clatter from somewhere up the line.

Mara wiped her hands on her trousers. Her heart beat hard enough to make the notebook tremble against her ribs. She told herself she had seen a person: someone starving, someone hurt, someone who had survived the evacuation and been left behind. The hand had been distorted by shadow. The voice had been a trick of the bridge.

She had told herself similar things when she was twelve, sitting on the back steps of her mother’s house in Connecticut, listening to her parents argue through the kitchen window. If she kept very still, she could pretend the shouting belonged to another family. If she waited long enough, someone would come outside and say the world was safe again.

No one had come.

At sixteen she started leaving before anyone could tell her to go. At twenty-two she crossed an ocean with two bags and no plan she trusted. At thirty-one she had bought a second-hand notebook and followed a railway into a country where a ruined school was said to contain objects that could change the rules of a life.

She took the notebook out and turned to the page with Snape’s name.

Under her list she wrote: **Someone is living near the line. Not human?**

The pencil broke on the question mark.

Mara stared at the snapped point. It was absurd to feel embarrassed. Still, she did. She had come prepared to face magic and was already frightened by a hand beneath a bridge.

She put the pencil away and looked west, toward the rise where the castle should be. The clouds had lowered until the hilltops disappeared. For a moment, between the rain and the grey, she saw a narrow tower standing above the ridge. One side was broken open. A faint, cold light glimmered in a high window.

The clouds shifted. The tower vanished.

Mara tightened the straps of her rucksack and continued north along the tracks. She did not hurry, but she did not stop again.

Behind her, under the bridge, something began to hum.

It was a children’s tune, slow and badly remembered. The melody drifted after her through the rain, rose and fell with the wind, and then stopped all at once.

Mara walked on.

The railway bent east, then west again, following the shape of the valley instead of the shortest route through it. She tried to estimate how far she had come by counting the telegraph poles. Most had fallen. The ones still standing leaned over the track like tired sentries. Their wires had been cut, though in places a loose length swayed in the wind and made a thin, singing sound.

At the next bridge, she stopped to drink. The stream below ran fast and brown, dragging twigs and yellow leaves toward the unseen sea. The bridge stones were fitted so tightly that no mortar showed. Someone had scratched names into them: initials, dates, a few hearts, and one long line of words that ended abruptly where a chunk of stone had broken away.

She ran her fingers over the grooves. The earliest marks were old enough to have softened. The newest were sharp, bright cuts. One said **M. Hallowell**.

Mara snatched her hand back.

It was only a coincidence. Hallowell was her own name, though the carving might have been made years before she arrived. The cuts were sharp enough to seem recent, but damp stone could preserve an edge, and she could not tell how old they were. Someone else might have borne the name. Someone might have written it for another reason. She took out her notebook and copied the inscription, then immediately crossed it out. The castle did not get to turn a resemblance into proof.

The road from the station had been visible through the trees at first. Now she could no longer see it. She had been told there were old farm tracks on the eastern side, but the map showed only a green wash with no roads at all. The person who had drawn it had added three symbols in pencil: a circle with strokes, a black square, and a shape like an eye. She had copied them without knowing what they meant.

Her source had been an account by a retired railway worker named Ewan MacLeod. He had given an interview in 1998, not long before his death, describing a night when the trains stopped running without warning. He claimed he had seen “children and their keepers” walking beside the line in the rain. He said they had carried no luggage, and that one of them had been holding a birdcage full of blue fire.

The interviewer had laughed politely. MacLeod had not.

“I was told there was a derailment,” he had said. “There wasn’t. Not a wheel out of place. The line simply ended for us. Every signal showed red. Every clock stopped at the same minute. We went home, and when we came back the next morning there was nothing there but the track. No children. No cage. No stationmaster. I never worked that line again.”

Mara had played that part of the recording three times. On the last replay, beneath MacLeod’s voice, she heard a second voice whispering the word **stay**.

She had assumed it was static.

Now she looked over her shoulder. Nothing followed her along the rails. The rain had eased to a fine mist. The green slopes were empty, the stone walls empty, the spaces between the birches empty. It was the sort of emptiness that did not reassure. It seemed arranged.

She thought of the life she had left behind in Connecticut—not because she missed it, exactly, but because there were moments when the distance became physical. Her mother’s kitchen had always smelled of coffee and lemon cleaner. The refrigerator door carried school photographs and coupons held by a ceramic magnet shaped like a strawberry. Her father’s study smelled of paper, dust, and the pipe he had promised to stop smoking. The rooms were ordinary. Their rules had been ordinary too, which made it harder to explain why she had felt so often as if she were standing outside them.

At eleven, she had found a book about the Salem trials in the school library. The teacher had warned the class that the word “witch” had once been used to justify cruelty. Mara had brought the book home and asked her father whether magic could be real. He had answered that people had always invented powers to explain what they feared.

“What if it’s not invented?” she had asked.

“Then it would be a scientific fact,” he said, already turning back to his papers.

Her mother had told her not to pick fights over impossible things. Mara remembered being embarrassed—not because the question had been foolish, but because she had asked it as if she expected the adults to tell her the truth.

Years later, when the first credible evidence of the wizarding world surfaced, she had felt no triumph. Only a fierce, private anger that there had been an entire world behind the one she knew, and that no one had thought she deserved to be told.

Of course, she had not been singled out. No Muggle had been meant to know. But the difference between being excluded by a system and being excluded personally had never comforted her as much as it should.

A wind moved down the line, bringing a smell of woodsmoke. Mara stopped again. The scent was faint but unmistakable. She looked toward the western ridge. No chimney showed. The hills rose in layers, darkening with distance.

There were people somewhere. Or something that made smoke.

She considered calling out, then remembered the note beneath the bridge and the voice that had said “Mine.” She could not tell whether the speaker had been warning her away or claiming the clothes. She had no idea whether the beings on the grounds understood English, or whether English was only what they used when they wanted to be understood.

That thought brought back another story she had copied into her notes: house-elves. They had served wizarding families and institutions for centuries, bound by magic and custom. The accounts disagreed about whether the elves had been willing servants, enslaved workers, or something between those descriptions. A wartime testimony described “the domestic folk” being sent through a passage before the children, carrying bundles of blankets and food. Another said the elves had refused to leave the castle at all.

The testimony ended with a list of names. Most were crossed out.

Mara had wondered what happened to the ones left behind. Now she wished she had not found out first-hand.

She checked the time. The watch had stopped at 4:17. She tapped its face. The second hand jerked forward, then stopped again. Her phone still showed no signal, but the screen had come back on. The battery icon was full. The date was wrong by two days.

She shut it down.

There was a sensible explanation for the watch. It was old, and she had dropped it more than once. The phone might have lost time because of the dead battery. She could produce explanations for anything if she allowed herself enough of them. The difficulty was that each explanation made the next strange thing easier to accept.

The track dipped beneath an overhang of rock. At its base, an old enamel sign had been bolted to the stone. Most of its paint was gone. She could make out a white arrow pointing away from the line and a single word: **HOGWARTS**.

Someone had scratched through the name until the metal beneath showed bright.

Mara photographed it, then crouched to inspect the ground. The mud was patterned with footprints. Some were boot prints, long eroded by rain. Others were small and bare, each toe ending in a claw-like mark. They led toward the arrow, but not all of them. One set came from the direction of the castle and turned back before reaching the tracks.

She touched the deepest impression. The edges were crisp. Whatever had made it had passed recently.

The sensible choice was to follow the rails. The sign had been defaced. The map warned her not to take the old road. The tracks were her one reliable guide. Yet the tiny prints seemed to say that something had walked this route ahead of her and turned back for a reason.

She had come to find traces. She could not pretend not to see them now.

Mara followed the prints for several yards, away from the railway. They led to a drainage ditch and vanished beneath a tangle of roots. There, wedged between two stones, lay a brass button with a green serpent embossed on it. She turned it over. A loop of thread still clung to the back.

The button was warm despite the cold rain.

She put it into her pocket. A faint pressure settled behind her eyes, as though she had just remembered a word in a language she did not speak. For one brief moment, she could see a stone corridor lit by green lamps. A boy stood at its far end, thin and black-haired, watching her with an expression that might have been contempt or fear.

Then the image was gone.

Mara stumbled back into the ditch. Her hand went to the button through the cloth. She could feel its warmth against her fingers.

“Just tired,” she said aloud.

The words sounded small in the valley.

She pulled the button out and wrapped it in a scrap of paper from her notebook. She would examine it later, somewhere safer. The thought of a safer place seemed almost funny. Still, it mattered to keep making plans. Plans were a kind of railing: thin, perhaps, but something to hold while crossing a drop.

She returned to the tracks.

The sky had cleared enough for a strip of pale light to appear over the hills. It revealed a long scar across the valley: a line of broken trees and a slope of exposed rock where the ground had been torn open. A few ruined buildings stood near its edge. One had been a farmhouse. Another might have been a school or a barracks. Their roofs had collapsed inward. The walls were blackened, but the stones around them were marked with pale, branching lines that resembled frost.

Mara remembered a photograph from the archive: the same valley, before the fighting. The farmhouse had been whole. Children stood in a row outside it, their faces blurred by the poor resolution. The caption called the site an evacuation post. Someone had written beneath the image: **No one was allowed to say where they were going.**

The war had not only destroyed buildings. It had destroyed the ordinary meaning of a road, a station, a school. People had left under cover of night and never returned. Others had been moved by magic to places their families could not find. A Muggle report from the final year described hundreds of missing persons in the region, many of them children. The report blamed the military. The military blamed the wizards. The wizarding account blamed both.

Mara had not wanted to think of the children while travelling here. It made Hogwarts less like a storehouse of wonders and more like a place where people had been cornered.

She took out the notebook and added a new line beneath Snape’s name: **Find out who was evacuated—and who wasn’t.**

A low sound rolled across the valley.

It might have been thunder. The sky was clearing, however, and the sound came from below the ground, not above it. The rails trembled. A small pebble bounced once on a sleeper and dropped between the tracks.

Mara stood very still.

From far off came a bell. One clear note, struck hard enough to carry through the hills.

She remembered the railway worker’s warning. She remembered the note in the hut, though she had not yet seen it. She could not explain why the sound felt familiar, but the moment it rang, the hairs along her arms lifted.

Then a second bell answered from somewhere behind her.

Mara turned. The station lay far out of sight. The track behind her was empty.

Ahead, beyond the bend, something gave a thin, wavering cry.

She thought of going back. She thought of the button in her pocket, the name carved on the bridge, and the tower that had appeared on the ridge. The fear inside her was real. So was the desire that had brought her this far. She had wanted magic because she imagined it would make fear obey. Perhaps the first thing it would teach her was that fear had been right all along.

Mara tightened her grip on the notebook and stepped forward.

The railway narrowed until the bracken brushed both her knees.
