# Chapter Eleven: The Second Dose

“Mara Hallowell.”

The voice rose from somewhere below the green-lit stair. It was unmistakably human, but it carried no breath, no echo, no warmth. It had spoken her full name with the care of someone reading it from a label.

Mara stopped on the thirteenth step.

The bell in her hand hummed beneath its wrapping. The note was so low she felt it in her teeth before she heard it. She gripped the cloth tighter. The green light along the stairwell flickered once, then steadied.

“Who’s there?” she called.

The silence afterward felt deliberate.

She should have gone back. The stair behind her climbed toward the service passage, the hidden doors, the ledger she had left on the stone floor. There were still routes back to the outer court, perhaps. But the voice had said her name. The castle had greeted her by name before. The bell had responded to water, doors, and sounds she could not locate. If she turned away now, she would carry the question with her, and she had not crossed an ocean to return with nothing but questions.

She stepped down.

The green line brightened around the next bend. A landing opened onto a low corridor with a narrow window slit. The sky beyond it was a flat, starless grey, though her watch insisted it was still the middle of the night. A human figure stood at the far end, facing away.

Mara raised the torch.

The figure wore a long coat buttoned to the throat. One shoulder sat higher than the other. Its hair was cropped close to the scalp, dark and silvered at the temples. It was too still to be waiting naturally, yet the hem of its coat stirred in a breeze Mara could not feel.

“Are you the one who called me?”

The figure turned.

Its face was narrow and pale, not skeletal, not transparent. A line of dark stitching ran from the corner of its mouth to the hollow beneath one ear. Its eyes were the grey of old water. Around its neck hung a tarnished healer’s badge: a bowl with a line through it, the same mark Mara had noticed on the ghost’s cracked badge in the corridor above.

The thing smiled. The stitched line pulled tight.

“I am the one who heard you coming.”

“Are you a ghost?”

“No.”

“Then what are you?”

“Your guide.”

Mara did not lower the torch. “That isn’t what I asked.”

The figure’s gaze slid to the bell. “You brought it back.”

“I took it from a room.”

“People call it taking when they wish to avoid the word choosing.”

The phrase landed too close to the house-elf’s warning. Mara felt her jaw tighten. “You know Aveline.”

The guide did not answer. It turned and walked farther down the corridor.

Mara stayed where she was. “If you want me to follow, tell me who you are.”

The figure glanced back. “I was a healer.”

“Were you?”

“I knew where to cut.”

“That’s not the same thing.”

“No,” it said. “It isn’t.”

It continued into the dark.

Mara should have heard the warning in that answer. Instead she heard information. The figure had been a healer, perhaps one of the people who ran the lower infirmary. It knew the bell and the dose. It might know what Snape had left behind. In the room at the end of the corridor, she could see a brass plaque, though the lettering was too far away to make out.

She followed.

The hallway descended at a shallow angle. Three-groove symbols had been pressed into the walls at regular intervals. Some were crisp; others were broken by blast scars or covered by a film of black residue. A pale line ran along the floor. It divided at each junction, and the branches that turned away from the guide’s path had been painted over in a grey so close to the stone that Mara only noticed them when her torch passed across the surface.

“Why are the marks different?” she asked.

“Different hands.”

“Were they directions?”

“They were instructions.”

“For whom?”

“For whoever was still here.”

The guide paused at a doorway. Beyond it, Mara glimpsed rows of iron beds and a counter crowded with bottles. The air that drifted out smelled of wet stone and bitter herbs.

“Did you work in there?”

“I worked where I was needed.”

“Did you treat the children?”

The guide’s smile returned. “I made certain they survived the treatment.”

Mara stared at it. The distinction sounded practiced, worn smooth by use.

Inside the infirmary, dust lay thick on the bed frames. The mattresses had collapsed into grey mounds. A stool stood on its side near the nearest bed. Across the floor, shallow channels carried a black, glassy substance toward a drain beneath the counter. The channels were dry in places. In others, the black remained liquid and still.

The guide walked to a cupboard at the far end. Its doors bore a faded label: **DOSES—BELL TRIALS**.

Mara followed more slowly. “What happened here?”

“People wanted a defense.”

“Against the war?”

“Against anything that could get through a door.”

The guide opened the cupboard. Inside stood six bottles in a rack. Five were empty, their labels reduced to water-stained strips. The sixth held a clear liquid, just enough to cover the bottom. Its label was legible:

**SECOND BELL DOSE. ONE DROP ONLY. DO NOT ADMINISTER AFTER THE THIRD RING.**

A row of small numbers had been written beneath it. The last three were scratched out.

Mara brought the torch closer. “What does it do?”

“It gives you power.”

The answer was so plain that she nearly laughed. After weeks of riddles, that bluntness sounded like another trick.

“What kind?”

“The room becomes legible. The hidden route shows itself. A sound can be given direction. A thing that has been called can be made to approach.”

“Summoned.”

“If you prefer.”

“And the price?”

The guide turned its grey eyes on her. “You become more able to do what you already wanted.”

Mara felt a small, involuntary chill. “That’s not a price. That’s a sales pitch.”

“Is it?” The guide’s stitched smile widened. “You came seeking Snape’s power. You admire the shape of it. You believe that if you can do what others cannot, no one will ever decide your life for you again.”

The room seemed to contract around the words.

Mara reached for the bottle. “You don’t know anything about me.”

“I know you left a place that could not keep you.”

“Everyone leaves somewhere.”

“And you came here to become someone who cannot be made to leave.”

Mara pulled her hand back. The small bottle trembled in its rack. “What does the dose do to people who take it?”

“It makes magic answer.”

“That still isn’t an answer.”

“It sharpens the will. It strips away the pause between wanting and acting.”

The bell hummed softly against her palm. The sound touched a place inside her that felt like a bruise. “And it makes people darker?”

The guide tilted its head. “Darkness is not a substance. It is what remains when you stop caring who must pay.”

Mara searched the figure’s face for mockery. There was none. That was worse.

She remembered Connecticut in a winter that seemed to last through half her childhood. Her mother standing at the kitchen counter in the blue light before dawn, sorting bills into piles and pretending the piles meant there was a plan. Her father’s boots by the door, mud drying in their seams, though he had been gone for months. Mara at eleven, watching a landlord’s hand move down a clipboard. Her mother had smiled at him, apologized for the late payment, offered a date that she could not guarantee. Mara had been old enough to understand that the decision was already made and too young to change it.

The family moved into her aunt’s spare room. Then to a one-bedroom apartment above a laundromat. Then out again after the building was sold. Her mother called each move temporary. Mara learned to pack quickly and never put the things she loved at the bottom of a box. She learned how to look calm when adults spoke about her as if she were luggage. She learned that asking for help made people assess whether she was worth helping.

Years later, she had watched another kind of power decide who could remain and who had to go. News reports, hearings, emergency orders, a war that turned neighbours into categories and houses into targets. She had been no soldier, no witch, no hero. She had watched from a screen as the world chose winners, then told the rest to be grateful they had survived.

She had come to Hogwarts because she refused to be helpless again.

The guide watched her. “You remember.”

“I remember what it costs to rely on someone else.”

“And so you wish to become the one who decides.”

“I wish not to be at someone’s mercy.”

“Those are not the same wish.”

“They’re close enough.”

Mara stepped toward the bottle. The fear in her chest did not disappear. But under it, her anger was clearer, more useful. She could feel the bell’s hum now as a thread stretched through the room, connecting the iron beds, the floor channels, the cupboard, and a section of wall behind the counter. A narrow outline showed in the plaster, invisible until she focused on it.

The dose had not touched her, yet the room was already beginning to make sense.

“Do I have to drink it?” she asked.

“You could keep it.”

“Would it still work?”

“Not for long.”

“How long?”

“Until the third bell.”

“And what happens then?”

The guide’s expression grew almost tender. “The power becomes hungry.”

Mara looked at the label again. The warning had not said the dose would expire after the third ring. It said not to administer it after the third ring. Someone had wanted it used before then.

A distant note sounded through the walls. One bell, low and slow.

The black liquid in the floor channels shivered. The guide raised its face toward the ceiling.

“First bell,” it said.

“What does that mean?”

“It means the room is waking.”

A second note sounded, deeper than the first. A ripple travelled through the liquid, carrying it toward the drain. The cupboard doors rattled on their hinges. The bell in Mara’s hand answered with a faint hum.

The guide reached for the bottle. “Take one drop.”

“Why do you want me to?”

“Because you need to see.”

“What do you need?”

The guide paused. For the first time, the stitched line at its mouth slackened. The smile vanished. “I need a hand.”

“Your own hands seem fine.”

“I have no hands that can hold the bell.”

Mara looked at the dark healer’s fingers. They appeared ordinary until the torch caught them. Then she saw that the nails had been cut too close and the fingertips were stained a permanent brown, as if they had spent a lifetime handling old medicines.

“You want me to ring it.”

“I want you to find the route.”

“You said the dose can summon something.”

“I said it can make a sound approach.”

“Something dangerous?”

“Everything useful is dangerous to someone.”

Mara took the bottle from its rack. The glass was warm. She held it up to the torch. The liquid inside clung to the walls instead of settling at the base. A thin green thread moved through it, curling like smoke.

She could put it back. She could walk away and climb toward the service court. She could keep the bell wrapped and wait until the voices ceased. But she had already heard her name in the lower stair, and the route behind her might no longer be safe. The power of the dose might show her a way past the black, or reveal the place the bell was trying to reach.

There was the thought she had been waiting for: a practical reason. A reason that made taking it sound like necessity rather than desire.

Mara tipped the bottle. One drop fell onto her tongue.

It tasted like iron.

The room snapped into focus.

The black channels were not channels at all, but lines drawn around a shape that covered the infirmary floor. Six circles connected by a single curve. Five circles were crossed out. The sixth lay beneath the bed at the far end, where the sagging mattress had left a pale rectangular shadow.

The hidden door behind the counter shone green around its frame.

The sound of the bell became visible: a thin silver thread that travelled from Mara’s hand through the wall, down beneath the floor, and into the dark. At its far end, something moved in answer.

Mara saw the room’s route. She saw the places where the black had pooled and the narrow strip of clear stone that led to the hidden door. She saw all of it at once, as if the world had been waiting for her to ask the correct question.

She also saw the guide’s shadow.

It was not shaped like the figure. It spread behind the healer across the wall, tall and many-jointed, with long fingers that reached toward the bottles. Its head was bent over the cupboard like a bird’s. No face showed, only a hollow where the mouth ought to be.

The guide noticed her looking. “What do you see?”

Mara’s throat had gone dry. “You.”

“Good.”

“No. Your shadow.”

“That is often more honest.”

The first bell had rung. The second had rung. The third note began somewhere below them.

Mara looked at the hidden door, then at the guide. “What’s beyond it?”

“A trace.”

“Whose?”

“Someone who left.”

“Aveline?”

The healer’s smile returned, but now it seemed stretched over something that wanted to split its face open. “If that is the name you need.”

The third bell sounded.

The bottle in Mara’s hand cracked down the side. The remaining liquid slid out in a green ribbon and struck the floor. The black channels lit from within. The silver thread of sound jerked tight.

Mara felt a force pull at her wrist. The bell strained toward the far end of its invisible line.

The guide stepped closer. “Ring it.”

The sealed cylinder in Mara’s pack pressed against her ribs. Snape’s warning surfaced: **If the bell is heard, do not answer it. S.**

Mara thought of the dose’s effect. It had made the hidden route legible. It had shown her how the sound travelled. It had put a power in her reach that she could not have touched before. The bell was not merely calling something. It could reveal what was hidden, and the dose made it possible to shape that call.

She lifted the bell.

“Don’t,” the guide said.

Mara looked at it. “You told me to ring it.”

“I told you to find the route.”

“Why change your mind?”

The healer’s shadow flexed its many fingers. The figure’s mouth pulled tight against the stitches. “Because you can hear me now.”

“What does that mean?”

“It means you can hear what I am.”

Behind the hidden door came a soft tapping. Three taps, a pause, then one. The pattern Mara had felt through the warm stone in the service passage.

A voice whispered from the other side. “Not yet.”

Mara’s heart lurched. That was the voice from the underwall. The voice she had been unable to place, the one that seemed to belong to the castle itself. She brought the bell closer to the door, listening for another sound.

The guide reached for her arm. Mara turned the bell toward it.

“Don’t touch me.”

The words came out colder than she intended. The healer stopped. Its shadow did not.

For the first time, Mara felt something answer her will. Not the bell’s vibration. Not a sound in the room. A current of pressure rose in her chest and travelled down her arm. The metal warmed. The guide’s shadow snapped back from her, its long fingers folding inward.

Mara stared at her hand.

She had done that.

A small, fierce thrill moved through her. It was not relief. It was not safety. It was the satisfaction of discovering that the world could be made to move when she decided it should.

The thrill frightened her. She held onto it anyway.

The guide’s grey eyes narrowed. “There it is.”

“What?”

“The part of you that thinks fear is an insult.”

Mara looked toward the hidden door. “I want to know what’s behind it.”

“You want to take it.”

“I want to learn.”

“You have learned. The dose gave you power over a path, not its owner.”

“Then let me see.”

The guide’s shadow shivered across the wall. “A sound can be given direction. But something must hear it.”

Mara took a breath. She placed her thumb against the bell’s rim. The three grooves warmed beneath her skin. She could feel the silver thread running through the wall and down into the dark. Instead of ringing, she pressed her will against the thread and pushed it toward the hidden door.

The bell gave one clear note.

The hidden door opened.

On the other side was not another room but a small, stone-lined well. Its inner surface shone wetly. A narrow platform crossed the opening, and on the far edge lay a single object: a strip of faded green cloth tied around a brass tag.

The tag bore a name. The dose made the letters clear.

**AVELINE.**

Beneath it, someone had scratched a second line: **I CHOSE THE DOOR.**

Mara stared. Aveline had not been dragged there. She had chosen. The words were not enough to explain what she had chosen, or why. But they changed the shape of the mystery. The empty sixth place was not simply a missing child. It had been opened by a decision.

Something shifted beneath the platform.

The well answered the bell with a sound like a breath drawn through a narrow throat. A shape rose in the black water. It had no face, only a slick, pale curve that reached toward the tag.

Mara could make it retreat. She knew how. The dose showed her the line of sound, showed the creature responding to its pull. All she had to do was turn the bell away and draw the note back.

Instead, she held the note steady.

The shape rose higher. The tag slid an inch toward the water.

The healer’s shadow stretched toward the well. “Now call it closer.”

Mara looked at the healer. “You said you needed a hand.”

“I do.”

“You want me to bring that thing up.”

“I want what it carries.”

“What does it carry?”

“A name.”

The shape under the platform reached again. The tag slid another inch. Mara could withdraw the bell’s note and let the thing sink. She could keep the creature at a distance and take the tag herself. Or she could make it come closer, let the healer reach for what it wanted, and see what it was willing to risk.

The choice came down to one question: what could she learn if she controlled the danger?

She had spent her life on the other side of decisions, watching someone else point to a line on paper and tell her where she could stay. She remembered the clipboard in her mother’s kitchen. The landlord’s pen. The careful smile that meant the answer would not change. The news footage of frightened families moving through checkpoints while people in clean offices debated whether they counted as a threat.

She had promised herself she would never again be a person with no leverage.

Mara tightened her grip on the bell. “Come closer.”

The words were barely louder than a breath. The dose carried them down the silver thread.

The shape rose.

Water spilled over the lip of the well. The platform trembled. The healer lunged toward the opening. Its shadow moved first, sweeping over the stone like a net. The pale shape struck the edge, and the brass tag skidded toward the healer’s hand.

Mara pulled the bell sideways.

The sound twisted. The creature struck the stone instead of the platform. The tag bounced once, then landed at Mara’s feet. The healer’s shadow snapped back. A shriek rang through the infirmary, high and furious. The black channels flared green.

Mara picked up the tag.

The letters changed under her fingers. For an instant the name Aveline disappeared. In its place, four words emerged, pressed into the brass from within:

**SHE LEFT SOMEONE BEHIND.**

Then the name returned.

The well’s water went black. The pale shape sank. The hidden door slammed shut. The healer staggered, clutching its stitched mouth. One of the stitches had come loose, and a thin dark line opened beneath it.

Mara kept the tag. “Who did she leave?”

The healer lifted its eyes. Its shadow had shrunk, but the hollow where its mouth should be had widened. “The one she meant to save.”

“Who?”

“I don’t know.”

“Or you won’t tell me?”

The healer’s smile was gone. “I know only what the room kept.”

The power in Mara’s chest began to ebb. The infirmary lost its sharp edges. The hidden route faded from her sight. The silver thread vanished. In its place, the ordinary dark returned: beds, cupboard, channels, door. But the bell still felt warm. The healer’s shadow remained visible, even without the dose’s clarity. It curled around the figure’s feet, too large for the body that cast it.

Mara slid Aveline’s tag into her pocket. “You wanted the name.”

“I wanted the person.”

“Why?”

“Because names can be carried. People can be used.”

The honesty of it chilled her. “You were a healer.”

“I was.”

“You harmed them.”

“I learned what a body would surrender to remain alive.”

“And you called that healing?”

“I called it necessary.”

Mara thought of the black channels arranged around the six places. The bottles. The dose. The warning about the third bell. “What did you do to Aveline?”

The healer moved closer. “I tried to keep her.”

“You said she chose the door.”

“She did.”

“Then what did you do?”

“I made her choice costly.”

The words passed through Mara like a cold draft. The dark healer had not merely worked in the infirmary. It had turned care into leverage and treatment into a means of control. The dose had been made to free a path, summon a response, and make its user more willing to take what they wanted. It was power built from the same logic as the healer’s “necessary” harm.

Mara should have thrown the bottle away. She should have dropped the bell and the tag, then run for the stair. Instead she checked the bottle. A thin residue remained inside, green and bright as a trapped filament.

The healer watched her do it. “You understand now.”

“I understand what you want me to believe.”

“And what do you believe?”

Mara did not answer. The sound of the bell had revealed the hidden door. The dose had given her the power to direct it. She had used that power to draw the creature nearer, then turned the sound at the last moment. She had gained the tag. She had also liked the moment when the healer recoiled from her.

That was the part she did not want to examine.

A knock came from the corridor outside. Three slow blows against the infirmary door.

The dark healer lifted its head. “We have to go.”

“Why?”

“The thing you called heard the room.”

“You told me to call it.”

“I told you to find the route.”

“You lied.”

“I gave you a reason to follow.”

The door shook under another blow. Dust fell from the lintel. The black in the channels began to move toward the threshold, gathering itself into thin, branching streams.

Mara backed away. “Where does the route lead?”

“Below.”

“Below what?”

“The place that keeps the names.”

“You said Aveline left someone behind.”

“She did.”

“Could that person still be there?”

The healer glanced toward the well. “A part of them.”

The door shuddered again. Its hinges groaned. The healer turned toward a narrow passage at the far side of the infirmary, partly concealed behind a standing screen. The dose had shown Mara a seam there, but the image was gone now. She searched the wall, found the faint outline, and pushed.

A panel swung inward.

The healer slipped through. Mara hesitated, looking at the black channels. The shadow at the threshold had begun to spread beneath the door. It was thin enough to be stepped over, but it moved with the patience of something that did not need to hurry.

She went after the healer.

The passage was so narrow that her shoulders brushed both walls. The door closed behind her. The pounding ceased at once. For several seconds, Mara could hear nothing but her own breathing and the distant creak of the castle.

The passage sloped downward. The healer walked ahead, its coat brushing the stone. Mara followed with the bell in one hand and Aveline’s tag in the other. The tag had grown cold. The bell remained warm.

“Why do you need a hand?” she asked.

“I cannot pass the next door.”

“Because you’re a ghost?”

“I told you I am not.”

“What are you, then?”

“Something the war made useful.”

“Did you die here?”

The healer turned its head. “Death is not the only way to be kept.”

The passage ended at a stone door with no handle. Six shallow circles had been carved into its surface. Five were crossed through. The sixth remained empty. Beneath it, someone had scratched the three-groove mark, followed by an interrupted circle.

Mara’s serpent button warmed in her pocket.

“Is this the sixth place?” she asked.

“It is one of them.”

“One of how many?”

The healer did not answer.

Mara placed the bell against the empty circle. It fitted the shallow curve of the carving. She felt the power left by the dose stir in her chest—faint, but present. The door did not open. She tried the serpent button. The brass flashed green, and the sixth circle glowed.

A voice came from behind the stone. A young woman, exhausted and very close.

“Not her.”

Mara’s fingers tightened around the button.

The healer leaned toward the door. “Aveline?”

The voice answered, “Not the sixth.”

The phrase matched the clue scratched into the old note. Mara felt a chill run down her back. “Who is the sixth?”

For a moment there was no answer. Then the young woman spoke again.

“The one she left.”

Mara leaned toward the stone. “Did she leave you?”

A faint knock sounded from the other side. Once. Then twice. Then a third time, softer than the others.

“She left me the way out.”

The healer’s shadow stretched over the door. Its dark fingers reached toward the glowing circle. “Open it.”

Mara turned sharply. “Why?”

“To bring her back.”

“You said she chose.”

“She can choose again.”

The three-groove mark beneath the circle glowed. The bell vibrated. Mara saw a thin line of green light appear around the stone door. The hidden route was beginning to reveal itself again, but it cost effort to hold the image in focus. The dose’s power was fading.

She could open the door. She could hear Aveline or the person she had left. The healer wanted her to do it. The sealed cylinder in her pack carried Snape’s warning. The bell had already drawn something close enough to pound on the infirmary door. Mara’s desire to know was still there, but now it had a face: the unknown person behind the stone, the one Aveline had left a way out for.

“What happens if I open it?”

The healer smiled. “You’ll learn.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

“It is the only one that matters.”

Mara looked down at the tag. The letters had changed again. Beneath AVELINE, the brass now read: **SHE DID NOT LEAVE ME.**

Mara stared. “The voice said she left someone behind.”

“The room wants you to misunderstand.”

“Or you do.”

The healer reached for her. Mara stepped away. The shadow’s fingers continued forward, stretching farther than the figure’s arm.

“No,” she said.

The word came with a pulse of heat. The bell answered. The shadow jerked backward, striking the wall. The healer’s stitched mouth opened. A hiss escaped it, thin and furious.

Mara felt the power return, brief and sharp. She had not taken another dose. She had only wanted the healer to stop.

And the healer had stopped.

The thrill came again, stronger this time. For one dangerous second, she imagined how easy it would be to make the figure kneel, to force an answer from it, to punish it for what it had done to the children. She wanted to see it afraid.

Then she looked at the stone door and understood how easily the same thought could become the next dose’s purpose.

She put the bell back into its wrapping. The green light faded. The door remained closed.

The healer straightened its coat. “You think refusal makes you good.”

“No. I think it makes this choice mine.”

“Then choose.”

Mara turned from the door. “Not this one.”

The healer looked almost pleased. “You’ll come back.”

“Maybe.”

“Not because you want to help.”

Mara did not deny it. “You don’t know what I want.”

“I know what you came here to take.”

The stone door gave one final knock from the other side. A small scrape followed, like a fingernail moving along its inner surface. Then silence.

Mara took the passage leading down. It was the only route that did not take her back to the infirmary. The healer drifted after her, keeping a little distance. The dose had allowed her to see the hidden way and direct the bell’s call; it had also brought the healer’s shadow into view, and it had made her own anger feel like a weapon she could use without consequence. She would not take another drop. At least, not yet.

The passage opened into a low chamber with a dry stone basin in its center. A single white mark ran along the floor and disappeared beneath a door at the far end. The door bore a brass plate with two letters: **S. S.**

Mara stopped.

Snape’s initials.

The dark healer paused behind her. “That is not the room you are looking for.”

“How do you know?”

“Because the room you are looking for is behind you.”

Mara checked the sealed cylinder inside her pack. Its wax remained unbroken. The initials on the door were real enough, but the healer had already lied once about why it wanted her to use the bell. It might be trying to turn her away from the one thing she had come to find.

She moved toward the door.

The healer’s shadow slid over the white mark and stopped before it crossed. “Do not open it.”

Mara turned. “You’re telling me what not to do now?”

“I’m telling you what I know.”

“Which is?”

“That Snape left something here.”

Her heartbeat quickened. “What?”

“A reason not to take it.”

The answer sounded familiar. Mara thought of the earlier warning in Snape’s hand: **If the bell is heard, do not answer it.** She looked at the door. Its handle was a small brass serpent, its mouth open around an empty hollow.

Mara did not put the bell in it.

She used the iron key from the service stair.

The lock turned. The door opened a few inches, releasing a smell of bitter herbs, smoke, and old paper. Inside, shelves lined a cramped workroom. A narrow table held a shallow glass dish, a bundle of notes, and a second cylinder sealed with green wax. A silver S had been pressed into it.

Beside the cylinder lay a folded piece of parchment. Mara could read the first line from the doorway:

**For whoever thinks power will make them safe.**

She did not cross the threshold.

Behind her, the healer made a small, breathless sound. The guide’s shadow pulled itself back from the white line on the floor. For the first time, the dark figure looked uncertain.

Mara took the serpent button from her pocket. It was warm now, but not burning. The tag in her other hand had gone still. Its final words remained visible: **SHE DID NOT LEAVE ME.**

The clue was stronger, not clearer. Aveline had chosen the door. Someone had been left behind, but the person behind the stone insisted Aveline had not abandoned them. Perhaps she had left them a way out. Perhaps the “sixth place” was not a person at all, but the space she had opened for someone to escape.

The sealed cylinder and the note could wait. The healer could not be trusted. The bell was still warm. Mara felt the dose’s remaining power in her chest, fading but not gone.

She wanted to read Snape’s words. She wanted the answer about Aveline. She wanted the dark healer to tell her what it had done and why. Most of all, she wanted to keep the feeling that the room had yielded when she commanded it.

She recognized the hunger now. Recognition did not make it smaller.

Mara folded the tag into her pocket and stepped back from the Snape room. “I’ll come back.”

The healer’s stitched smile returned. “You always do.”

“I haven’t been here before.”

“Not you.”

The phrase reached after her like a hand.

Mara turned toward the white-marked corridor. The door to Snape’s room remained ajar, the parchment waiting on the table. The second bell dose had shown her a hidden route and allowed her to turn a sound into a summons. It had also made her more willing to use anger as power. The next time she took it, she might not stop at one drop—or at the edge of a closed door.

She walked away anyway.

At the corridor’s end, the white marks turned left, toward a narrow staircase climbing into darkness. The healer followed but did not pass the pale line. Its shadow remained behind, stretched across the stones like a stain.

Mara looked back once. “What’s your name?”

The dark healer touched the badge at its throat. “Names are what the room keeps.”

“That’s not an answer.”

“No,” it said. “It is a warning.”

The bell hummed beneath the scarf. Not a summons this time. A quiet, steady note, as if it had heard the healer and remembered something it had been told not to forget.

Mara climbed. Behind her, the healer spoke once more.

“You came here to become powerful.”

She kept going.

“You have.”

At the top of the stairs, the green light faded. Mara reached the landing and found a narrow window opening onto the ruined inner court. The first grey of morning pressed against the glass. She could see the broken greenhouse roof, the toppled statue, and a line of wet tracks crossing the flagstones below.

They began at the service stair.

They ended at the infirmary door.

There were no tracks between.

Mara touched the bottle in her pocket. Its remaining drop had turned dark. She did not take it out.

In the glass, her reflection looked older than it had in the basin: hollow-eyed, pale, and holding something she could not quite see. Behind her reflected shoulder, a second figure stood in the stairwell. It wore a healer’s badge and a long coat. Its face was turned away.

Mara spun around.

The landing was empty.

She looked back at the window. Her reflection stood alone. The bell’s wrapped handle pressed into her palm, warm as a living pulse.

At the bottom of the glass, where the tracks ended, someone had written a message in the fog:

**SHE LEFT THE DOOR OPEN.**

Mara wiped it away with her sleeve.

The words appeared again, this time on the other side of the glass.

A bell rang once, far beneath the castle.

Mara closed her hand around the serpent button and began climbing toward the next level, carrying the dose, the bell, and a clue she could not yet tell was a warning or an invitation.
