## Chapter Two: The Last Station

The railway narrowed until the bracken brushed both her knees. The rails dipped through a cutting where water had gathered between the sleepers. Mara picked her way along the raised stones, trying not to look at the black pools. The map showed the line ending at a signal hut, though no hut had appeared yet. She checked her watch. The hour hand seemed to have moved too quickly. The sky had darkened from pewter to slate.

She should have turned back to the station. The thought came plainly, without drama. There was still enough daylight to reach it. There might be a working phone somewhere down the line, or a house on the road, or a person who could tell her whether the thing beneath the bridge had followed.

Mara continued.

She had spent too many years mistaking retreat for prudence. Sometimes it was prudence. Sometimes it was fear wearing a sensible coat. She could no longer tell which without testing the door.

The signal hut appeared around a bend, a low stone building with its roof fallen inward. A faded railway emblem clung to the wall. The doorway had been blocked with planks, but one had rotted loose. Mara pushed it aside and slipped through.

The smell inside was old damp and mouse droppings. A desk lay on its side. A metal cabinet had been pried open, its drawers scattered across the floor. On the back wall someone had painted a thick white line, then crossed it out with three slashes—the same mark as the one on the warning post.

Mara took a photograph. Her phone’s screen flickered and went black.

“Great,” she muttered.

She tried the power button. Nothing. She took out the battery pack and cable, but the phone would not respond. She set it aside and searched the hut by torchlight. There was a cracked mug, a rusted signal lever, and a paper map tacked behind the desk. Most of it had rotted away. A dark, circular stain covered the western side, as if someone had held a flame to the paper and then changed their mind.

In the remaining corner, she found a handwritten note.

**If the train stops short, do not walk the road after dark. Use the line. If the line is blocked, wait for morning. Do not answer the bell.**

No signature. The paper was dry, though the rest of the room was sodden. Mara folded it and slipped it into her notebook.

A bell rang once outside.

She held still.

The sound was not loud. It seemed to come from far off, beyond the hills, yet it vibrated in the hut’s stone walls. A second ring followed, fainter than the first.

Mara went to the doorway. The rain had stopped. The landscape lay under a blue-grey twilight. She could see the railway behind her and the low ridge ahead, but nothing that might hold a bell tower.

A third ring sounded.

Something on the slope answered with a long, low cry.

Mara remembered the note: **Do not answer the bell.** She stepped back inside and closed the door as quietly as she could. It did not latch. She braced the rotting plank against it and waited, one hand on the knife at her belt.

The cry came again. This time it was closer, and she heard the flap of wings.

She looked through a crack between the boards. A huge birdlike shape crossed the sky, its wings bent at wrong-looking angles. It landed on the signal post. Its head turned toward the hut. The creature was pale, almost skeletal, with a beak that shone wetly in the fading light.

Mara knew the word from one of the lists she had copied: hippogriff. But this one was smaller than the drawings, its feathers sparse and clotted. One leg dragged as it shifted on the post. A leather strap hung from its neck, the buckle rusted shut. Beneath the feathers at its shoulder, a dark wound cut across the skin in three parallel grooves. The edges were not torn as if by a fall. They were pressed inward, as though something with immense strength had gripped it and let go.

The hippogriff tapped the metal with its beak. The sound was exactly like the clatter she had heard after running from the bridge.

Mara lowered the knife. She did not know whether the creature was hungry, injured, or simply curious. The hippogriff’s eyes fixed on the gap in the boards. It lowered its head, then raised it again, fighting to keep its balance. A wet breath rasped through its beak.

A whisper rose from outside.

“Not yours.”

The voice came from below the window. A narrow face appeared in the crack between the boards, one enormous ear flattened against the wood. The elf from the bridge—or another like it—watched her with a pale, cloudy eye.

Mara glanced from the elf to the wound. “What did that?”

The elf’s eye shifted toward the hillside. Its mouth worked, but no words came. It raised one thin hand and made a clawing motion in the air. Then it struck the wall twice with its knuckles.

The hippogriff screamed.

It launched itself from the post and struck the hut’s roof edge with its claws. The building shuddered. Mara ducked as grit and a broken tile pattered across the floor. Its wings beat unevenly; one dragged, leaving a smear of dark blood on the stone. It landed hard beyond the hut and gave another furious cry, not a plea but a warning.

The elf vanished from the crack.

Mara peered through the boards. The hippogriff was limping toward the trees, turning its head every few steps to watch the ridge behind it. It was not fleeing her. It was fleeing something farther up the slope.

For an instant, a shape stood between the stones on the hillside. Too tall and narrow to be a person, it bent at the waist as though examining the ground. The light shifted. The shape was gone. On the rock where it had stood, three fresh furrows cut through the moss.

Mara backed away from the door. She understood then that the wound was not the most frightening thing about the hippogriff. It was the fact that it had survived whatever made it.

Night came quickly.

Mara considered staying in the hut. It had walls, but no roof worth trusting and no way to bar the door. The map showed a culvert running beneath the railway and a path on the far side. If she could reach it before darkness settled, she might find shelter among the trees.

She checked the torch. The beam had weakened. She pulled on her rain jacket and tied the notebook inside her coat. Before leaving, she looked at the note again. The instruction was to use the line, and she was on it. She decided that counted.

The tracks climbed out of the cutting and curved west. In the fading light, the valley opened below her. A narrow river ran through it, silver where the sky still showed. Across the water, a road disappeared into the hills. A row of stone posts marked its edge. They were too evenly spaced to be natural, but some had fallen and others leaned at angles. One bore the remains of a carved crest, three animals tangled around a shield.

Mara stopped.

She had seen that crest in the old photograph from the archive. It had been above the entrance to a school building—Hogwarts, according to the caption. The animals were almost worn away, but she recognized the lion and the serpent. The third might have been an eagle.

A smear of orange light moved among the far trees.

At first she thought it was a lantern. It rose, paused, and drifted sideways without dipping or swinging. Another light appeared beside it, then a third. They moved in a line along the ridge.

Mara crouched behind a railway signal box. The lights were not flames. They were too cold, too steady. As they drew nearer, she could make out shapes inside them—pale heads, arms, the trailing hem of a robe. One figure moved with its feet several inches above the ground.

Ghosts.

She had read that ghosts were impressions, memories that refused to fade. Most of the accounts described them as harmless: pale residents of old houses, melancholy scholars, a knight who had lost his head. These did not drift aimlessly. They searched the slopes, turning their faces toward the railway whenever the wind shifted.

The first ghost wore a long coat that seemed burned along one side. Its face was hidden behind a dark veil of hair. It stopped directly below the signal box.

Mara held her breath.

“Anyone there?” it called.

The voice was almost ordinary. A woman’s voice, hoarse with cold. The figure beside her replied in a language Mara could not understand. A third ghost turned its head toward the signal box.

The first one looked up.

Mara dropped flat against the ground.

She heard the dead woman climb the signal box. The metal steps creaked once, then again. A hand, translucent and rimmed with pale blue light, appeared over the edge. Mara backed along the ballast, keeping low. The box stood beside the track, with a narrow ditch running beneath it. She slid into the ditch and pressed her cheek against wet grass.

The ghost’s fingers closed around the lip of the box.

“Warm,” it whispered.

Mara could feel the cold radiating from it even from below. The ghost tilted its head. The hair shifted, and beneath it she saw a face marked by dark veins, the mouth stretched open as though someone had pulled it apart. A silver badge clung to the coat. It bore no readable letters, only a small starburst symbol.

“Warm,” it said again.

Mara gripped the knife, knowing it was useless. The ghost lowered its face toward the ditch. Its eyes were white and filmed over.

A twig snapped behind the signal box.

The ghost turned.

Something moved across the slope, low to the earth. The ghost gave a shriek that made Mara’s teeth ache. The other figures swept toward the sound, their robes streaming behind them. For a moment the hillside filled with blue-white light.

Mara ran.

She scrambled up the far bank and took the tracks toward the river. Behind her, the ghosts cried out, and the low shape answered with a wet, rattling hiss. The noise followed her down the track. She slipped once, struck her palm on a stone, and kept moving. Her torch dropped from her pocket and rolled into the grass. She left it.

At the culvert she found the stream swollen with rain. The tunnel beneath the line was half-submerged. She hesitated, then crawled through with the water up to her thighs. The cold stole her breath. On the far side, she hauled herself onto the bank and lay in the mud, shaking.

Above her, the sky had become a single dark sheet. The castle was somewhere beyond the ridge, hidden by weather and night. The ghosts had not followed through the culvert. Or they had chosen another way.

Mara sat up and checked her hand. The palm was scraped but not badly cut. The notebook remained dry beneath her coat. She found the railway note and read it again by the light of her phone, which had briefly flickered back to life.

**Do not answer the bell.**

She had not answered it. She had heard it. Perhaps that was enough.

The phone’s light made a weak circle over the page. Mara read the note once more, then folded it along its old creases. The paper was warm from her coat, but the ink had not run. Someone had written the warning in a firm hand, as if making the letters neat could keep the fear out of them.

She wondered who had left it. A railway worker? A witch or wizard using the hut as a landmark? Someone who had known the bell and lived long enough to warn the next person? The note had no date. Its paper was old, but the writing looked newer than the map. It might have been written during the war, when the railway still carried evacuees. It might have been written years afterward by someone who came searching, as she had.

Mara pictured a person crouched in that hut, listening to the same bell, deciding to leave the warning rather than run. The idea unsettled her more than the ghosts. A ghost could be explained by magic. A warning meant someone had understood the danger and expected another person to come.

She slid the paper into the notebook, between the map and Snape’s name. The pages had begun to swell with damp. She moved the book beneath her sweater and tried to work out where she was. The river ran roughly south. The rails curved west. The castle lay north-west, if the map was right. She could follow the river to shelter, then climb toward the old road at first light.

The light had nearly failed. She had no torch now. The phone could give her a little illumination, but the battery might be draining despite the full icon. She could preserve it by shutting it down, though that left her in darkness. She made herself choose deliberately: phone off, knife in hand, follow the river until she found a place to sleep.

She had taken perhaps twenty paces when something scraped along the far bank.

Mara stopped. The sound moved in short, dragging intervals. She aimed the phone across the water, then remembered she had turned it off. The river reflected no stars. On the opposite bank, a narrow shape lowered itself to drink.

The creature was too thin to be a dog. Its back rose in a ridge of bristled fur, and its front legs bent outward like a spider’s. It drank without lowering its head; the water seemed to climb into its mouth. When it lifted its face, two pale eyes fixed on Mara.

She held her breath.

The creature made a noise like a kettle beginning to boil. From behind it came another answering sound.

Mara backed away slowly. The second shape emerged from the brush, larger, its body hidden by the dark. It smelled of wet bark and rotten meat. She put one foot on a stone. It rolled beneath her boot and clacked against another.

Both creatures turned.

She ran downstream.

The bank narrowed and the river pressed close. Mud sucked at her boots. A low branch struck her cheek. She heard splashes behind her, then the uneven rhythm of claws on stone. The animals did not bark or growl. Their silence was worse; it made her imagine that they were not chasing for hunger but because they had been taught to follow.

A narrow stone bridge crossed the stream ahead. Mara scrambled up its side and reached the crown. There was no path beyond it, only a slope of heather and a tangle of fallen trees. She dropped beneath the bridge, into the shallow space between the arch and the water, and pressed herself against the cold masonry.

The first creature arrived. Its claws clicked over the stones above her head. It paused at the bridge’s edge and sniffed. The second gave its boiling whine. Something tapped the stone once—twice—then passed on.

Mara remained where she was until her knees began to shake. When she finally crawled out, a damp strand of black hair clung to the underside of the arch. It had not been there when she entered. She looked upstream and down, but saw no one.

Her fingers found the green-serpent button in her pocket. It had grown hot enough to sting.

She pulled it out. A little curl of smoke rose from the metal. The button’s stitched loop had burned away, leaving a clean hole. Mara dropped it into the river. It struck the surface and disappeared without a splash.

A pulse of green light travelled under the water, moving against the current. The creatures on the far bank shrieked. Their cries overlapped, almost human in their distress, and then faded into the trees.

Mara stood with empty hands, breathing hard. She did not know whether the button had protected her or attracted the creatures. She did know she had been carrying it without understanding it, and that the castle’s first gift had nearly led her into the dark.

She made herself continue toward the shelter.

The old shepherd’s hut was marked on the map as a square beside the river. It stood in a fold between two hills, roof low, walls made from mismatched stone. One corner had collapsed, but the remaining space was dry. Before entering, Mara circled the building. She found no tracks except her own and a scattering of tiny prints that began at the wall and ended in the grass, as if whatever made them had stepped out of the air.

Inside, she found an empty tin, a cracked cup, and a strip of old newspaper wedged beneath a stone. It had been used to stop a draught. The print was brittle. She eased it free and held it against the last light.

**EVACUATION OF NORTH HIGHLANDS: MINISTRY DENIES SCHOOL WAS USED AS REFUGE**

The word “Ministry” had been added in pencil above a blacked-out name. Below the headline, most of the article was missing. What remained mentioned a “temporary civilian relocation” and quoted a local official insisting there had been no children at the site. A second column, printed sideways on the fragment, showed a photograph of a line of people walking beside a railway. Their faces had been scratched away.

Mara sat down on the stone floor.

She had seen those denials before, but not so close to the place they described. The paper smelled faintly of smoke. At its edge, someone had written in a cramped hand: **They told us the children were taken south. They told my sister she had imagined the lights. They told us to stop asking.**

There was no name.

Mara thought of the two men at the café. The one who had said his sister saw blue lights. The one who told him she said many things. Had she been protecting her sister from disbelief, or protecting herself from memory? How many people had spent the rest of their lives being told they had not seen what they had seen?

She folded the clipping and put it into the notebook. The war had become a thing she could touch: paper, ash, scratches where faces had been. It was no longer only a sequence of contradictory accounts. The people who had lived through it had been forced to carry the contradiction as part of their ordinary lives.

A faint tapping came from the hut’s outer wall.

Mara went still.

Tap. Tap-tap.

Not the wind. The rhythm was deliberate, almost identical to the bird’s beak on the signal post. She edged toward the doorway, keeping to the shadow. A small figure crouched outside with its back to her. It wore a coat made from several sleeves sewn together. One foot was bare; the other was wrapped in a piece of blue curtain. It tapped a short stick against the stone and listened between each strike.

Mara thought of the elf beneath the bridge. She could see the long ears, the thin shoulders, the grey skin mottled like bruised fruit. The creature’s head turned a fraction. An ear lifted.

“Hello,” Mara said, then immediately wished she had chosen a better word.

The figure spun around. It was not the same elf. Its face was narrower, its left eye covered by a patch of cloth. It held the stick like a weapon.

“I’m not taking anything,” Mara said. “I’m just—”

The elf made a sharp clicking sound.

Mara took a biscuit from her pocket and placed it on the ground between them. She stepped back. The elf stared at the biscuit, then at her. Its lips moved. No sound came at first. Then it whispered, “Train.”

“Yes. I came on the train.”

“Train gone.”

“It has.”

The elf made a soft, broken laugh. “All gone.”

Mara wanted to ask what it meant. The elf snatched the biscuit and darted behind the wall. She heard it chewing, then a low mutter. Two words seemed to recur: “warm” and “door.”

“Do you know Snape?” Mara asked.

The chewing stopped.

She should not have said the name. She understood that at once. The air around the hut seemed to draw tight. A shadow passed over the doorway though nothing had crossed the sky. The elf appeared at the edge of the wall again, its one visible eye bright with fear.

“Not name,” it rasped.

“Why not?”

The elf struck the ground with its stick. “Name hears.”

Mara’s stomach tightened. She had heard of names having power in the magical world. She had dismissed that as folklore, or metaphor. The elf’s fear did not feel metaphorical.

She backed into the hut. “I won’t say it again.”

The elf watched her a moment longer, then darted away, moving so quickly its ragged coat barely stirred the grass.

Mara waited until the night had settled fully before she ate. She rationed two biscuits and a little water. The cold seeped through her clothes. She folded her rain jacket beneath her as a cushion and kept the knife within reach. Sleep came only in brief intervals.

Each time she woke, she heard a bell somewhere in the dark. It never rang twice in the same place.

Once, she woke to the sound of people talking outside. An old man’s voice said, “The school was empty.” Another answered, “Then who kept the lights on?” A third voice, a woman’s, said, “They sent the children away.”

Mara crept to the doorway. There was no one there. On the far hillside, pale figures moved in a line, carrying small points of blue light. She remembered the newspaper photograph, the faces scratched out. For a second she imagined the procession was the same one, still walking, never reaching wherever it had been sent.

She shut her eyes.

Near dawn, a voice spoke outside the shelter.

A rustle came from the brush. Mara turned, knife raised.

A small black shape stood at the river’s edge. A cat, thin as a bundle of sticks, with one torn ear and a collar of tarnished brass. It watched her, then looked toward the hillside. A greenish glow shone between the trees.

The cat crossed the stream without getting wet.

Mara stared after it. She had read of Kneazles and magical cats, but the creature might just as easily have been a trick of exhaustion. She pushed to her feet and followed the river downstream, away from the glow.

She found a shepherd’s shelter after midnight: three stone walls and a roof of slate, set into a fold in the hillside. Someone had left a tin cup there. It was clean. Beside it sat a small bundle of black feathers tied with red thread.

Mara did not touch the feathers. She ate two biscuits and wrapped herself in both sweaters. Sleep came in thin, broken strips. Each time she woke, she heard a bell somewhere in the dark, though it never rang twice in the same place.

Just before dawn, a voice spoke outside the shelter.

“American.”

Mara reached for her knife.

A pause. Then the voice, rough and small: “Snape’s.”

She sat very still.

Something scraped along the stone wall. A narrow shadow passed across the entrance. Then came the rapid clicking she had heard beneath the bridge—three quick notes, a pause, two more.

Mara did not answer.

When the morning light finally reached the valley, the shelter was empty. The feathers were gone. In their place, on the ground outside, lay a strip of grey cloth and a single button bearing a green serpent.

She picked up the button. It was warm.

Across the river, the ruined road climbed toward the high ground. Beyond it, hidden by mist, Hogwarts waited.

Mara put the button in her pocket and started walking.
