## Chapter Nine: The Names We Keep

The upper passage climbed in a slow spiral around the buried part of the castle. Mara kept one hand against the wall and the other near the strap of her pack. The injury in her ankle had stiffened during the descent, and each step sent a bright ache up her shin. She welcomed it. Pain belonged to the body. It did not pretend to be a message.

At the first landing she stopped to listen. Somewhere below, a door clicked shut. A murmur followed—low, adult, and indistinct. There was no childish cadence to it, no classroom call-and-response. It sounded like people speaking at the far end of a corridor, careful not to be overheard.

She checked her watch. Its hands had stopped at 4:17. Mara tapped the glass and turned the winder. The second hand trembled without advancing. The watch had been unreliable since the underwall, but the time had changed twice in the service court. She wrote it down anyway: *4:17. Watch stopped. Do not use it to navigate.*

A brass plate beside the narrow stair read RECORDS—LOWER STAFF. She wiped it with her sleeve, then climbed.

At the top, an oak door stood ajar. Beyond it lay a long room divided by rows of tall cabinets. Their drawers had swollen in the damp and were furred with grey mould. A narrow desk sat beneath a high window, though no daylight reached it. On the desk lay a ledger, its cover open and its pages held down by a cracked ceramic inkpot.

The room smelled of wet paper, cold ash, and something sweetly rotten.

Mara did not enter at once. Her torch found the damage first: a blast scar across the far wall, stone blackened and pitted as if something had burst against it from the corridor side. A section of ceiling had collapsed over the last bank of cabinets. Broken beams and slabs of masonry lay mixed with a heap of belongings—coats, a child-sized shoe, a cracked teacup, a bundle of letters tied with a strip of curtain. The heap had been searched or ransacked. Drawers gaped open; papers and personal things had been dragged out and trampled into the dust. A toppled cabinet leaned at an angle against the rubble, its contents spilled beneath it.

The war had not left this room untouched. It had left it interrupted.

Mara swept the torch over the threshold. White flakes clung to the floor. Plaster, she thought, until one shifted away from the beam. A moth, dead or nearly so, dragged itself into the dark beneath a cabinet.

She crossed to the desk. The ledger was ruled into columns: NAME, ROOM, MEAL, DOSE, BELL. Most entries were unreadable, their ink eaten through by water. Six names appeared in a vertical list. Five had been crossed out with a single black line. The sixth row was empty, though a faint depression showed where a name had once been pressed into the paper hard enough to mark the leaf beneath.

Beside that row, someone had drawn a square around a word faded almost entirely away. Mara could make out an E and the ending *-line*.

Aveline.

A brushing sound came from behind the cabinets, slow and deliberate, like cloth pulled across wood. Mara raised the torch. The narrow aisles were empty. A drawer slid open by itself. Something pale and thin reached over its edge—perhaps a finger, perhaps a strip of wet paper.

She moved toward the door. It swung inward and struck the warped frame. The latch clicked.

A voice came from the far end of the room. It was adult, low, and roughened by distance. “Which one?”

Mara gripped the torch. “Who’s there?”

A pause. Then the voice repeated, with the weary insistence of someone asking a question that had already been answered badly: “Which one?”

The drawer slid farther out. Mara turned back to the ledger. If the room was asking about names, she needed to know what it meant. Beneath the empty row she found an impression showing through from the page below. She turned the leaf carefully.

Hallowell.

The first name was indistinct, the date column torn away. The surname was unmistakable. A second name had been pressed beneath it in the same hand, too faint to read. A line connected the two entries and ended in a small six-place mark.

“Not yours,” said another adult voice, this one nearer the collapsed wall.

Mara turned sharply. No one stood among the cabinets. The open drawer was empty now.

It was an old record, not proof of anything. Hallowell could be coincidence: a staff name, a donor, a family who had once sent a child to Hogwarts. She had carried her surname into every room as if it were a key. That did not make it one.

A black thread emerged from the seam between the pages. It crossed the H, forked at the w, and continued toward the empty row. Mara shut the ledger.

The latch turned by itself, but the door would not open. Something held it from the other side. A wet scrape travelled beneath the cabinets. A narrow black line crawled across the floor, branching around the desk legs.

Mara took out the serpent button. It was cold. She pressed it in her palm. Nothing changed.

“Clear,” she whispered, remembering the elf’s gesture: not a word, a way.

A narrow service door stood behind the iron shelves, partly hidden by a hanging canvas screen. The route to it crossed the spreading black. Mara shifted the torch and pulled the ledger toward her. It was heavy, its damp cover swollen. She did not know why she took it—perhaps because it contained her name, perhaps because leaving it felt like surrender.

The black reached the desk. Mara dropped the inkpot into its path. It shattered. The black divided around the glossy spill, its edges drawing thin. It did not retreat. It hesitated.

She ran. The canvas caught on her pack; she wrenched free, leaving a strap torn loose, and shoved through the service door. A narrow stair descended between two walls. She pulled the door shut. Something struck it from the other side, once. The wood shuddered and went still.

Halfway down, a shallow niche held a cracked wooden bowl and a folded scrap of cloth. Three grooves had been stitched along one edge. Mara did not touch it.

A whisper came from below. “You brought it.”

The house-elf crouched on the next step. Its spine bent into a sharp S beneath skin stretched tight as damp parchment. One shoulder sat far higher than the other. Its long arms hung unevenly, one nearly brushing the floor, the other twisted backward at the elbow. Its feet pointed in opposite directions. A blackened band around its wrist had spread across its palm in branching veins, and three fingers curled the wrong way, twitching as if pulled by invisible strings. Its mouth was too wide for its narrow face. One eye fixed on Mara; the other watched the stair behind her.

It held out a narrow iron key.

“Service stair,” it said. “Not records.”

“Why help me?”

“Not help. Choice.” Its eyes moved to the ledger beneath her arm. “I open this. You leave book.”

“You want me to put it back?”

“I want it not carried. Names go with it. Black follows names.”

“What do you want?”

For a moment the elf’s mouth worked without sound. The black veins tightened across its palm. “Freedom.”

“From me?”

“From black.” It pressed its hand against its chest as if something inside were trying to pull it apart. “It takes no. Takes names. Makes hands open.” Its crooked fingers spasmed. “I want my no back.”

The voices drifted through the masonry again. They were adults, several of them, speaking in fragments. Mara could not make out the words, only the direction: farther down, beyond the stair. One voice said something that might have been “this way.” Another answered, too faint to understand. The sound tugged at her attention like a path through fog.

The elf lowered the key. “Then no door.”

Mara looked at the Hallowell ledger. It might be the only evidence she would ever find. The room behind her was already giving the black a way out. If she carried the book, she might carry the danger with it. If she left it, the record—and whatever remained of her past in it—could be lost.

She set the ledger on the stone floor, careful not to open it. “I’m leaving it here.”

“Not giving.”

“No.” She could not make herself say more. “I may come back.”

The elf studied her, then slid the key across the step. “For now is a kind of taking.”

“I know.”

The elf nodded once and vanished into a narrow gap between the tiles. Mara picked up the key. The ledger remained on the floor, its cover darkening with damp.

She descended to the disused laundry. Stone troughs lined the walls. Most were dry; one held a shallow sheet of water so still that the torchlight seemed painted onto it. Above the basin someone had scratched: KEEP THE CLEAR WAY CLEAR.

A soft hum stirred from Mara’s pack. The bell’s clapper was still caught. She brought it near the water. Beneath the surface a pale shape moved from one end of the trough to the other, too thin to be a fish and too slow to be a reflection. She wrapped the bell and put it away.

A knock came from the laundry’s far door: three taps, then two, a pause, one tap. Mara did not open it. A strip of paper slid beneath the boards. Six circles had been drawn on it. Five were filled in. The sixth was open, with a line running out toward the edge. On the reverse: DO NOT COUNT.

The voices sounded again, farther off now, drawing her toward the passage beyond the laundry. “—down here—” one seemed to say. Another answered. Their words remained indistinct, but their direction did not. Mara knew the sound was bait, or could be. She followed the white marks anyway, keeping to the narrow route.

At a bend, the corridor split. The marks continued along the left wall toward a handleless door. The right-hand passage sloped down toward the voices and a low sound like water running through pipes. Mara took the left route. The marks began fading behind her, one by one, but she did not turn back.

The voices followed through the stone. An adult voice said her name—not loudly, not as a call, but as if reading it from a list.

“Mara Hallowell.”

She stopped. The bell gave a faint hum. She did not answer. She did not open the handleless door.

A second voice, dry and irritated, came from somewhere below. “If the bell is heard, do not answer it.”

Mara took out the sealed cylinder marked S. She had carried it for days without finding a safe way to open it. A narrow seam had appeared along its side. Beneath the seal, someone had scratched a line of tiny writing, nearly hidden by tarnish:

**If the bell is heard, do not answer it. S.**

The voice might have been Snape’s. Mara had never heard him speak; she could only suspect it from the terse wording and the impatience in its tone. The warning felt genuine—not a promise of safety, but an attempt to keep someone from making a known mistake.

From behind the wall came the bell’s soft, three-note hum. Mara put the cylinder away without trying the seal.

The voices had moved farther down the passage. They seemed to be leading toward the lower rooms, toward the sound of water. Mara kept to the marked route, but every step away from them felt like a step away from the Hallowell record too. She pictured the ledger on the floor, the damp spreading through its cover, the black thread crossing her name.

She had left it to keep the black from following. She had also left it because the elf had asked, and because she could not bear to make the danger worse for one more chance at knowing who she was.

The thought did not comfort her. It made the loss sharper.

At the next landing, Mara wrote in her notebook: *Ledger left behind. Hallowell entry may be lost. Do not pretend this was not a cost.* The ink ran where her hand shook.

The distant voices murmured below. One of them said, “This way.” They were leading toward danger; she knew that now. She kept walking, carrying the bell and the cylinder, and regretting both what she had taken and what she had left behind.

She did not know whether the Hallowell record had been her past. She knew only that she had chosen to leave it in the dark—and that somewhere behind her, the castle still had her name.
