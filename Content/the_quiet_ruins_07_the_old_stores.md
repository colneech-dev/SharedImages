## Chapter Seven: The Old Stores

The child’s voice did not count again.

Mara descended until the stairs narrowed to a steep stone throat. Her torchlight reached only a few steps ahead. Water ticked somewhere in the walls, each drop separated from the next by a long pause. She kept one hand on the damp rail and the other near the pack at her shoulder, where the sealed cylinder lay wrapped among the papers.

The scent of bitter herbs travelled with her. It had become so familiar that she only noticed it when it changed: a trace of smoke, a mineral coldness, or the sour smell of water that had stood too long in darkness. Now the herbs grew sharper as she went down, distinct enough that she could almost imagine separate ingredients beneath the scent—something dry and woody, something green crushed between fingers.

She thought of the ledger fragment: wormwood, feverfew, six measures of bitter draught. No child was to be given the second bell dose.

The note had not explained what the dose did. It had not explained why a child might be given it at all.

The stairs ended beneath a low arch. Mara ducked through into a narrow corridor. The walls were rougher here than in the service court, their blocks set unevenly, their mortar black with damp. A series of shallow grooves ran along the right side at shoulder height. Some had been polished smooth by hands. Others were hidden under pale deposits like dried salt.

She unfolded the map fragment beneath the torch. Its route descended from the service court, bent beneath a block of rooms, then ended at **OLD STORES—SEALED AFTER BREACH**. The scale was too vague to be useful. The line might represent this passage, or the one below it, or a route that no longer existed.

A thin chalk arrow had been drawn on the map beside the final words. Its point was almost hidden in the crease.

Mara followed the corridor.

The child’s shoe lay near the first turn, pushed against the wall as if someone had set it aside to clear the way. Its leather had shrunk and split at the toe. A dark line of dried mud marked the sole.

She crouched and looked inside. Empty. No foot, no scrap of paper, no hidden message. Just a shoe small enough to belong to one of the children in the drawing.

Mara stood and brushed grit from her palm. She disliked how quickly her mind made the connection. Six figures in a drawing, six portions on a slip, six places in a room. One shoe in a corridor. The facts could belong together; they could also have been gathered from different years and different lives. She had learned in the cottage that a record could survive long after its context had vanished. She had not learned how to stop inventing that context for it.

The corridor bent left. Ahead, a door stood ajar, its edge no more than a hand’s width from the stone jamb. A circle interrupted at the top had been carved into the lintel. Three short grooves lay beneath it.

The same pattern again.

Mara paused. The air moving through the gap was colder than the corridor air. It carried the bitter-herb smell and the faint odour of rusted iron. She listened for the small, bright note she had heard above. No note came.

She pressed the door with the flat of her hand. It opened without a sound.

Beyond it lay a long, low room braced with iron. Cabinets lined one wall, their doors hanging open or missing. Hooks and empty brackets covered the opposite wall. A cracked bucket, a coil of chain and a wooden-handled brush remained where they had been left. Dust coated everything except for a narrow trail that crossed the floor toward a heavier door at the far end.

A tarnished plaque above the door read **STORES—SEALED**. A downward arrow had been cut beneath the words.

Mara checked the map, then the room. The label matched, but the map did not show the door. It showed only a dark rectangle and a line stopping short of it. The mapmaker might have drawn the store room from memory. They might have meant that the route continued below.

She began with the cabinets.

The first held grit and a strip of grey cloth. The second held three broken jars, their paper labels melted into brown smears. The third had a swollen drawer. Mara tugged it once, then braced a boot against the cabinet and pulled harder. The drawer came free with a crack that seemed impossibly loud.

Inside lay a tarnished teaspoon, a stack of paper labels and a grey envelope. She opened the envelope and found a card folded in half.

**Six cups. Six stools. Six blankets.**

**Do not leave the sixth place empty.**

A different hand had added, beneath the first lines: **Aveline says the room is too small.**

Mara read the name twice. Aveline had appeared on the inventory slip in the service court, beside the notation about six portions and a key to the north stair. There had been no surname and no date. The name looked old-fashioned, preserved in the ink like something that had outlasted whoever wrote it.

It did not belong to her. That should have been a relief. Instead, seeing it twice made the service rooms feel linked by a history she could not yet read.

She folded the card and tucked it beside the inventory slip. Six cups, six stools, six blankets. A room too small. She imagined children sharing a narrow space and immediately distrusted the image. The evidence suggested people, perhaps children, but did not say who they were, why they had gathered, or whether Aveline had been one of them.

Something scraped behind the sealed door.

Mara raised the torch. The scrape came again, slow and heavy, like a chair dragged over stone. She approached only as far as the disturbed strip of dust near the threshold. The iron bands were dark with age. A round plate had been fitted at the centre, stamped with three circles. The middle circle was split by a vertical groove.

There was no handle. No keyhole. No visible seam.

Mara leaned toward it, then stopped before her ear touched the metal. The service-room voice and the mother-voice had taught her nothing except that answering could be dangerous.

A breath moved behind the door.

She stepped away.

“Who’s there?” she called, and disliked herself for asking.

No reply.

The serpent button warmed inside her pack. Its small, steady heat touched her ribs through the cloth. She placed a hand over it.

“Not now,” she whispered.

A click came from the corridor behind her.

Mara turned. Something small and pale crossed the open doorway—one long hand, a narrow shoulder—and vanished around the corner. It moved too quickly for her to see whether it had been the twisted elf, a child, or something else entirely.

She did not follow. The elf’s broken warning returned in scraps: mouth, door, water black, water clear. She had understood no more of it at the time than she did now.

Under the next cabinet, a loose paper edge protruded from the dust. She crouched and eased it free. It was a torn ledger leaf. The ink had run, but several lines remained legible:

**—one tin dried wormwood**

**—two boxes powdered root**

**—one jar feverfew, cracked**

**—six measures, bitter draught, hold for E.**

At the bottom, in a different hand: **No child is to be given the second bell dose.**

Mara folded the ledger leaf and put it away. The second bell had appeared on the evacuation list, and now it appeared beside a dose withheld from children. It might be a potion’s name, an instruction tied to a signal, or both. The page offered no answer.

The sealed door knocked once.

Mara backed toward the centre of the room. A second knock came, softer than the first.

The downward arrow beneath the plaque pointed to the floor. She searched the stones, finding a long slab among the square ones. Three grooves marked its edge. Beneath dirt and waxy residue, an iron ring had been sunk into the stone.

She cleared it with her fingers and pulled. The slab lifted a fraction, then dropped. She braced a boot against the neighbouring stone and tried again. The ring bit into her fingers. The slab scraped sideways, opening a dark seam.

Cold air breathed up through it, carrying the smell of wet earth and ash.

A square shaft descended beneath the room. Iron rungs vanished into darkness. Mara lowered her torch. The light picked out six rungs and no floor.

She set down her pack and tested the nearest rung. It held. After securing the pack to the ring with its strap, she lowered it into the shaft. The bag bumped against the wall on its way down, then struck stone somewhere beneath her.

A child’s voice rose from the shaft.

“Two.”

Mara held still.

The voice came from below, not from behind the sealed door. She listened for another speaker, but there was only the one small voice.

“Three.”

The opening remained above her. The passage back to the service court remained behind it. She could climb up, return to the upper corridor, and try to find a safer route. She could also imagine the way closing as soon as she turned her back. The castle had given her no reason to trust either possibility.

“Four.”

Mara lowered herself onto the first rung.

“Five.”

She descended.

The shaft was narrower than it had looked from above. Her shoulders brushed stone, and the rungs were slick beneath her palms. The pack swung against the wall, its strap looped around her wrist. She descended slowly, counting the iron bars without meaning to.

At the seventh rung the child’s voice stopped.

Mara waited. The darkness below had the shape of a room, though she could not see its boundaries. She angled the torch down. Its light reached a stone floor and the rim of a shallow basin.

“Six,” she whispered.

Something tapped inside the basin.

She continued.

The last rung ended several feet above the floor. Mara lowered the pack first, then dropped and landed with her knees bent. Pain jolted through her left ankle. She leaned against the wall until it passed, listening for another tap.

The chamber was small and almost round. Its stones had been cut smoother than those in the corridor. A basin stood in the centre, shallow as a washbowl, its black surface perfectly still. Six notches were cut into the rim. Five held a grey, waxy residue. The sixth was clean.

Mara kept to the wall. A narrow shelf ran around the chamber at waist height. Six dull green cups sat upon it. One had been turned upside down. The other five were upright and empty.

“Six cups,” she murmured. “Six stools. Six blankets.”

There were no stools, blankets, or signs that anyone had used the place recently. Only a pale rectangle on the floor where something had once rested. A box, perhaps. A crate.

The smell of herbs was strongest beside the basin. Mara took out the ledger leaf and held it near the torch. Its blurred lines named ingredients and measures, but not what the draught did. She remembered the note: no child was to be given the second bell dose. If it was a potion, why call it by a bell? If it was an instruction, what happened at the second bell?

A ripple crossed the basin.

Mara froze. There was no breeze. The surface reflected a round, wavering flame. Beneath that reflection, a shadow moved against the direction of the light.

The tap sounded again, this time behind her.

She turned. The wall was bare except for a narrow seam. A small iron latch sat at knee height, nearly hidden beneath mineral deposits. She brushed grit away with her sleeve. It shifted under her thumb.

She did not pull it.

Instead she examined the room. Three grooves had been cut into the floor directly beneath the basin, paired with a broken circle. The mark again: on the cloth, the doors, the walls. It might be a warning, a maker’s sign, or part of some mechanism whose pieces were scattered through the ruins. She could not make it mean anything without pretending she knew more than she did.

The serpent button warmed against her chest. Mara drew it out, still wrapped in grey cloth. One corner of the cloth showed three parallel grooves. The brass serpent’s curled body seemed darker than before.

She did not bring it near the basin.

A folded strip of paper lay beneath the nearest cup. She slid it free with two fingers. The writing was neat and small, though the paper had gone soft at the edges.

**Keep the clear water for washing.**

**The black is not water.**

The final line had been scratched through so violently that the paper had torn. Mara tilted it toward the torch, trying to read the indentations beneath. One word might have been *drink*. Another might have been *bell*. The paper could not tell her which.

She put it away.

The basin gave a slow, wet creak.

Mara stepped back. Its black surface had begun to rise, not spilling over the rim but lifting into a dome. The glossy skin held together as it swelled, like a bubble stretched too far. Torchlight bent across it. Something pale shifted underneath.

She turned for the shaft.

The opening above was a square of darkness. Her pack lay beside the lowest rung, its strap having slipped from the iron ring. She grabbed it. The basin made a sound like a breath drawn through clenched teeth.

Mara slung the pack over one shoulder and climbed.

The first rung held. The second did too. Below, something touched the floor with a soft slap.

She climbed faster. The pack dragged against the wall and snagged at the opening. She shoved it upward and felt the strap catch. Her boots skidded on wet iron.

A hand closed around her ankle.

The fingers were cold and long. Mara kicked hard. Her heel struck something yielding, and the grip tightened. From the basin came a sound like a sheet being pulled from a bed.

She looked down.

A pale shape stretched from the basin across the floor, thin as soaked cloth. Fingers clasped her boot. It had no face she could distinguish, only a slick bulge where a head might have been. The rest kept flowing from the basin, folding over itself without breaking.

Mara kicked again. Its grip loosened. She climbed, scraping her shin against a rung. One hand reached the opening; she hooked an elbow over the lip and hauled herself up. The pack caught once more. She yanked the strap free, rolled onto the floor above, and shoved the stone slab aside.

It scraped shut just as something struck from beneath. Dust fell around her. A second blow rattled the iron ring. Then came a third, gentler than the first two, almost measured.

Mara backed away.

Her breath came too quickly. Her left ankle throbbed; her right palm had split where a rung had cut it. She pressed the bleeding hand against her coat and stared at the slab. No liquid seeped through the seam. No fingers appeared.

A tiny sound travelled through the room, far below.

“Six.”

Mara flinched, then forced herself to check the room. She had climbed back into the iron-braced stores. The heavy door still stood at the far end, its round plate unchanged. The card, ledger leaf, and water note were in her pocket. The sealed cylinder remained inside her pack. The serpent button had cooled.

She studied the trapdoor. The map’s arrow had pointed down, but that did not explain the chamber. Perhaps the basin held something useful, or perhaps it was meant to keep people away from whatever lay lower. The note said the black was not water. It was a warning, though not a complete one.

She wondered whether the one-eyed elf had been trying to tell her this. *Water runs black, water runs clear.* Or perhaps it had meant the reverse: that clear water was dangerous and black was only a name. The elf had spoken in fragments. Mara had supplied the grammar herself.

She would not go down again.

The sealed door gave a faint click.

Mara turned. A thin green line glimmered across the middle circle in the iron plate. The serpent button warmed once, briefly, like a coal stirred beneath ash.

She took a step toward the door and stopped.

The line vanished.

A second plaque was fixed to the left wall, nearly hidden behind a fallen cabinet. Mara shifted the cabinet enough to read it: **RETURN TO SERVICE COURT BY NORTH PASSAGE**. Beneath it, in smaller letters, someone had scratched: **Do not use after the second bell.**

The engraved words were formal; the warning was hurried and uneven. Beside it, someone had carved three grooves beneath an interrupted circle.

Mara compared it with the patterns on the door, basin, and cloth. Each differed slightly. The grooves were not always the same length; sometimes the circle was open at the top, sometimes split through its centre. It might be one symbol modified over time, or several marks that happened to resemble each other. She could not tell.

Still, *after the second bell* joined the warning in the ledger. This place had been organized around some recurring event. The bell, the dose, the children, the water: pieces of a system whose purpose had been lost.

She unfolded the map. Its chalk arrow pointed toward the dark rectangle marked **OLD STORES—SEALED AFTER BREACH**. The paper’s crease shifted the point; when she turned the fragment, it seemed to point back toward the service court. For a moment she thought the map had changed. Then she saw how handling had distorted the fold.

She smoothed it and read a faint line beneath the rectangle: **lower store, water test**.

Mara’s mouth went dry. She had found the test. She had not found what it tested.

The heavy door knocked once.

She stepped away. The blow was softer than before, but a voice followed.

“Six places.”

The speaker sounded old, its words strained through distance. It was not the child from the shaft, nor the voice that had used her mother’s voice. Mara heard no breath or movement behind the iron. The silence settled again.

“Who are you?” she called.

After a pause, the voice answered, “Not yours.”

The words might have warned her about the room, Aveline, or the six places. They might have been a fragment of an old recording triggered by her voice. Mara had no way to distinguish mechanism from presence here.

A scrape sounded behind the door, followed by the faintest ringing note.

One bell.

The sound was so thin she might have imagined it. It travelled through iron and stone, trembling against her teeth. She remembered the railway note: **Do not answer the bell.** She had not answered. She did not speak now.

A second note began, deeper and slower.

Mara ran.

She crossed the room and reached the corridor. The tone followed her, but did not grow louder. It seemed to come from beneath her feet, from the stone itself. At the turn, her torch beam caught the small pale figure again. It stood at the far end with one hand against the wall, its head bent at an angle too sharp for comfort.

Mara halted.

The figure raised its hand and pointed toward the stairs. For one irrational second she thought it was helping her. Then it turned its palm outward. Seven long fingers; two fused at their tips. A brass key hung from a cord around its wrist.

The elf.

Its sideways mouth worked. A wet click came, then a wheeze.

“Black… not below.”

Mara could not tell whether it meant the black was not below, or that the danger called black was not confined to the lower chamber. She took one step toward it.

The elf recoiled. Its shoulder struck stone, and it folded in on itself with a pained hiss. Beneath the ragged fabric, its ribs shifted in the wrong direction. It looked less like a creature preparing to attack than one trying to escape a blow that had not yet landed.

“Wait,” Mara said. “The second bell—what is it?”

The elf’s one visible eye fixed on the pack. The sealed cylinder seemed to pull at the fabric, though Mara knew that was impossible. Its mouth opened wider.

“Not for—”

A bell rang again, close enough to shake dust from the ceiling.

The elf darted around the corner. Mara heard its quick, uneven steps fade toward the stairs.

She followed, but kept her distance. At the first turn, the chalk arrow she had noticed earlier appeared to point toward the shaft room. Now it seemed to point back the way she had come. She touched the wall beside it. The chalk was dry. No fresh line marked the stone; no handprint showed in the dust.

She left it alone.

The stairs climbed toward the service court, and the air grew less cold. Wet earth gave way to stone dust, old oil, and the bitter herbal trace that seemed to have followed her from the stores. At each landing she paused to listen. Nothing followed. Nothing called her by name.

Halfway up, she found another chalk mark: six short lines in a row, the last drawn through twice. Beneath it someone had written **A**. The letter was small and careful. It resembled the A on the inventory slip, though resemblance was not proof of a common hand.

Mara put the slip away.

At the top, the service-court door remained open. The room beyond looked unchanged, though the strip of light beneath the far door had gone dark. She reached the threshold and waited. She had expected the castle to recognize her again, to greet her or answer in a borrowed voice. It did not.

She crossed into the service court.

The child’s drawing remained pinned to the wall. Six small figures stood beneath a bell-shaped mark. The sixth had no face. The paper’s lower edge had curled, exposing writing on the back that Mara had not noticed before.

**If the sixth place is empty, do not call the name.**

She stared at it. Her mind supplied Aveline, then Elspeth, then the initial E from the ledger. She could not tell which name the warning meant, or whether it meant any of them. The line might have been written by a child, an adult trying to frighten children into obedience, or someone who understood the danger and had no time to explain it.

Mara pressed the paper flat and stepped away.

The cylinder’s wax seals were unbroken. She checked them twice. The silver S caught the weak light. Snape’s name had not appeared in the lower stores, but the herbs, hidden route, and sealed object suggested that someone had prepared these places for a purpose. She wanted to believe it had been his. Then there would be a plan in the ruins, a mind behind the scattered evidence, useful objects waiting for someone clever enough to find them.

But the papers spoke of children, doses, and bells. They did not speak of Snape. The workroom she had glimpsed beneath the wall remained sealed behind rubble. Her hope was not proof.

She lifted the pack onto her shoulders. The serpent button pressed against her breastbone, cool now, almost ordinary. She considered leaving it behind. The elf had called it a mouth and a door; the pattern tied it to these lower rooms. Every instinct that had kept her alive suggested that carrying it deeper was reckless.

She kept it.

The return plaque had named a north passage, but its warning forbade using it after the second bell. She had heard two tones. Whether they were real bells or echoes in the walls, the route was now barred by a warning she had no reason to ignore. She would have to retrace her path through the service court and find another way, or wait until she understood what the ringing meant.

From somewhere far below came one quiet tap.

Mara did not answer.

She turned toward the upper passage, carrying the six-place card, the ledger leaf, and the water warning. Behind the walls, the bell did not ring again. But as she left the service court, a small, steady warmth returned beneath her coat.
