## Chapter Six: The Service Court

Mara remained on the landing until the last trace of the voice had disappeared into the stone.

She kept her eyes on the door. The iron plate had gone still. The three circles pressed into the wood looked ordinary again, shallow dents darkened by age. Beneath the threshold, there was no green light, no line of illumination to prove the door had opened at all. Only a seam of dust and the faint smell of rain.

“Welcome back, Mara.”

The words had not sounded like her mother. They had not sounded like the dry voice that had said not yet. They had been almost kind. That was worse. A threat announced itself. Kindness invited an answer.

She had not answered. She had not said where she was. She had not told the voice that she had never been here before. The warning had worked, if the warning was meant for her. Or the voice had chosen to stop speaking.

Mara forced herself to look away.

The stairwell curled down into darkness. The route back to the lower rooms was still there, though she could no longer trust that it would lead to the same rooms. She could go down, wait for dawn, search for another way out. The crawlspace near the broken threshold might have sealed itself; the creature might still be there. Even if she reached the underwall, she would be returning through passages that had doubled her chalk marks and changed direction without turning. She could attempt it. She could not call it a plan.

The iron plate was cold beneath her fingertips.

She had made her choice before the bell rang twice. That mattered. The voice had not chosen for her, and the door had not dragged her through. She had wanted to know what lay inside Hogwarts; she still did. Fear had not canceled that desire. It had only made the price visible.

Mara checked her pack straps, tightened the knot in the torn one, and put the phone in low-power mode. The battery showed twelve percent. Her knife was secure. The sealed cylinder remained inside the padded pocket, and the serpent button was warm through the cloth where she had wrapped it. She took the chalk from her pocket and marked the landing wall with a small arrow pointing toward the stairs.

Then she touched the door again.

The wood felt damp, though no water showed on her hand. She leaned close, careful not to press her ear against it.

Nothing.

She turned the plate.

It resisted, then yielded. A thin green line appeared beneath the door. This time the bell did not sound. The seam widened slowly, and the smell of rain came through, carrying ash and that bitter herbal scent she associated with the sealed cylinder. She paused, waiting for the voice to repeat her name.

It did not.

Mara set her shoulder against the door and pushed. It opened inward by several inches, then stopped. She pushed harder. The hinge groaned. A thin line of cold air crossed her face. She kept one hand on the plate and swept the phone beam through the gap.

A flagstone floor. A narrow strip of open sky. A wall opposite, broken at the top. No movement.

She widened the opening enough to slip through sideways.

The service court was smaller than she had imagined. It lay between the inner castle and a high outer wall, a long enclosed space open to the weather above. A square of night sky showed between roofless walls. Rain had recently fallen; the stones shone with wetness, though the air was still. On either side, narrow doorways led into dark service corridors. At the far end, a mound of collapsed masonry blocked what might once have been an archway.

Mara stepped out and turned immediately to face the door.

It stood in the wall without frame or handle, almost indistinguishable from the surrounding stones. The wooden face had vanished. The three circles were carved into a block at chest height, and the iron plate was set into its centre. She searched for a latch, a hinge, some practical explanation. The wall was solid around it.

She touched the circles.

A brief warmth crossed her fingertips, then faded.

Mara withdrew her hand. “You are not a mouth,” she muttered. “You’re a door.”

From somewhere within the wall came a dry clicking, three beats and a pause. She backed away.

The service court answered with ordinary sounds: the drip of water from a broken gutter, wind combing through the roofless space, a loose piece of slate ticking softly against stone. Ordinary was not the same as safe, but it let her breathe.

She took a photograph of the door and its markings. The phone’s screen flickered, displaying the image for a moment before the picture collapsed into a black field. The battery icon showed eleven percent.

“Fine.” She shut the camera app. “I saw it.”

She moved toward the centre of the court, where the surface dipped into a shallow drain. Leaves and black grit had collected there. In the far corner, a rusted iron handcart lay on its side, one wheel missing. A heap of pale rags rested beside it. Mara kept away from the rags. The wind shifted and carried a smell of damp wool, old smoke, and something sweetly rotten.

She studied the court’s two doorways. The left one was narrow, its lintel cracked. The right was wider and had a faded sign nailed above it. Most of the writing had flaked away, but she could make out **KITCHEN** and, below it, a line of smaller letters: **NO ENTRY AFTER SECOND BELL**.

Mara looked up at the open sky. Clouds moved in a low, ragged sheet. No bell sounded. She tried to estimate the time from the moon and failed. The air felt like deep night, although it might have been only late evening. In the tunnels, time had become another thing she could not trust.

She turned to the left doorway. A line had been scratched into its stone jamb: three short grooves, then a longer one. The same family of marks, not the same symbol. The grooves were polished at their edges, as if many hands had touched them. Below them, a faint arrow pointed inward.

She went to the right doorway instead.

The kitchen sign might be a trap, but it was also a direction. A place that had been used by servants and stores would have had water, food, and perhaps a route upward. She did not intend to enter blindly. She stood at the threshold and listened.

There was a faint tapping inside.

Three taps. A pause. Three more.

Mara stepped back.

A second later, the sound came again, from behind her.

She turned sharply. Nothing stood in the court. The pale rags had not moved. The concealed door in the wall remained shut.

The tapping sounded once more, this time from the kitchen doorway. It was not an echo. The beats had a brittle, deliberate quality, as though a fingernail touched wood.

The warning on the sign said no entry after the second bell. There had been two strikes. If the sign was old, the instruction might no longer matter. If it was new, someone had meant it for a person arriving now.

Mara chose the left passage.

The stone corridor beyond was narrow and sloped slightly upward. A shallow groove ran along one side of the floor, carrying water toward the court. Here the smell of rain gave way to cold ash. She walked with her back close to the wall, pausing every few steps to listen. Behind her, the court stayed quiet.

The passage opened into a square vestibule. A narrow stair climbed ahead, its first five steps intact and the rest broken away. To the left, an arch led into a room with a low ceiling. The doorway had been partly blocked with a wooden screen, rotted at the bottom. The wood was blank except for a child’s drawing in charcoal: six small figures in a row, each with a round head and straight arms. The seventh figure stood apart, its face carefully rubbed away.

Mara stopped so suddenly her pack swung forward.

She crouched, keeping the knife ready. The drawing was faint, as if someone had tried to erase it without success. A small line beneath the figures might have been a path or a bell-rope. She recognized the uneven proportions from the cupboard drawing in the lower room. Six children again. The face rubbed away again.

She looked behind her. The corridor was empty.

The picture did not name the children or explain where they had gone. It only repeated the count, as if the person who drew it had wanted that number remembered. Mara felt the urge to connect it to the evacuation list and Elspeth; she made herself stop at what she could actually see. Six figures. One erased face. No names.

She raised the phone to photograph it. The display went black. She pressed the side button. Nothing happened.

She set the dead phone in her pocket. For several seconds, the only light came from the opening behind her, a narrow slice of clouded sky. She could leave. The thought arose without drama. She could go back through the service court, descend to the underwall, and keep moving until daylight. She would have to risk the collapsing route and the creature below, but she would be choosing the risk she already understood.

Then something scraped behind the wooden screen.

Mara drew the knife.

A pale hand slid through the gap at the bottom. It was too small to be an adult’s. The fingers were long and thin, the skin translucent in the faint light. A dark line ran under the wrist, like a groove cut into the flesh.

The hand opened and closed once.

Mara stepped backward, blade raised. The fingers withdrew.

A child’s voice whispered from beyond the screen. “You came back.”

Mara’s breath caught. She did not answer.

The screen shivered, though nothing touched it from her side. A second whisper followed, weaker than the first.

“Not you.”

The corridor went quiet.

Mara did not approach the screen. She watched the gap at its base and backed toward the vestibule, keeping the knife between her and the doorway. Her heel struck the first stair. The broken staircase rose only a few steps before ending in a jagged fall of stone. She had taken it for a way up; it was only the remains of one.

The chamber to the left had no other exit that she could see. The court behind her had two. She could return there and test the kitchen door, or cross to the opposite passage. She looked once more at the six figures on the screen. The erased face seemed less like damage now and more like a deliberate omission, though she could not tell who had made it or why.

She lowered the knife but did not put it away.

The little hand had not reached for her. The voice had said not you. It might have been a warning. It might have been disappointment. Either way, the room knew a distinction she did not.

Mara turned back through the corridor to the court.

The rags were gone.

She stopped in the threshold. The handcart remained, one wheel missing. The heap beside it was no longer there. In the wet grit, a narrow trail led from the corner to the kitchen doorway. The prints were bare, small, and pointed inward. At the threshold, they stopped.

The kitchen door stood open wider than before.

Mara could hear no tapping now. She walked around the courtyard’s edge, giving the open entrance a wide berth, and approached the opposite passage. It was the one marked by the three grooves and longer stroke. The sign above it had been torn down. A rectangle of cleaner stone showed where it had hung.

She entered.

The corridor sloped down, contrary to the arrow. After several yards, it divided. The left branch smelled of cold water. The right of ashes and bitter herbs. Mara touched the cylinder through her pack. It remained cool, but she could feel the faintest vibration under her fingers, a tremor like a distant engine.

She chose the right branch.

A series of alcoves opened along the wall. Each held a shallow stone basin, dry and blackened at the bottom. In one, a scatter of silver-grey feathers had gathered. In another, a small pile of buttons: wood, bone, brass, and one dark metal serpent with its head broken off. Mara’s own button warmed beneath its cloth wrapping.

She did not touch the pile.

At the third alcove, a folded sheet of parchment had been wedged beneath the basin. Its edge was browned, but it had not crumbled. Mara leaned close and opened it with the tip of her knife. The handwriting was cramped, written in black ink. A list of supplies filled the top half: powdered moonstone, dried nettle, three jars of ash, clean water. The lower half had been torn away. Beneath the list, in a different hand, someone had written: **If she asks for the store-room, send her to the north stair. Do not call her by the name on the label.**

Mara read the line twice.

She looked at the empty corridor behind her. “Who?”

No one answered.

The note could have been about a woman who had worked here, or a prisoner, or someone whose name had been dangerous to speak. The instruction sounded practical rather than mystical. That did not make it less disturbing. It made the castle’s habits feel older and more ordinary: people had learned to protect one another by withholding names.

She folded the parchment along its old crease and put it in her pocket. She would not take it if the place seemed to be asking her to. But it was a record, not a relic. It might help her understand the route and the warnings.

Beyond the alcoves, a low door opened into a store-room. Its hinges were rusted. The room beyond smelled sharply of dried plants and soot. Shelves lined two walls, most of them fallen. A worktable stood in the centre, its top scarred by knife marks and rings left by jars. On the far wall, a blackened patch formed the outline of a hanging cabinet. The cabinet itself was gone.

Mara recognized the bitter scent now. It was stronger here, and close to the smell that had drifted through the door on the landing. She set her pack down but kept one strap over her shoulder. The sealed cylinder lay in its pocket. She did not take it out.

A narrow drawer under the worktable had survived. It was locked with a simple brass catch. She tested it. The catch lifted easily, and the drawer slid out.

Inside lay a bundle of papers tied with green thread. The paper had yellowed, the thread faded nearly to grey. A folded inventory slip rested on top, pinned in place by a small, darkened brass tack. Its edges were brittle, but the centre had been protected from light. Mara eased the tack free with the tip of her knife and unfolded the slip.

The first lines listed jars and ingredients in a cramped hand. Beside several entries, someone had drawn small circles, then crossed them out. At the bottom, in a more careful script, was a single name: **Aveline**. No surname. No date. A faint brown thumbprint blurred the final letters.

Mara stopped breathing.

She turned the paper over. The back bore a neat column of figures and a brief notation: **six portions; one withheld; return the key to the north stair**. The number six pulled her thoughts back to the children’s drawing. It did not prove a connection. This could have been a kitchen tally, an ingredient count, or a record from a different year altogether. The name might belong to a servant, a student, or someone who had never lived in the castle.

Mara read it again. Aveline. An old-fashioned name, unfamiliar in the way of something preserved rather than chosen. It was not hers. That should have been a relief. Instead, the word seemed to make the papers more real: someone had been here, had counted portions, had kept a key, and had been reduced to a name with no family attached.

She did not touch the bundle beneath it.

The ink was dark where it had been protected and faded at the fold. Whoever wrote the name had not placed it there as a message for Mara. It belonged to the record. That was the simplest explanation, and for now she held to it. The unsettling part was elsewhere: the castle had spoken Mara’s name, while this older name had surfaced beside the same count of six she had seen in the children’s drawing.

She set the slip on the table and examined the bundle. The green thread was knotted twice, with a narrow paper tag caught beneath it. The tag had once carried writing; only the tail of a capital letter remained, a dark stroke shaped like a hook. She could untie the bundle without cutting the thread. Inside might be a date, a surname, or nothing but further lists. She might disturb something she could not put back.

Mara sat on the stool by the table. Dust rose around her boots. The room held no movement, no whisper, no breathing. Her hands shook, so she pressed her palms flat against the table until they steadied.

The name on the slip did not tell her why the castle had called her Mara. It belonged to someone else, someone whose surname and fate were absent from the page. The only link was the count of six—and even that might be coincidence. She held to that uncertainty. A pattern was not an answer.

She lifted the bundle. The green thread was dry but strong. She could untie it without cutting. She could read the papers without damaging them. She could stop if they became personal, though she did not know what that would mean anymore.

As she reached for the knot, the paper tag shifted beneath the thread. A tiny scrap slipped free and landed on the table. It showed the lower half of a handwritten date, the year lost where the paper had torn. Mara set it beside the inventory slip. She did not open the bundle. Whatever it contained, she would examine it somewhere she could see the door.

Something heavy moved in the corridor outside. It dragged one step, paused, then dragged another. The rhythm was uneven, as if one leg were injured. Mara slipped the card into her pocket and gathered the bundle without untying it. She placed it inside her pack, on top of the cylinder, and shouldered the weight.

She moved to the door and looked out.

The corridor was empty. At its far end, the passage bent toward the court. A line of water shone on the floor, leading toward the kitchen entrance. The small bare prints were gone. In their place were three deep grooves, carved directly into the damp stone. They pointed toward the store-room.

Mara shut the door quietly.

She was not alone in the castle. That was hardly new. But something had been coming toward her, and then had stopped at the bell. She could not tell whether the bell had protected her or summoned whatever waited outside.

The store-room had no rear door. The shelves were too unstable to climb, and the opening beneath the fallen cabinet was no more than a black seam. She went back to the corridor, knife in hand, and moved toward the courtyard as silently as she could.

At the junction, she heard a faint scrape to her left, down the water-smelling branch. She froze. The sound came again, followed by a soft clatter, as if a metal object had rolled across stone.

She did not investigate.

The corridor to the court remained empty. At the threshold, she looked both ways. The kitchen doorway still stood open. The left passage, the one she had first avoided, was now closed by a wooden door she had not seen before. It filled the arch exactly, its surface blistered with age. A line had been painted across it in red, three circles connected by a stroke.

Mara reached the courtyard.

The hidden door behind her remained invisible. The night sky above had cleared. Through the roofless space she could see one bright star and the ragged profile of the castle wall. The service court had changed while she was below; or she had failed to notice what was there before. A narrow stone stair rose from the far end beside the collapsed archway, tucked behind a rain barrel. It climbed to a landing set into the castle’s outer face.

A way up.

She approached the stair carefully. The first step was cracked but held. The second had a dark smear on it. She crouched and studied the mark. It looked like soot, except its edges glistened. She touched it with the knife tip. The stain stretched like tar and drew back from the metal.

Mara wiped the blade on the wet stone.

The stair rose to a small platform beneath a narrow arch. From there, she could see an open corridor inside the castle. A torn tapestry hung along one wall, its threadbare design showing a green serpent winding around a silver tree. The sight gave her an abrupt, almost painful sense of recognition. Not because she had seen this exact tapestry, but because the symbols belonged to the private map she had carried in her head: Slytherin, Snape, hidden rooms, a world that had once seemed to promise another way of being.

The reality was mold, rain, and a dark stain creeping down the stair.

She climbed to the platform. The corridor beyond the arch was blocked in one direction by a fallen beam. The other way sloped gently inward. On the wall, someone had nailed a brass plaque. Its lettering was worn almost smooth, but the last line remained readable: **POTIONS—LOWER STAFF ACCESS**.

Mara’s heart began to beat faster.

The direction might lead toward Snape’s old spaces. It might only be a general service route. The map fragment in her pack had marked old stores sealed after breach. The cylinder bore an S. The workroom below had smelled of herbs. Each clue was still only a clue, but together they pulled her toward the centre of the castle.

She touched the cylinder through her pack. The faint vibration had stopped.

At the far end of the corridor, a door stood ajar. A strip of pale green light showed beneath it. The door had no sign, only a small round plate at chest height, stamped with the same interrupted circle she had seen on the iron disks in the tunnels. The three-groove mark had been carved into the stone beside it.

Mara raised the knife and stepped closer.

The corridor smelled of crushed leaves, old paper, and a trace of smoke. She heard no creature behind the door. No bell. No voice.

Then a quiet, familiar sound came from within: the soft scratch of a quill across parchment.

Mara stopped at the threshold.

The writing continued for several seconds, then ended with a small, decisive stroke. A page turned.

She thought of the name on the inventory slip. Aveline. She thought of the voice at the landing, greeting her as if she had returned. She thought of the warnings against names, and of the six portions recorded on the paper beside a name that had lost its surname and date.

She wanted to open the door. She wanted to know whether the papers inside bore Snape’s handwriting, whether the room connected to the workroom she had glimpsed and lost. She wanted the answers enough to feel the want as a physical pull.

But she had learned something in the lower rooms: a call could be a trap, and a name could be a way to make a person answer. She would not let curiosity make the decision for her.

Mara stepped away from the door and examined the hinges, the floor, and the wall around it. A line of tiny scratches ran beneath the threshold, as if something had been drawn in and out of the room many times. The plate was not a handle. Its interrupted circle matched the iron disks, but there was no slot.

She took the cylinder from her pack and held it near the plate without touching either to the door.

The metal warmed.

Inside, the quill began moving again.

Mara returned the cylinder to its pocket. The writing stopped at once.

The silence that followed was so complete she could hear her own pulse. She backed away another step. The door did not open. The plate did not glow. No voice called her name.

She had found a connection, not an answer.

The corridor’s far end disappeared around a corner. Mara chose to move along it, keeping the door in sight until the bend hid it. A few paces later, she found another plaque, its surface scratched. Only two words remained legible: **STORES—SEALED**. Beneath them, an arrow pointed down.

She took out the map fragment. The mark for old stores had been drawn beside a similar arrow. The route on the fragment could not be followed precisely, but the shape of the corridor matched the line she had memorized. She was above the underwall now, or close to it. The service court had delivered her into the castle’s lower levels, where the old routes crossed and doubled back.

A way forward. Not a way out.

She drew a small chalk arrow beside the plaque, pointing toward the route she had taken. The chalk line came away grey instead of white. The wall drank its colour, leaving only a faint groove.

Mara stared at the mark.

The castle had not moved. The chalk had not vanished. Still, the arrow she had drawn seemed to point down the passage in both directions, a short white stroke with heads at either end.

She rubbed it with her thumb. The line stayed.

“Not yet,” she whispered.

The words were no longer a question. They were a boundary she could set for herself. She would not open the door tonight, not until she understood more about the cylinder and the name on the card. She would follow the marked route to the old stores, but she would not answer if the castle called again.

A faint sound rose from below: a bell, distant and uncertain, followed by a child’s counting.

“One,” the voice said.

Mara faced the descending corridor.

The second bell had already sounded at the service court. Whatever counted below did not seem to know that, or did not care. She tightened the strap across her shoulder and started down, keeping her hand away from the serpent button.

At the first turn, she glanced back.

The passage behind her was empty. The green-lit door was gone from sight. On the wall where it had stood, a small brass label caught the light from nowhere.

She could not read it from this distance.

She did not go back to find out.

The stairs descended into a deeper, colder dark. The count below continued, slow and patient.

“Two.”

Mara kept walking.
