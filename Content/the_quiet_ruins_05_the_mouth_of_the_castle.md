## Chapter Five: The Mouth of the Castle

For a long time, Mara did nothing.

Her left hand held the serpent button. Her right hovered near the gap that showed daylight. The two choices had become absurdly simple: stone and air, or stone and dark. One promised no safety, only the chance to get out. The other promised nothing at all. She had crossed an ocean for the second promise.

The button warmed in her palm.

She closed her fingers around it. The metal had once burned her; now it was only warm, as if it had been carried near a body. She could feel the tiny ridges of its coiled serpent through her skin. At the button’s mouth, the black notch seemed to collect the dimness rather than reflect it.

“Not yet,” Mara whispered, trying the words for their weight.

The tunnel did not answer. Somewhere behind her, far away, came the soft clatter of a stone settling. That was all.

She put the button away and faced the gap. The opening was too small to pass through with the rucksack on. She could push the pack ahead, crawl into the rubble, and work her way toward daylight. If the stones shifted, she might be pinned. If they held, she would reach the terrace, where she could rest, tend her cuts, and decide what to do in the open air.

The thought of returning to the terrace did not feel like retreat. It felt like a sensible choice, the sort a person made when she had seen enough.

Mara took one step toward it.

Her boot met something soft.

She jerked back, raising the phone. A pale strip of cloth lay in the dust at the threshold. It had not been there when she first looked. She crouched without touching it. The cloth was grey, stiff with age, and marked by three dark circles pressed in a row. The pattern was familiar now. It had appeared on the cottage fabric, and the wounds on the hippogriff had been three lines, not circles, but the rhythm was the same: three marks, a space, then nothing.

A faint scratching sounded from the daylight gap.

Mara froze.

The sound came again, a slow drag against stone. The rubble at the opening shivered. A grain of grit rolled down and tapped her boot. Then the gap narrowed with a grinding shift. A slab slid across the opening, leaving only a seam of grey light. Dust breathed inward from the collapsing stones.

Mara backed away. The seam shrank another inch.

She could still try to crawl through, but the shifting rubble had made the choice for her: one wrong movement might seal her between the stones, or bring the whole shelf down on top of her. The route to daylight was no longer a route she could trust. She waited, listening for another scrape. Nothing followed. The silence did not make the opening safer.

It might have been the creature feeling its way through the collapsed passage. It might have been loose stone. It might have been one of the small things that moved in the walls, drawn by the same warmth as the button. Whatever the cause, retreat had become a gamble with a passage already closing around her.

Mara looked toward the deeper tunnel. She did not mistake it for safety. She chose it because it was the only direction still open.

The tunnel rose from the threshold in a shallow curve. Its walls were laid in large blocks, unlike the brick of the transfer route or the rough stone of the crawlspace. The joints had been filled with a dark material that looked like mortar but held the light in a dull sheen. At shoulder height, the serpent mark had been scratched into the nearest block, so faint she had mistaken it for a crack.

A second mark waited farther in.

She went toward it.

The air changed as she moved. The smell of damp stone gave way to a dry, bitter fragrance, like crushed leaves steeped too long. The scent reminded her of the sealed cylinder in her pack. She touched the pocket through the canvas. The cylinder was still there, cool and solid.

The tunnel bent right, then left. Mara counted each turn, making a map in her head: threshold, right, left, long slope. She marked the wall with a short chalk stroke every dozen paces. After the third mark, she discovered the first again, though she had not turned back. The chalk line stood ahead on the wall, clean and white. She checked behind her. Her first mark remained there too.

Two identical strokes.

She went on more slowly. The floor dipped, then rose. A narrow gutter ran down its centre, dry except for a few dark grains. Along its edge, someone had set small iron disks into the stone at regular intervals. The disks bore no visible writing, but when she passed the first, the serpent button gave a pulse of heat. The second disk was cold. The third made the phone screen flicker.

Mara stopped beside it. The phone’s battery indicator had fallen from forty-three percent to twenty-eight.

“Don’t do that,” she muttered.

She had no idea whether she meant the tunnel or herself.

The next disk was half hidden beneath a thin layer of dust. She brushed it clear with her sleeve. A symbol had been stamped into its face: a line crossing a circle, with a small break at the bottom. It looked like a diagram for an opening. She compared it to the serpent button’s mouth, then to the three circles in the cloth. Nothing matched exactly, but the same idea of interruption appeared in each mark—a line broken, a seam not quite closed.

At the end of the passage, a low arch opened into a chamber.

Mara waited before entering. No sound came from within. The arch stones had been blackened, though the room beyond looked dry. She leaned in and swept the phone beam from side to side.

The chamber had once been a receiving room. A row of hooks stood along one wall, each bent down under a burden no longer there. Beneath them sat three narrow benches, all cracked. On the far side, an iron grille divided the room from another passage. The bars were rusted, but the gap between them was wide enough for an arm.

A wooden sign hung above the grille. Its lettering had flaked, but she could read the surviving words: **LOWER ENTRY—…SERVANTS AND STORES**.

The missing letters might have said “reserved for” or “for servants and stores.” Either way, it had not been built for visitors. Mara had found the hidden underside of Hogwarts, a route used by people who brought food, supplies, or messages without walking through the front doors.

She crossed the chamber. The benches were dusted with a fine grey powder. Beneath one sat a small shoe, its leather collapsed, the brass buckle missing. Mara’s eyes went to the melted buckle she had left in the scorched passage. She felt the old urge to collect it, to make the scattered pieces into a story she could own. She forced herself to leave the shoe where it was.

At the grille, a rusted latch held the bars together. She tried it. It did not move. The metal was cold, and a smell of wet fur rose from the other side. Mara drew back.

Something breathed beyond the bars.

She turned off her phone light.

The chamber darkened completely. Mara listened. A faint wet clicking came from behind the grille, slow and intermittent. It was not the same as the tunnel creature’s sharp clicks, but it was close enough to make her grip the knife.

She waited until the sound stopped, then switched the light on. The passage beyond the bars was empty. At its far end, a narrow strip of greenish light lay across the floor.

The serpent button warmed.

Mara tested the latch again, keeping her body away from the bars. This time it lifted with a gritty squeal. She pulled the grille open by a few inches. It scraped across the floor, protesting. Nothing attacked. The smell of wet fur remained, along with a sour trace of old smoke.

She squeezed through and eased the grille shut behind her.

The passage beyond was wider, with a shallow ceiling and a line of hooks along both walls. Some still held fragments of string, rotten leather, or what might have been the remains of a small satchel. The walls had been painted once, pale yellow beneath the dirt. In places, someone had scrawled words over the paint. Most were too faded to read. One remained clear: **KEEP NAMES OUT OF THE LOWER ROOMS**.

Mara stopped. She thought of the supply list: Do not store names with the ingredients. Then of the voice behind the claw-marked door: Elspeth.

Was the warning practical? A precaution against a spell? A superstition born of a terrible accident? Whatever it meant, someone had written it twice, in two different places, and both times in a tone that suggested the danger was real.

She turned away from the words.

The greenish light ahead came and went, not like a lamp but like a pulse. Mara stopped and listened. Nothing moved in the passage. Still, the light had a rhythm to it: brightening, fading, then brightening again after the same measured pause.

She counted the intervals, then counted them once more. Whatever made the light was not approaching. It was fixed beyond the bend.

At the far end, the passage opened into a broad room with a low ceiling. Two columns supported the centre. The floor was stained dark near a drain. Along the walls stood cupboards with their doors hanging open. A row of hooks ran above them, and the dust beneath the hooks bore the long, narrow outlines of things once hung there.

Mara entered slowly. The room felt less like a lair than a workplace abandoned in haste. Chipped bowls were stacked in one cupboard; another held folded grey cloth and a glass jar sealed with a cork. She did not touch the jar. On the inside of the cupboard door, someone had drawn six small figures in pencil. Beside them stood a taller figure, its face rubbed away. Under the drawing, in a child’s uneven hand, were the words **WAIT FOR SECOND BELL**.

Mara photographed it. The image looked ordinary on the screen. That helped less than it should have.

She spent a minute examining the drawing for additions or hidden writing. There were none. The six children stood shoulder to shoulder, all facing the taller figure. The taller figure’s arms were raised—not threateningly, she thought, though it was hard to tell with its face erased. Perhaps it was counting them. Perhaps it was directing them somewhere.

Mara sat on the nearest bench, keeping the cupboard in view. She needed to stop treating every mark as a message aimed at her. That was how people got themselves killed in stories: they found a pattern and mistook it for an invitation. But the count of six was difficult to ignore. The evacuation list at the cottage had named six children, and Elspeth. This drawing might have been made by any child, at any time. The service rooms could have been used for decades. Yet the pencilled instruction about the second bell tied it to the same system of warnings she had followed beneath the wall.

She took out her notebook and copied the drawing as accurately as she could. The act steadied her. Six small bodies, a taller figure, a line beneath. She wrote the words next to it, then added the location and the time shown on her phone. Her hand shook on the first attempt. She tore out the page and started again.

On the back of the cupboard door, beneath the pencil figures, she found a faint impression in the wood. Someone had pressed a name there hard enough to leave a groove, then rubbed the letters away. Mara angled her phone light across the surface. The last two letters might have been **th**, or they might only be scratches. She did not try to reconstruct the name.

The warning above the hooks had been written in a firm hand: **KEEP NAMES OUT OF THE LOWER ROOMS**. The child’s drawing said to wait for the second bell. At the cottage, the evacuation list had been kept in a Civil Defence box, but the list itself was not a map. It recorded people; it did not explain where they had gone or why the lower path had failed.

Mara’s first instinct was to call the clues evidence. She corrected herself. They were traces. Evidence required a conclusion she could defend, and she had none. Still, the people who left these marks had been frightened enough to repeat themselves. Whatever they feared, they had expected someone else to come after them.

She wondered whether the bell had been a signal for the children to move, or a sound they were warned not to follow. The two instructions contradicted one another only if the bell meant the same thing in every place. Perhaps the first strike warned the people in the tunnels; the second released them. Perhaps the warnings had been written at different times by people who no longer trusted the same system. The war had turned even an evacuation into a sequence of guesses.

For a moment she imagined the six children waiting here. Their shoes on the floor, their small voices hushed because a grown-up had ordered silence. The taller figure might have been an elf, or a keeper, or one of the children grown older. She imagined the bell sounding above them. Then another sound, closer, from the passage. She could not tell whether anyone had come to lead them out.

She shut the cupboard gently. The latch clicked, a small ordinary sound that made her flinch. After a moment, she opened it again to confirm that the drawing remained where she had seen it. It did.

The words on the wall returned to her: **KEEP NAMES OUT OF THE LOWER ROOMS**. She began to understand the warnings as parts of one instruction, though she still had no idea what danger they described. The children had been told to wait for a bell. Someone had thought names could be dangerous here. The evacuation list at the cottage had recorded Elspeth and six children. She could not prove this drawing belonged to them, but the number made coincidence feel less comfortable.

A second thought troubled her more: the warnings seemed to have been left for whoever came after. The rubbed-out name on the cupboard. The words about answering a call. The repeated insistence that a name was not to be given. Perhaps the castle did not need to know who entered its lower rooms. Perhaps it learned by listening.

The far side of the chamber held a narrow doorway. A rusty chain hung from a hook beside it, cut cleanly through. Beyond it came a faint sound of water. Mara did not follow it. Instead, she looked for another exit and found a staircase climbing behind a half-open cupboard panel. A cool draught descended from above, carrying the smell of rain and leaves.

She chalked the left side of the panel before climbing through. The narrow steps were damp but stable. At the first landing, the green light from the chamber fell away. Her phone beam caught three parallel grooves carved across the wall, repeated at intervals. The marks were old, softened by touch or weather. They might have been a guide for hands in the dark, or a warning. She had seen the pattern too often to dismiss it, but not often enough to understand it.

She touched the serpent button through her pocket. It remained warm.

At the top of the stairs, the passage ended at a small door, no wider than her shoulders. A strip of yellowed paper had been pinned to it. The handwriting was a child’s, uneven but careful: **IF THE HOUSE CALLS YOU BACK, DO NOT SAY WHERE YOU ARE.** Beneath it, in a different hand, someone had added: **It can hear the answer.**

Mara read the words twice. She thought of the old warning about names, and of the voice that had said Elspeth behind the claw-marked door. She understood now that the danger might not be the name itself. It might be the answer that followed a call: a location, an admission, a confirmation that the person being called was still there.

A soft voice came from the other side of the door.

“Mara?”

She went cold.

No one here knew her name. She had told no one beneath the wall. The elf had never asked for it. The paper in the cottage had held lists of children and evacuees, but none of them could know her.

She had not spoken her mother’s name, either. She had not spoken of the garden in Connecticut, or of the rain that had once called her in from it. Yet the voice carried that childhood warmth with unnerving precision—as if it had found the memory by listening somewhere deeper than the tunnels.

The voice repeated her name, no louder than before.

“Mara.”

It sounded like her mother.

Not the voice her mother had now, older and brittle with years of disapproval. This was the voice from childhood, before arguments acquired history, before every conversation became a negotiation. It was gentle, warm, and concerned. The sound of someone calling her in from the yard because rain was coming.

Mara’s chest tightened. For a single treacherous instant she was eight years old again, standing at the edge of a Connecticut garden, sure that somewhere there had to be a door to a larger life.

She backed away.

The voice behind the door sighed. “You always leave.”

Mara kept her mouth shut. Her eyes stung, but she was not sure whether it was from dust or memory. The voice had chosen the words with care. It might have heard them in the years of her life, or it might simply have known what a lonely person could be made to hear.

A second voice spoke from inside the door. It sounded like a man, low and impatient.

“Open it.”

The words were not familiar. The tone was. Authority disguised as certainty, the promise that obedience would be simpler than doubt. Mara felt the old reflex rise in her: to prove she could do what she was told, to make herself indispensable, to earn the right to choose afterward.

She drew the serpent button from her pocket.

The metal was cold. She placed it in the centre of her palm, where the tiny mouth faced upward. The voices stopped.

In the silence, something tapped from inside the door: three times, pause, three times.

The same pattern as above the tunnel junction.

Mara put the button away. She turned and walked on.

She did not run until the green light came into view. By then, her legs had begun to shake. At the bend, the light dimmed and held steady. The passage beyond was empty. Mara waited, listening for the clink she had heard before. It did not come again.

She left the green-lit bend behind and continued toward the stairs that rose toward the castle. The first steps were broad and shallow, carved from dark stone. Moss grew in the seams. The air coming down was cooler and carried no smell of wet fur or stagnant water. She listened for the creature that had followed her through the crawlspace. Nothing clicked. Nothing dragged.

She started up.

The staircase spiralled around a central shaft. Halfway up, she found a landing with a small window set at head height. The glass was clouded and cracked, but a narrow pane remained clear. Through it she saw a slice of night sky.

Mara stopped. She had thought there was still daylight outside. The tunnel had swallowed more time than she realized. Through the pane, clouds moved across a sky the colour of bruised steel. A thin sliver of moon showed between them.

Below the window, someone had scratched a message into the stone: **THE FIRST DOOR IS FOR THE LIVING.**

The words brought no comfort. She looked at the stair above and the dark shaft below. The first door might mean the cottage, the underwall, or the threshold itself. It might refer to a route made for living people, as opposed to the voices and things that remained. She had no way to know.

A sound came from the upper landing: a faint, slow breathing.

Mara killed the phone light. The stairwell turned black. She listened, holding the knife low beside her leg. The breathing continued for several seconds, then shifted upward. Something was climbing away from her, one step at a time.

She waited until the sound vanished.

When she turned the light back on, a small dark stain marked the stair above. It was wet and had a reddish glimmer in the beam. Mara touched it with the tip of the knife. The blade came away clean.

She climbed around it.

At the top of the stairs stood a wooden door. It had no handle, only a round iron plate with a narrow slot in the centre. The same three circles had been punched into the wood, one above another. Mara looked at the serpent button. Its mouth fitted the slot in shape, though she did not know whether it would fit in size.

She did not try it.

A faded sign hung from a single nail: **SERVICE COURT**. Beneath the words, someone had scratched a small arrow pointing down, back toward the underwall. The sign was old enough to have been here before the war, but the arrow was fresh, bright where the wood had been cut.

Mara placed her ear against the door. Beyond it, wind moved through a broad open space. She heard the loose clatter of something striking a flagstone and, far away, a bird calling once.

Then came the bell.

One low strike rolled through the stairwell. The iron plate beneath her palm vibrated. Dust drifted from the lintel.

Mara stepped away from the door. The warning at the hatch returned to her: if you hear the bell, do not open. She had heard one bell before, then another, and the lower route had followed. Here there was no visible second sound, only the first strike fading through stone.

She waited.

The bell did not ring again.

Mara checked the cylinder in her pack and the cut strap she had tied in a knot. She checked the knife, the phone, the chalk. She had lost time, blood, and the easy certainty that each passage would remain where she had left it. She had not lost the map fragment or the button. She had not opened the cylinder.

The door might lead to the service court, a way into the castle. It might lead into another sealed chamber. The bell could have been a warning, a call, or an echo of the evacuation that had never finished.

Mara put her hand on the iron plate but did not turn it. The metal was cold beneath her fingers. She could not tell whether the bell had been a warning or a signal, only that someone had taught frightened people to wait for a second strike before moving. She let go of the plate.

She had come to Hogwarts believing that the dead had left power behind like abandoned tools. She had imagined she would find a wand, a potion, a book, some object that could make the world answer to her. Instead, every clue had been a threshold, every threshold a question, and each answer had made the tunnels more interested in her.

She could still turn around. She could descend, retrace the route as best she could, and try to find a way out before night deepened. She could leave the key where it lay and the cylinder sealed. She could tell herself the workroom had been a trick of light.

But the voice behind the door had known her name. It had found the sound of her mother in a memory she had kept to herself, and the castle had answered her presence with signs before she had ever reached this landing. Something under the clear water had touched her. The room with the serpent had been real, and so had Snape’s handwriting. She could not pretend the castle held nothing worth finding.

Mara drew a slow breath and rested her forehead against the door. She was afraid, and she knew the bell and the warnings might be a trap. No one had forced her hand to the plate. No voice had given her permission. She chose to go inward because she wanted what the castle might hold—and because turning back now would mean admitting that the power she had crossed an ocean to find might already have noticed her.

“Not yet,” she said—not to the voice, not to the tunnel, but to the fear that wanted to make the decision for her.

She turned the iron plate.

It resisted, then gave a little with a sound like a lock waking after a long sleep. The three circles in the wood darkened. A line of green light appeared beneath the door, thin as a blade. From the other side came a smell of rain, ashes, and something bitterly herbal.

The bell struck a second time.

The door opened inward by the width of a finger.

Mara did not push it wider. She leaned close to the gap and listened. The wind beyond had stopped. In the silence, a whisper moved across the threshold, too soft to make out.

Then it spoke clearly, with the quiet familiarity of someone greeting a person expected home.

“Welcome back, Mara.”

Mara’s hand left the plate.

She had never been here before.

The green line beneath the door went dark. The wood swung shut, not slamming, but closing with the slow certainty of a mouth.

Mara stood alone on the landing. The phone light trembled in her hand. Below, the unseen child had stopped counting. Somewhere in the stone, the bell’s vibration continued to fade.

She had not entered the castle. Not yet.

But the castle had opened its mouth, and said her name.
