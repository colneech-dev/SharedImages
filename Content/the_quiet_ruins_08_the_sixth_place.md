## Chapter Eight: The Sixth Place

The service court had one door she had not tried.

Mara found it behind a leaning rack of iron hooks, on the wall opposite the entrance she had used. Its narrow lintel was almost concealed by a hanging length of canvas, stiff with dust and damp. She pushed the rack aside. One of its feet scraped across the floor, and the sound ran down the passage behind her, growing thin and distant.

She stopped to listen.

No answering scrape. No bell.

The door bore no plaque. Only a shallow mark had been cut into the wood: an interrupted circle above three grooves. Beneath it, someone had scratched a small letter A.

Mara took out the inventory slip. The Aveline entry had been written on a torn edge; the ink was brown, the A narrow and upright. The letter on the door was cut into wood, not written, yet its crossbar slanted at the same severe angle as the ink. A coincidence, she told herself. A letter was only a letter.

Still, she looked at it for a long moment.

The drawing of six children had been pinned to the far wall. The back carried its warning: **If the sixth place is empty, do not call the name.** Aveline had said the room was too small. Six cups, six stools, six blankets. Six measures held for E. The evidence was accumulating around a person she had never met, and Mara had begun to feel that the castle would not let her walk away until she understood why.

That feeling could be the place working on her. It could also be her own need to turn every fragment into a path.

She tucked the slip away and tested the door.

It opened inward, resisting at first, then giving with a soft, reluctant sigh. Beyond was a short passage, sloping gently north. The stone floor was dry. A string of brass hooks ran along one wall at child’s height. Each held a small square of faded cloth, except the last. That hook was empty.

The sixth place.

Mara stepped inside. The air smelled of old wool and candle smoke. Her torch picked out a narrow archway at the far end and, beyond it, a room with a low ceiling. She noticed a line on the floor where dust had been disturbed—not a recent trail, but a shallow groove worn by repeated traffic. It led from the door to the room, then turned right, toward a second opening.

She marked the turns in her notebook. Service court, unmarked door, north-sloping passage, low room, right-hand opening. She drew an arrow back the way she had come and wrote **stair to stores** beside it. That stair ended at the iron-braced chamber. The shaft and basin lay directly beneath that room; this passage was separate, running parallel to it. She had not discovered a route out of the lower stores so much as a different corridor in the same buried section of the castle.

The map fragment did not include it.

Mara entered the low room.

Six stools stood around a table. Five had been pushed in. The sixth lay on its side beneath the table, one leg broken clean through. Six cups rested on a shelf, all green glass, all empty. Six folded blankets had been stacked in a wooden chest. A dark stain marked the lid.

She did not touch it.

The room was barely large enough for the table and stools. A child would have had to turn sideways to pass between them. The note had been right: it was too small.

On the table lay a slate and a short piece of chalk. There were no words on the slate, only six parallel lines. The last had been crossed out. Beside them was a smaller line, separated from the others by a wide gap.

Mara leaned close. The chalk had softened with age, but the line was fresh enough to leave dust on the table. Someone had marked it after the rest.

A quiet tick sounded in the passage behind her.

She turned. The doorway was empty.

Mara backed toward the table, keeping it between herself and the open arch. She lifted the torch. The flame’s electric glare threw her own shadow across the wall, long and thin. Beyond it, another shadow lay low beside the floor—too narrow to be a person, too still to be smoke.

She waited. The shadow did not move.

The elf’s warning returned to her: black, not below. The black was not simply the pool, then. Or the words referred to what the pool had carried. Mara thought of the wet, pale shape climbing from the basin and the way it had reached for her ankle. It had not lunged at the light or the noise. It had reached for the warmest, most vulnerable part of her as she climbed away.

A thin black line crept beneath the far door.

Mara backed away from it.

The line thickened, spreading against the floor without reflecting the torch. It flowed uphill, against the slight slope of the corridor, and divided around the table legs. The black did not behave like water. It moved as though it could feel the room’s shape.

The empty hook by the entrance gave a small metallic tremor.

Mara looked at it. A scrap of grey cloth lay looped through the hook, so faded she had not seen it at first. Three grooves had been stitched into one corner. The same pattern as the cloth around the serpent button.

She took one step toward it.

The black line advanced another inch.

Mara’s first impulse was to run. Her second was to understand what the mark did. The serpent button had warmed at the basin, and the sealed doors had answered its presence. If it could open something, if it could make the castle respond—then it was more than a clue. It might be the first thing she had found that could give her leverage.

The thought came easily. Too easily.

She unwrapped the button. The brass was cool against her palm. Its serpent was worn almost smooth, its head tucked against its coil. She held it toward the empty hook.

The black stopped.

For a breath, the room became still. The stain at the door flattened. The button warmed in her hand, then gave a single pulse of green light. A line appeared in the wall behind the hook—an outline of a small hatch, concealed beneath layers of paint and dust.

Mara could see the choice in front of her. Put the button away and retreat. Or use it while the black was held still, risk whatever waited behind the hatch, and learn what the castle had hidden from her.

She thought of the open doors she had been unable to pass, the collapsed workroom, the sealed cylinder with its silver S. She thought of how often she had arrived at the edge of knowledge and turned back because she lacked the means to force it open.

She had not crossed an ocean to remain powerless.

Mara pressed the serpent button to the outline.

The hatch clicked.

The black surged forward.

She threw herself sideways. The stain struck the table leg and climbed it in a glossy ribbon. The wood hissed. Its varnish blistered and peeled, leaving a grey, pitted track behind. The black recoiled from the button’s green flare, then flowed around the table toward her boots.

Mara yanked the hatch open.

A small alcove lay behind it. Inside was a brass bell with a cracked handle, wrapped in a child’s blanket. A tin tag had been tied to the clapper. The letters on it were nearly erased, but the name could still be read: **AVELINE**.

Mara reached for the bell.

The black touched the broken stool. Its remaining legs blackened and collapsed. The table lurched. The six green cups rattled, and one fell to the floor. It shattered. A shard struck Mara’s cheek, opening a thin cut beneath her eye.

The black line recoiled from the sound, drawing back toward the doorway.

Mara closed her fingers around the bell and pulled it free. The tag was cold. The clapper hung crooked, caught against the cracked handle. She could not tell whether the bell had been used to summon the children, warn them, or mark the second dose. But Aveline’s name belonged to it. It was the first object that tied her to a specific person, not merely a list or a room.

She wanted it badly enough that she had opened the hatch while the black was in the room.

The bell was not a weapon. It might not be magical at all. Still, she had risked herself for it, and the room had paid for her choice. The table was damaged; the stool was ruined; one cup lay in pieces. The black stain had left a smoking, colourless scar on the wood.

Mara wrapped the bell in the child’s blanket. As she did, the serpent button’s warmth faded.

The dark line withdrew under the door. It left behind a thin residue that clung to the stone like soot. The room smelled sharply of wet iron and burnt wool.

Mara stood very still, waiting for the pool creature—or whatever had followed the black—to come through.

Nothing crossed the threshold.

A shape crouched in the far archway. The house-elf’s long fingers were wrapped in a strip of grey cloth; beneath it, the skin at one wrist looked raw and darkened, as if the black had burned it before. It watched the residue, not Mara.

“Clear,” Mara whispered.

The elf shook its head. “Not word. Way.” It pressed its bandaged wrist to the stone. Its face tightened, but it held there until the soot-thin stain stopped creeping. Then it pulled away, trembling. “She chose. Aveline chose. Not bell. Not call.”

“Who did she choose?”

The elf looked at the wrapped bell in Mara’s hand. “You take. You ask later.” It backed into the archway, then raised one finger toward the passage floor, tracing an invisible route that curved away from the black-marked door. “Clear keeps feet. Black keeps names.”

Before Mara could ask what that meant, the elf vanished. A wet, shallow breath lingered in the room, followed by silence.

The hook rattled once more. The scrap of grey cloth slipped free and dropped to the floor. On its reverse, nearly hidden by rot, someone had written a single word in faded ink: **CLEAR**.

Mara looked from the cloth to the broken circle carved above the passage. The elf’s warning had not described two different waters. It had named a boundary: black belonged to the thing in the pool; clear was the path that could be used without drawing it. The black could leave its basin, but it was slowed or stopped by the button’s brief light. She had seen the reaction. She had no idea whether it was a defence, a lure, or a temporary accident.

She would not test it again.

A soft sound came from the far passage: three taps, then two. A pause. One tap.

Mara’s fingers tightened around the wrapped bell. The pattern could have been a knock, an old signal, or the building settling. She had heard enough strange sounds here to know that naming them did not make them safer.

She extinguished the torch for a moment. Darkness filled the room. In it, a thin green spark remained behind the hatch, where she had pressed the button. Then even that went out.

When she relit the torch, the black residue had stopped moving. The line under the door was gone. Mara could either return through the same corridor to the service court or follow the passage beyond the low room. She chose the return route. She had what she came for, though she still did not know why Aveline’s name mattered.

At the threshold, she looked back at the table and the shattered cup. The room had been made to hold six places. Now one place was broken, and she had made the damage.

She did not apologize to the empty stools.

The passage back seemed longer. At its far end, the unmarked door waited, its shallow letter A catching the torchlight. Mara checked the map in her notebook and retraced the route exactly: low room, north-sloping corridor, door into the service court. The stair to the iron-braced stores branched elsewhere; she would not confuse the two again.

Before she reached the door, she heard a faint click behind her. She kept walking.

The service court was empty. The drawing of six figures still hung on the wall, its faceless sixth turned toward the bell in Mara’s pack—or so it seemed from the angle of the paper. She placed the wrapped bell beside the drawing without untying it.

A line of writing had appeared on the exposed slate beneath the paper. Mara was certain it had been blank when she last looked.

**Aveline was not the sixth.**

She read the words once, then again. The chalk letters were small and careful. No hand had been near the slate while she was in the passage. Yet there they were.

Mara took out the inventory slip and compared the writing. The A was different. This hand leaned forward, impatient, and the final stroke of the e ran long. Whoever had written the note had not used the same hand as the inventory keeper.

A name could be a clue. It could also be bait.

She wrapped the bell more tightly and stowed it in her pack. The silver S on the sealed cylinder caught her eye as the flap fell closed. For the first time since entering Hogwarts, the Snape object seemed almost irrelevant. She had come seeking his power, but it was Aveline who had made her risk blood and let the black into the room.

Mara touched the cut beneath her eye. Her fingertips came away red.

The castle had given her a name and a warning, and she had answered with a theft.

At the far end of the service court, behind the door marked SERVICE COURT, something scraped slowly across stone. Mara did not approach it. She opened her notebook and wrote down the sentence from the slate, then drew a small diagram of the room: six stools, six cups, the hatch behind the empty hook, the route back to the court. Beside the damaged table she marked a dark patch and wrote **black reached this room; reacts to button-light; burns wood**.

The note made the event look orderly. It did not make her choice feel any better.

She closed the notebook.

From the wall behind the drawing came a quiet, dry voice—not the mother-voice, not the child’s counting.

“Not the sixth.”

Mara turned toward the paper. The six small figures remained as they were. Their faces were simple strokes, except for the faceless one. Beneath them, the bell-shaped mark had acquired a faint green stain.

She put one hand over the serpent button and the other over the bell in her pack. One object opened a hidden hatch. The other carried Aveline’s name. Neither had given her an answer.

But both were hers now.

She left the service court by the upper passage, moving carefully so the injured ankle would bear her weight. Behind her, the drawing rustled though the air was still. She did not look back until she reached the corridor’s bend.

The paper had fallen from the wall.

On the stone behind it, the six-place mark had been scratched into the plaster. The sixth groove was fresh, bright against the old dust.

Mara continued upward, carrying the bell, the button, and the knowledge that the black could follow her beyond its pool. The desire for power had not diminished. If anything, the cost had made the thing she had taken feel more valuable.
