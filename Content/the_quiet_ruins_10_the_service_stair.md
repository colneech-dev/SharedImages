## Chapter Ten: The Service Stair

The service door shut behind Mara with the small, final sound of a book closing.

For several seconds she kept her hand on the iron key, though it had already turned back in the lock. The passage beyond was steep and narrow, built for servants carrying baskets rather than people with packs. A shallow groove ran down the middle of each stone step. The walls pressed close enough that her shoulders brushed them if she forgot to turn sideways.

She listened for pursuit. The voices from the records room had gone quiet. In the stillness, she could hear the uneven rasp of her own breath and the soft tick of the bell against the sealed cylinder in her pack.

She froze. The bell was wrapped in cloth; the clapper was bound. That little tap had come from her movement, not from any hand or magic. She wrote it down anyway, then added Snape’s warning beneath it: If the bell is heard, do not answer it.

The words had sounded like a rule when she first read them. Now she wondered whether they were a threat disguised as one. If the bell could summon something, then carrying it through these passages was already an invitation. She should have left it behind.

She tightened the pack strap instead. A bell that could summon something was not merely dangerous. It was useful. And if Snape had spent his last days studying it, Mara would not turn away just because he had told her to be careful.

She wrote one more line: Find Snape’s work. Learn what he knew. Do not let the warning make the choice for you.

Even as she wrote it, she knew that was not what Snape had meant.

The stair descended in a square spiral. At each turn, a small slit had been cut into the wall, narrow as a letterbox. Through the first she saw only black stone. Through the second, a pale thread of light. Through the third, a glimpse of a room with a wooden bench and a row of hooks. A coat hung from one hook, its sleeves brushing the floor. It was too far away to see whether it was cloth or shadow.

She continued down.

On the next landing, she found a shallow niche containing a tin cup, a dry heel of bread and a chipped saucer. The bread crumbled when she touched it. A small grey handprint marked the wall above the saucer. Four fingers and a thumb, each too long. The stain had been scrubbed at until the stone around it was lighter.

Mara leaned close. The handprint was old. Beneath it, almost hidden by the abrasion, someone had scratched three grooves into the stone. The marks were parallel, their ends interrupted by a short crossline.

She did not touch them.

At the next turn, a smell rose from below: wet wool, cold ashes, and the sharp medicinal bitterness of wormwood. The scent pulled her back to the stores, to the six measures of draught and the warning about the second bell dose. She took the sealed cylinder out of her pack. Its silver S glimmered where the torch caught it. The scratched warning remained legible beneath the wax: If the bell is heard, do not answer it.

The metal seemed colder than the air.

She put it away. She would not open it in a stairwell where there was no room to retreat.

The steps ended at a landing with a low door. It was painted dark green, though the paint had bubbled and peeled. A label had been fixed to the centre with two brass tacks. Only SERVICE remained readable. The key from the twisted elf fitted the lock.

Mara held the key still.

A sound came through the door: the slow scrape of something being dragged over stone.

It stopped.

She waited. Her ankle throbbed. Her cheek, cut by the glass in the old stores, had stiffened beneath its dried blood. The loose pack strap pressed against her collarbone. She could go back up, but the way behind her led to the records room and the voices. She could stay on the landing until thirst or pain made the decision for her.

The dragged sound began again, this time moving away.

Mara turned the key.

The door opened inward onto a cramped room with a vaulted ceiling. Iron hooks lined one wall. A narrow bench stood beneath them, its surface worn smooth in the middle. On the floor lay a scatter of grey dust and one small object: a wooden spool wound with green thread.

The thread was damp. It ran from the spool beneath the opposite door.

Mara did not cross the room immediately. She swept her torch over the corners. A bucket stood beneath a cracked pipe. The water dripping into it was clear. A row of shallow cupboards had been built into the far wall. Their doors hung open, empty except for rusted hinges and a single folded scrap of paper.

The paper bore a list of names, each written in a different hand. Most were too faded to read. At the bottom, in darker ink, someone had added: **ELSPETH — TAKE THE CHILDREN THROUGH THE LOWER WAY. WAIT FOR THE SECOND BELL.**

Below that: **AV. MUST NOT BE COUNTED.**

Mara’s throat tightened. Aveline. Elspeth. The six places. The empty row. The words were not proof of one story, but they leaned toward one another as if the castle had set them out to be mistaken for a map.

She folded the scrap and put it in her notebook.

The green thread under the opposite door was taut. Something on the other side had pulled it straight. Mara took the spool and felt a faint tug against her fingers.

She let go.

The spool rolled toward the door.

It stopped at the threshold. The thread slackened, then tightened again. Once. Twice. A patient invitation, or a mechanism testing for weight.

Mara backed away. The bell gave a small, muffled knock inside her pack.

From beyond the door came a whisper: “Not the bell.”

It was one of the voices she had heard in the walls. Adult, low, and frayed at the edges. The words seemed to come from farther away than the door, as if the room had gathered them from a corridor beyond.

Mara kept her hands still.

“Not the bell,” it repeated.

She pulled the pack around to her front and checked the blanket wrapping the brass bell. The clapper remained caught by a twist of cloth. She had not knowingly moved it. The bell’s rim was cool against her fingertips.

“Who’s there?” she asked.

The thread snapped.

The spool shot beneath the door and struck the other side with a wooden click. Something scraped across the floor beyond it. Then came a soft, quick sound—like a child running barefoot down a hall.

Mara stepped back toward the service door. It had swung almost closed. She pushed it wider with her boot and kept the key ready.

The opposite door opened by itself.

A narrow corridor stretched beyond, lined with pale green tiles. The thread lay in a heap just inside. At its far end, a weak yellow light moved from side to side. There was no person carrying it. The glow shifted, paused, then slid out of sight around a bend.

Mara thought of the white marks in the other passage, the clear way that had begun to fade. She could retreat to the stair and try another route. Or she could follow the moving light.

She checked the corridor floor. Dust lay undisturbed except for a small track of wet impressions. They were not footprints. Each mark had three narrow ridges, as though something with a ribbed underside had dragged itself along the tiles.

The marks led toward the bend.

Mara shut the service-room door behind her, leaving the thread and spool where they were. The corridor smelled of the same bitter herbs. As she walked, the yellow light appeared again, far ahead, swaying in place.

The green tiles had been installed in panels, each panel divided by a thin black line. Some bore old scratches. Others had been covered with chalk marks: circles broken at the bottom, three short grooves, and arrows pointing in opposite directions. At one junction, the arrows all pointed toward a blank wall.

Mara stopped before it.

The wall was warmer than the corridor. She pressed her palm against it and felt, beneath the stone, a faint tremor: three beats, a pause, then one. The same pattern she had felt at the handleless door.

She pulled her hand away.

A square of plaster above the skirting had been scraped clean. On it, in a child’s small writing, was one word: **AV.**

Mara took out the brass serpent button. It rested cold in her hand. She held it near the wall, but the metal did not warm, and no green light appeared. She put it away. The castle had not granted her a universal key, whatever she had hoped when she first found the button.

The yellow light moved around the bend.

Mara followed.

Beyond it, the corridor opened into a low, broad passage. A row of old service doors stood along the left wall, each painted a different colour. Most had been forced open. Their rooms contained collapsed shelves, broken crockery and the blackened remains of small fires. One had been made into a shelter. A blanket still hung from a pipe, stiff with dust. Beneath it were two mugs, one large and one small, set neatly side by side.

The second mug had a name carved into its handle: AVELINE.

Mara’s fingers hovered over the letters. She could not tell whether the mug had belonged to a child, an adult, or someone who had borrowed the name. The carving was shallow and irregular. A small chip had removed part of the final E.

She left it where it was.

A sound came from the far end of the passage. Not a voice this time. A single bell note, thin and distant.

Mara stopped breathing.

The note travelled through the air plainly enough; she heard its high edge and the faint rattle that followed it. The bell in her pack answered with a hum against her spine. The sound and the response were separate: one came from ahead, the other from the brass she carried.

Snape’s warning returned with cruel simplicity: If the bell is heard, do not answer it.

She put her back against the wall and held still. The hum faded. At the far end, the yellow light paused.

A second note rang. The carried bell answered. This time the chalk arrows on the tiles trembled, and dust sifted from the ceiling. The light moved sharply toward the bend. Behind Mara, the passage gave a low stone groan.

She should retreat. Instead she leaned forward, listening for a third note. If the sound could wake the bell, then the bell might reveal what Snape had been trying to reach—or keep away. Mara wanted that knowledge badly enough to mistake danger for an opportunity.

“Stop,” she whispered.

Her own word travelled down the passage and returned softened, in a voice that sounded like hers: “Stop.”

Then another voice, adult and male, said, “You’re late.”

The phrase was familiar from the cottage. There, she had understood it as an echo, a scrap of sound caught in a place and released by her arrival. Here, it carried no warmth or welcome. The speaker sounded exhausted and angry.

Mara shut her eyes for one second, then opened them. Nothing had appeared in the passage. The light at the far end had vanished.

She turned to retreat.

The small door through which she had entered stood open, but the corridor beyond it had changed. Where the tiled passage should have continued, a wall of pale stone filled the doorway. The service room was gone.

Mara took one step toward it. The wall remained.

She touched its surface. Cold. Solid. No vibration.

The bell hummed again in her pack.

She spun around. At the end of the broad passage, the yellow light had returned. It now illuminated a narrow archway that she had not seen before. Beside it, on a brass plate, were the words **LOWER WAY**.

Mara looked at the blocked door, then at the arch. The castle was offering a route. The castle had also spent the last several hours ensuring that she understood the cost of accepting an invitation.

She opened her notebook and wrote: Bell rings below; carried bell responds. Do not answer. Passage behind changed. Lower Way marked.

She underlined the warning twice.

Then she approached the arch, not the light. She kept to the wall and searched the floor for signs of the black substance. There were none. The tiles were dry, though a faint residue glimmered in the grout like oil. She stepped over it.

Beyond the arch lay a circular room with a domed ceiling. Its centre was occupied by a stone table, its surface divided into six shallow depressions. Five had been filled with ash. The sixth contained a brass ring, darkened almost to black.

Mara stopped at the threshold.

On the wall behind the table, a strip of faded paper had been pinned beneath a knife. The paper curled in the damp. She could make out only a few lines:

**…when the first bell fails…**

**…do not send the sixth…**

**…Aveline has chosen…**

The rest had been eaten away.

Mara read the last line again. Aveline had chosen. Not been chosen, not counted, not sent. The phrasing made her pause. The six hollows and the warning not to send the sixth suggested a ritual, but this line suggested a refusal—or a decision made on her own terms.

She thought of the empty row in the ledger, the name pressed into the leaf beneath, and the house-elf’s warning: names go with it. Black follows names. She had traded the ledger away, perhaps the only record that could have told her who Aveline was. The loss sat under her ribs like a stone.

The brass ring was the only thing in the room that might tell her more. Mara knew she should leave it where it lay. Instead, she stepped inside, drawn by the possibility that Snape had left a way to understand the bell here.

A small movement disturbed the ash in one of the depressions. It lifted in a thin grey curl and settled again. The brass ring in the sixth hollow gave a faint click.

Mara stayed at the threshold and listened. No bell note followed the click; no answering hum came from her pack. The room remained still.

A yellow light appeared above the stone table. It had no source. For a moment, a girl stood on the far side, hair tied back, one hand extended toward the ring. The girl’s mouth moved, but Mara heard nothing. Then the figure vanished.

Mara’s torch had not flickered. She could not tell whether she had seen a ghost or a trick of light. The phrase on the paper returned to her: Aveline has chosen. The girl had reached for the ring. It might be a clue, or a warning. Either way, Mara wanted to know what Snape had learned about this room.

A voice came from behind her. “Not that one.”

The whisper was close, the same one that had spoken in the records passage. Mara did not turn. She did not answer.

The light went out. A sharp metallic note rang from the table. The ring rolled to the sixth hollow, tipped upright, and spun. At the note, the bell in Mara’s pack hummed against her spine. Dust shivered from the table’s edge.

She should have backed away. Instead, she stepped closer, trying to hear whether the ring would sound again. She wanted the bell to show her what Snape knew. When her pack slipped from her shoulder, its buckle struck the stone with a loud clack.

The sound seemed to wake the room.

The ring spun faster, whining against the stone. The bell answered with a hard vibration that knocked against the sealed cylinder. The five ash-filled depressions darkened. A pressure pushed at Mara’s ears, and dust streamed toward the arch as if something on the other side had drawn breath.

Mara seized the pack and stumbled backward. The moment she crossed the threshold, the pressure released. The ring fell flat. The arch narrowed behind her as a stone slab slid across it, leaving only a hand-wide gap.

She had wanted a sign from Snape. Instead, she had made enough noise to call something nearer.

The archway narrowed behind her. A stone slab slid across it, leaving a gap no wider than her hand. It stopped before closing completely.

She stared at the remaining crack. The castle had shut the room away, or kept it available. She could not tell which.

The corridor beyond the arch led down a shallow slope. The air grew colder. The smell of wormwood faded, replaced by wet stone and the sour odour of old smoke. A set of three iron pipes ran along the ceiling. One had split, but no water dripped from it. A faint murmur travelled through the pipework, rising and falling like conversation heard from another room.

Mara paused beneath the pipes.

“—leave the name—”

“—not the child—”

“—she has the bell—”

She moved on before the voices could resolve into words.

The slope ended at a small landing. A narrow iron stair descended into darkness. Its railing had been bent outward, as if something heavy had struck it from below. A smear of black marked the wall beside the first step. It was not a pool, only a thin crescent, but the stone beneath it had softened and slumped.

Mara took the serpent button out. Its brass remained cold. She did not bring it close to the stain. The button had made the black recoil once; it had also helped her open the hatch that let it into the room. She no longer trusted the distinction between protection and permission.

She packed it away.

From beneath the stair came a faint scrape.

Mara listened. The sound repeated, slower this time, followed by a low ripple of water. She remembered the pool, its surface reflecting the wrong light, and the water that cleared when the bell hummed. Her hand tightened around the pack strap.

The stairs did not look safe. The route behind her had already altered. The service room was gone, the circular chamber sealed. She could remain on the landing until whatever moved below chose to come up.

Mara descended.

Each step groaned under her weight. The iron was cold through her boot soles. Halfway down, she noticed a mark scratched into the railing: three grooves, then a small, interrupted circle. Beneath it, a line of writing had been scratched with a nail or a knife.

**KEEP THE WAY CLEAR.**

The words were old, but the cut edges looked clean.

At the bottom, a stone passage ran beneath a low arch. A trickle of water crossed the floor and disappeared into a drain. The water was clear. Mara crouched and touched it with one finger. It was icy, and it tasted faintly of iron when she lifted the finger to her lips.

She heard the bell again.

This time the note came from behind her, above the stair.

The wrapped bell at her back answered with a single hum.

Mara straightened too quickly and pain flashed through her ankle. The water in the drain darkened. The change began at the edges, curling inward like ink dropped into a glass. She stepped away. The blackness followed the trickle toward her boot, climbing against the slope of the stone.

She backed toward the arch. The serpent button pressed against her ribs through her coat. She could take it out and risk another green flare. She could try the bell, though Snape’s warning made that feel like stepping into a mouth. She could run.

Mara ran.

The ankle buckled on the first stride. She caught the arch with one hand, pushed through, and half-fell into the passage beyond. Water slapped against stone behind her. The black did not splash. It flowed silently, tightening into a narrow line along the floor.

At the end of the passage, a door stood open. Beyond it she saw a chamber lit by a dull red glow. She reached it and crossed the threshold. The door swung shut behind her with enough force to shake dust from the lintel.

The black line stopped on the other side.

Mara leaned against the door, gasping. She pressed the wrapped bell to her chest and waited for a knock, a hum, a voice. Nothing came. The room was small and bare except for a stone basin set into one wall and a low stool overturned beneath it. A narrow window looked into solid rock.

On the basin’s rim rested a brass nameplate. Its surface was green with age. She wiped it with her sleeve.

**A. VANE — NIGHT SERVICE**

Mara stared at it. The name was not Hallowell. It was not Aveline. It was another fragment, another name set before her in a place built for keeping records. Beneath it, a second line had been scratched away until only three grooves remained.

Something knocked from inside the basin.

Mara stepped back.

The basin was dry. Its drain had been stopped with a wad of cloth. She could see nothing inside but dust and a small, pale object at the bottom. It looked like a button. It might have been a tooth.

A second knock came from within the stone.

Then a third.

Three beats, a pause, then one.

Mara turned away from the basin. On the opposite wall, someone had written in charcoal: **SIX IS A ROOM, NOT A CHILD.**

The handwriting was hurried and slanted. A line beneath it had been rubbed away, leaving only the word **AV**.

Mara touched none of it. She opened her notebook and copied the sentence. The charcoal on the wall looked fresh enough to stain her fingertips, but she kept her hands to herself.

A muted sound travelled through the door behind her. Not a bell. A soft, dry scrape, like a fingernail moving across wood.

Mara looked down at the brass nameplate. A. Vane. Night service. She thought of the carving M. Hallow, of names that appeared in the wrong places, of the Hallowell entry she had left on the records-room floor. She had wanted proof that she belonged to this story. Now she wondered whether the castle was simply learning which names made her turn her head.

The basin knocked once more.

The sound came from above her this time.

Mara lifted the torch. The ceiling was bare stone. A thin line of water had appeared along the mortar, crawling against gravity toward the centre of the room. The red glow dimmed.

The bell inside her pack began to hum.

She held it still, but the sound continued. She did not unwrap it. She did not speak. She did not move toward the basin.

The hum stopped.

From beyond the door, a voice said, “Mara Hallowell.”

The same precise voice that had spoken her name at the handleless door.

Mara’s throat tightened. She stood with both hands on the pack straps and said nothing.

A pause followed. Then came a second voice, quieter, uncertain: “Which one?”

Mara kept silent.

The iron latch lifted by itself.

She drew the serpent button from her pocket. It was still cold. She held it in her fist, resisting the urge to press it against the door, to force an answer out of the castle or force a way out of the room. The metal edges cut into her palm.

The latch lowered.

The voice beyond the door did not speak again.

Mara waited until the trembling in her knees eased. Then she crossed to the basin and examined the cloth blocking the drain. It was a strip of red wool, frayed at both ends. A faded stitch marked one corner: E.

Elspeth’s scarf had been red. This was not the same cloth; the weave was finer, the colour darker. Still, Mara felt the familiar pull of an almost-answer. She resisted it. She had built too many conclusions from scraps.

She used the end of her torch to lift the wool. Beneath it lay a small metal disc, no larger than a coin. The disc was pitted and dark. Three grooves marked one side. The other side was blank.

Mara did not take it. She replaced the wool over the drain.

A faint click came from inside the basin, as if something below had accepted the covering.

The red light brightened again.

She examined the room’s door. There was no keyhole, only a narrow slot where a key might have been inserted from the other side. At floor level, a gap opened into the next passage. The gap was too small for her body but wide enough for the torchbeam. Beyond it, she could see stone steps climbing upward.

A way out, perhaps. Or a route further in that happened to rise.

Mara knelt and peered through. Dust covered the steps. A thin white mark had been painted on the wall beside them. Then another, several steps higher. The clear marks.

The path she had followed from the laundry had returned in another form.

She tested the door with her shoulder. It did not move. She tried the iron key in the narrow slot, but it was too large. She could force the latch with the serpent button, though the room’s warmth and the basin’s quiet made her hesitate. Instead, she searched the walls and found a small iron lever hidden behind the nameplate.

The plate slid sideways when she pressed its edge. Behind it was a keyhole.

Mara fitted the iron key. It turned with a dry click.

The door opened onto the stair she had seen through the gap. The red-lit room remained behind her, the basin silent. She did not take the nameplate, the metal disc, or the scrap of wool. She left everything in place.

At the threshold she paused and looked back. The basin had returned to darkness. The words on the wall—SIX IS A ROOM, NOT A CHILD—were just legible in the torchbeam.

She copied them once more in her notebook, then shut it.

The stairs rose through the stone. The white marks ended at a landing where a narrow window opened onto a dark shaft. Far below, a faint silver glimmer moved across water. The stair continued upward, but the wall on the next flight bore a fresh scratch: three grooves, their ends interrupted by a short line.

Someone had carved beneath it: **SECOND BELL. WAIT.**

Mara looked at the words for a long time.

She did not know whether the instruction was a warning, a memory, or bait. She did know that she had heard one bell already and that the wrapped bell in her pack had answered. Snape’s message remained the only clear instruction she trusted, and it forbade the very thing the inscription seemed to demand.

She wrote the phrase down without obeying it.

Then she climbed.

The stair ended at a low landing with two exits. One was a heavy oak door with no markings. The other was a narrow opening screened by hanging strips of canvas. From behind the canvas came the smell of cold air and wet leaves. Somewhere beyond, a creature gave a short, breathy snort.

Mara lifted the canvas with the tip of her torch.

The opening led to an exterior passage. Through a break in the stone she could see a strip of night sky, clouded and nearly black. The ruined grounds lay beyond, indistinct beneath the trees. She had come around to the outer wall again—or to a place that looked like it.

A shape moved across the gap in the clouds. Large wings, slow and silent.

Mara lowered the canvas.

She did not know whether the stair had brought her nearer the surface or merely shown her an imitation of it. The oak door might lead deeper into the castle. The canvas passage might open onto the grounds, where her injured ankle and dwindling food would leave her exposed. She had no map for either route.

The pack shifted. The bell remained silent.

A sharp, deliberate knock came from the oak door.

Mara stepped away from it.

Three knocks followed, evenly spaced.

Then one.

The same pattern she had felt in the walls.

The canvas stirred though there was no wind.

Mara moved toward the opening but did not lift it. She put one hand on the stone beside it and felt the cold of the outside air through the crack. For a moment, the possibility of leaving seemed almost real. She could abandon the bell, the clues, the castle’s elaborate game of names. She could find the rail line again, follow it south, and try to return to a world that had never promised her magic.

She imagined the open sea, the long crossing back to New York, the ordinary rooms waiting in Connecticut. None of them had ever felt like safety. Hogwarts had not made her powerful. It had made her leg bleed, her cheek scar, her past uncertain. It had also put a bell in her pack and a warning from Snape in her hand.

The oak door knocked again.

Mara turned from the canvas.

She took out the sealed cylinder. The wax seals were still intact. She ran her thumb over the silver S and felt the tiny ridge of the scratched warning. If the bell is heard, do not answer it.

She had not answered. She had followed, carried, approached, and listened. Perhaps that was already enough.

The cylinder’s seam had widened. A dark line ran through the wax, fine as a hair. Mara put it away without opening it.

Then she chose the oak door.

The iron key did not fit. She tried the serpent button, but it remained cold. She pressed her ear to the wood. No sound came from the other side. She set the wrapped bell on the floor, stepped back, and watched it carefully.

Nothing happened.

The door’s latch lifted.

Mara did not pick up the bell. She took one step backward, toward the canvas opening. The latch lowered again.

She tried once more, lifting the bell by its cloth wrapping. The latch rose a fraction.

She set it down.

The latch clicked shut.

Mara stared at the door. The castle was not asking for the bell to ring. It was asking her to bring it close. That distinction might matter. It might be the kind of distinction that got a person through a door and left them unable to come back.

She picked up the bundle and tucked it beneath her coat, holding it against her ribs. The latch lifted fully. The door opened inward.

Beyond lay a short passage with a ceiling so low she had to bend. The stones were dark with moisture. At the far end, a weak green light shone through a crack. The air smelled of ash and old parchment.

Mara entered. The oak door closed behind her.

The bell did not ring.

The passage ended at a small chamber containing a desk, a broken chair and a wall of narrow shelves. Most of the shelves were empty. One held a row of glass jars, their contents dried to brown flakes. Another held a single leather-bound book, swollen from damp. On its cover, someone had pressed a silver S into the leather.

Mara stopped just inside the doorway.

The book might be Snape’s. It might be a trap shaped like her desire. The sealed cylinder in her pack suddenly felt heavier than the bell.

She did not touch the book.

Instead she examined the desk. A thin layer of dust covered it, except for one clean rectangle where something had recently been removed. A folded sheet lay near the edge, weighted by a black stone. The stone bore three grooves and an interrupted circle.

Mara lifted the paper with two fingers.

It contained a single line, written in a sharp, disciplined hand:

**If you have found the bell, you are already too late to keep it.**

Beneath that, in smaller writing, an initial: **S.**

Mara’s pulse struck hard in her throat. She read the line again. It did not tell her what to do. It did not explain what the bell was, or why Snape had warned against answering it. It only told her that finding it had changed something.

She turned the paper over. The reverse was blank.

From somewhere under the floor came a faint, distant ring.

The wrapped bell in her arms began to vibrate.

Mara held it tighter. She did not unwrap it. She did not speak.

The green light in the chamber narrowed to a thin line beneath the far shelf. A hidden panel had opened there, revealing a dark space behind the wall. From within came the sound of water running uphill.

Mara looked at the book with the silver S. Then at the opening. Then at the bell.

The line on Snape’s paper blurred as damp spread across it, though no water touched the desk. The ink ran into a dark thread that crossed the word **bell**, then split into three fine branches.

Mara put the page in her notebook.

She backed away from the hidden opening and moved toward the chamber door. It had no handle on this side. The latch was a shallow groove in the wood, too narrow for her fingers. She tried the iron key. It turned nothing. She pressed the serpent button against the groove.

The brass warmed at last.

A green pulse moved beneath its surface, faint but unmistakable. The latch clicked. The door opened onto the narrow stair behind her.

She stepped out and pulled it shut. The glow vanished from the button. She had not used it to gain something. She had used it to leave.

That, she thought, was a difference worth remembering.

At the top of the stair, the canvas passage had gone still. Beyond it, the strip of night sky had disappeared. The opening showed only a rough stone wall, close and featureless.

The outside route was gone.

Mara stood alone on the landing, the bell pressed beneath her coat, the cylinder unopened in her pack, and the paper from the chamber tucked in her notebook. Her ankle hurt. Her cheek stung. The stair below was silent.

From behind the oak door came one quiet knock.

Mara did not answer it.

She continued upward, following the white marks until they ended at a door painted the same tired green as the service-room door. A brass plate had been fixed to it. The letters were scratched almost beyond reading, but she could make out two words:

**STAFF COMMONS.**

She tried the handle. It turned.

The room beyond had been ransacked. Chairs lay overturned. A long table had split down the middle, its surface scarred by heat. A row of cupboards had been torn open and emptied onto the floor. Blast damage had blackened the far wall, and a section of ceiling had collapsed across the hearth. Someone had made a shelter in one corner, piling blankets over a low frame of broken furniture.

On the wall above the shelter, a faded evacuation notice remained pinned beneath a knife. The paper had curled but not fallen. Mara read the surviving words by torchlight:

**…families and noncombatants to the lower route…**

**…wait for the second bell…**

**…do not leave anyone uncounted…**

The lower edge had been torn away.

Mara entered carefully. The bell stayed quiet. The room seemed empty, but the shelter blankets had been drawn tight around the frame. She approached without touching them. A small wooden toy lay beside the shelter: a horse with one painted eye and a wheel missing.

She crouched to see whether anything lay beneath the blankets. The cloth was stiff with dust. On its upper fold, someone had traced a word with a fingertip through the grime.

**AV.**

Mara rose. A narrow door stood behind the broken hearth, half hidden by fallen stone. The iron key from the elf might fit. She crossed the room and tested it. The lock turned smoothly.

Beyond the door was a passage climbing toward the castle’s upper floors. A draft carried the smell of leaves and distant rain. On the wall, a small white mark indicated the route upward. The mark was fresh, its edges still bright.

Mara looked back once at the shelter, the evacuation notice, the toy horse. She had not found a child. She had not found Aveline. The words she had collected seemed to point to a choice made long ago, but every time she thought she understood it, the castle rearranged the evidence.

She stepped into the passage.

Behind her, the staff-room door swung shut. The wooden toy rolled a few inches across the dusty floor and came to rest beside the shelter.

Mara kept climbing. At the next landing, the white marks ended at a stone arch. Three grooves had been cut into its keystone. Beneath them, someone had scratched a name into the stone.

**AV.**

The bell in Mara’s arms gave a small, answering hum.

She did not answer it.

A figure stood beyond the arch: a girl in a dark, old-fashioned school uniform, pale as the dust on the stones. Her outline wavered where the torchlight passed through her. One side of her face was hidden by hair, but the visible eye fixed on the bundle in Mara’s arms.

The girl lifted one translucent hand. “You brought it back.”

Mara could see the stair through the ghost’s body. The figure did not flicker away or vanish when Mara’s torchbeam crossed her. She was there, unmistakably dead and unmistakably present.

Mara stayed where she was.

The ghost’s hand lowered. The bell fell silent beneath its wrapping.

Then the girl turned and began to descend. Her feet never touched the steps.

Mara raised her torch and followed. The stair went down.
