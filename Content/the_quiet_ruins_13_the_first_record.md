## Chapter Thirteen: The First Record

At the far end of the court, the door opened inward.

Mara kept walking.

The movement behind her made no sound. No hinge groaned; no latch clicked. Yet she felt the door open as plainly as she felt the rain stop against her coat. The inscription above it—**M. HALLOW**—seemed to darken, the letters drinking the thin light from the service court.

“You can come in now,” said the voice that almost belonged to her mother.

Mara crossed the court toward the service arch.

She had left the bell below, in the shaft beside the round door. She had wedged its clapper and run when the first note came free. She had chosen to leave it. That mattered. It had to matter. But the bell had rung again after she abandoned it, and the door had opened anyway.

The castle had taken her choice and continued without her.

She reached the arch. A cold pressure tightened around her ribs. Her pack strap pulled sharply backward, as if someone had seized it. Mara stumbled, caught herself against the stone, and looked over her shoulder.

Nothing held the strap. The new door stood open. Beyond it was not a room but a depth of darkness with a pale floor far below, as though the doorway had been cut through the castle and into a buried night. A shape waited at the threshold. It had her height, her coat, her pack. Its face was hidden.

Mara dragged the strap forward. It stretched tight against her shoulder.

“Let go.”

The shape lifted one hand.

The pain in Mara’s burned palm flared. Her fingers opened against her will. The silver-S cylinder shifted inside her pack and struck her spine. She clutched the strap with her good hand, dug her boot against the floor, and leaned toward the arch.

The force pulling her did not feel like a hand. It felt like an instruction her body had decided to obey.

Mara went down on one knee.

She reached for the knife at her belt, but the door drew her backward. Her pack dragged across the stones. She thought of letting it go, of saving herself and abandoning Snape’s cylinder, the record strip, the ledger scraps. The offer had found its way into her mind before the voice made it: power without a price, if she would only step through.

She refused to look at the doorway.

“Not yours,” she said.

The pull strengthened. Her knee slid over wet stone. The door’s darkness opened wider, swallowing the pale floor beyond it.

A second voice called from the service arch behind her.

“Hold on.”

Mara looked up. The dark healer stood beneath the arch, its healer’s badge torn away, its stitched mouth split at one corner. Black veins webbed its throat. One arm hung crooked, and the other reached toward her. It had followed her from the infirmary—or the castle had placed it here. She could no longer tell the difference.

The healer grabbed the back of her coat.

For one instant, the two forces pulled in opposite directions. Mara’s shoulder screamed. The healer’s fingers were cold through the cloth, but they held.

“Why?” she gasped.

The healer’s mouth worked around its torn stitches. “Bargain.”

“You want me to ring the bell.”

“I wanted.” It swallowed with effort. “Not now.”

The door pulled harder. The healer’s boots slid. It did not let go.

“What do you want?”

“Close the place.”

“Then close it.”

“I cannot.”

The voice was raw and human enough to make her distrust it more. Mara twisted, trying to get her feet beneath her. “Why should I help you?”

The healer looked past her at the open doorway. Its eyes had gone almost entirely black. “It uses you. It uses me. You can choose.”

“I already chose. I left the bell.”

The creature gave a short, broken laugh. “It heard.”

The pull jerked Mara backward. The healer grunted and locked both hands around her coat. The seam at her shoulder began to tear.

Mara had no time to decide whether the bargain was a lie. “What will you give me?”

“Way in.”

“To the Sixth Place?”

The healer nodded once.

“What else?”

“Power.”

The word caught inside her like a hook. She hated that it worked. She wanted magic. Not a glimpse of a hidden route, not a button that burned her, not a bell that belonged to someone else. She wanted the ability to make the castle answer when she spoke. She wanted control over the thing pulling her, over the black, over every locked door that had kept its secrets from her. She wanted to stop being the person who had to run because someone else had made the rules.

She wanted what Snape had left behind because she believed it could make her more than a Muggle with a knife and a pack full of stolen clues.

“Show me,” she said.

The healer’s split mouth tightened. “First you help.”

“Then you show me.”

“Then I show.”

“What does it cost?”

The healer said nothing.

Mara looked at the black opening. She could feel the pull in her teeth now, in the joints of her fingers, in the old part of her that had learned to obey whoever sounded certain. She remembered a winter room in Connecticut, radiators clanging without heat. She remembered her mother saying, Just until Friday, and the landlord not coming, and the window latch broken in Mara’s hand.

She had learned then that a promise could keep you waiting. She had also learned that taking apart the latch did not make the room warm.

“Say the cost,” Mara demanded.

The healer’s grip slipped. “Your name.”

“No.”

“Not the sound. The hold.”

Mara understood only enough to be frightened. “You want my name?”

“It already has it.” The healer’s eyes flicked to the door. “I can loosen it. If you help me close the place.”

“You’re asking me to trust you.”

“No.” The healer’s voice broke. “I am asking you to bargain.”

The distinction should not have mattered. It did.

Mara looked at the healer’s hand gripping her coat, the black channels under its skin, the badge mark still pale on its chest. It had hurt patients. It had used the bell. It had helped make the door. None of that was erased by its fear. But it was afraid of the same thing that wanted her.

“Tell me what happened to Aveline,” Mara said.

The healer closed its eyes. “Not here.”

“Who did she leave behind?”

“Not here.”

“Then we have no bargain.”

The creature opened its eyes. “In the Sixth Place.”

The door dragged Mara another foot across the court. Her pack scraped over a crack in the stone. The healer’s arms shook. She could not hold out much longer.

“Take me there,” Mara said. “Help me close it. Then tell me what you can. If you lie, I leave you.”

The healer nodded.

“And the power?”

It hesitated. “I show what was left.”

“Snape’s work?”

A nod.

“Enough to control the black?”

“No.”

“Enough to control something?”

The healer looked at her with its dark eyes. “Maybe.”

Mara hated the answer because she wanted it anyway.

“Agreed.”

The healer pressed its thumb against the back of her neck. A cold line passed from its skin into hers. Mara flinched. The pull from the doorway slackened—not gone, but eased enough for her to plant a boot and get upright. A black thread ran under the skin of her wrist, then vanished beneath her sleeve.

The healer released her.

“Was that the price?”

“Mark.”

“What does it do?”

“Lets the door find you.”

Mara stared at it. “That was not in the bargain.”

“It knew you already.” The healer glanced at the open doorway. “Now it knows where.”

Mara struck it across the face with the back of her hand. Her burned palm blazed. The healer did not retaliate.

“Next time,” she said, “tell me the cost before you take it.”

“Next time,” it rasped, “there may not be.”

The open doorway shuddered. A line of white appeared along its inner edge. The shape in the threshold leaned forward. Its hidden face turned toward Mara.

The healer seized her uninjured wrist and pulled her through the service arch.

They ran.

The service court bent around the infirmary wing. The healer led her to a narrow stair hidden behind a collapsed screen. Mara followed with her knife out and her pack clutched close. The mark at her wrist tightened whenever she drifted toward the open court, guiding her by pain. She did not like how easily her body learned to follow it.

At the stair’s end, they reached the records room. The shelves had shifted. Several drawers had been pulled out and tipped onto the floor. Black threads crossed the pages, linking names to empty lines. Mara saw **HALLOWELL** on a sheet beneath a smear of ink. The thread that touched it was taut, running down through the floor.

She stopped.

“Who wrote my name?”

The healer looked at the page and then away.

“Who?”

“Your family.”

Mara felt the words before she understood them. “My family never came here.”

“Not your mother.” The healer pointed at the older handwriting. “Before.”

The name beneath Hallowell was almost erased. She made out **ELSPETH VANE**. A second mark connected them: a circle with a gap at the top, crossed by three grooves.

“How?”

“Blood. Work. A promise.”

“That means nothing.”

“It means they carried the threshold.”

The black line on the page twitched. Mara stepped back. Her family’s history had always been something she could keep in a small box: a string of moves, rooms rented by the week, relatives who never stayed long enough to become stories. Her mother had given her no account of old names or old obligations. The closest thing to a family heirloom was a battered tin of photographs and a silver thimble that had belonged to someone Mara never met.

“Did my family help build this?”

The healer shook its head. “Helped hold it shut.”

“Then why is my name on the list?”

“Because someone opened it.”

Mara looked again at the thread. It was not only connected to her name. It ran from the page through the floor, toward the lower chambers. Hallowell had not been entered as a patient or a visitor. It was written beside the words **FIXED LINE**.

A memory rose—her mother standing at the kitchen sink in Connecticut, telling Mara never to answer a voice through a closed door. Mara had thought it was a warning about strangers. Her mother had stared at the door while she said it. When Mara asked what she meant, her mother had changed the subject and started talking about the rent.

“How long did she know?” Mara whispered.

The healer turned its face away.

The answer was in the silence.

Mara folded the page and put it in her coat. If her family had helped hold the threshold shut, then their history was tied to the war and to Hogwarts in a way she had never imagined. The Muggle-wizard war had not merely destroyed the castle. It had altered whatever arrangement kept this place closed. The blast scars, the collapsed rooms, the hurried evacuation—perhaps the war had broken the line. Perhaps someone had used the chaos to open the Sixth Place. Her family’s old promise had been carried across generations until it reached her, an American Muggle who had come looking for Snape’s power.

“Why did no one tell me?”

“Fear.”

“Whose?”

The healer did not answer.

A shiver passed through the records room. Every drawer slid shut at once.

Mara’s mark tightened. The thread on the Hallowell page went slack, then snapped. Somewhere deep below, a bell rang.

The healer grabbed her sleeve. “Now.”

They crossed the room and entered a passage behind the shelves. It descended to the hidden shaft. The hatch hung open, bent at one corner. Mara recognized the three grooves around its rim. The healer climbed down first, moving with a stiffness that made each step look painful.

At the bottom, the round door waited. The bell lay on the stone in front of it, its clapper free. Mara had left it wedged. The stone she used had been crushed to powder. The brass was blackened around the rim, and a crack ran from its handle to its lip.

She had abandoned it, but it had not stayed abandoned.

Mara crouched to pick it up. The healer caught her wrist.

“No.”

“I need it.”

“It needs you.”

“It’s mine.”

The healer’s dark eyes flicked to the mark at her wrist. “No.”

The word struck too close to the truth. Mara yanked free and took the bell. Its brass was warm. The crack in it glowed faintly, a greenish light like the serpent button’s lost flare.

“What does the bargain require?” she asked.

“Bell silent.”

“You knew I would take it.”

“I hoped you wouldn’t.”

The admission did not make Mara put it down. She carried it to the door. The narrow slot at the bottom was shaped like a mouth. Behind the door, something breathed. The black stains in the grooves moved, not outward but inward, drawn toward the room beyond.

The healer pointed to a shallow depression beside the threshold. It was the exact shape of the bell.

Mara set it down. The fit was perfect.

When the bell touched the stone, the black veins in the healer’s throat faded. In exchange, the mark at Mara’s wrist tightened until her hand went numb.

“Now what?”

“First record.”

“Where?”

The healer touched the door. “Here.”

Mara thought of the brass machine in the hidden room, its recording of Elspeth Vane. Snape’s note said the first record was not the last. The line scratched into the shaft wall had said the first record was not a voice. The healer’s answer made the door itself the record, or the room behind it.

She set her good hand against the stone.

The door was warm.

A sound traveled through it: a woman counting under her breath. One, two, three, four, five—then silence. The count began again. At the fifth number, the black in the grooves drew back. At the missing sixth, it surged.

Mara heard another sound beneath the counting, faint and familiar: her mother’s voice, saying, “I didn’t know they would keep it.”

Mara pulled her hand away.

The healer leaned close. “Family remembers.”

“What does that mean?”

“It knows the first line.”

“You said my family held it shut.”

“Not all.”

The door gave a low, grinding shift. The black outline beneath it widened. A thin stream crossed the stone and curled around Mara’s boot. The healer stepped between her and the door. It raised its injured hand and spoke a sequence of syllables in a language Mara did not know. Each word made the mark at her wrist burn. On the last, the healer’s fingers closed. The black paused.

Mara understood the bargain more clearly then. The healer was not offering a safe route or a clean answer. It was offering access—at the price of a mark that let the door find her, and a task that might close the place or make it wider. It had not lied about the danger. It had simply decided she would accept the cost once the door began pulling.

“Give me the power you promised,” she said.

“After.”

“Now.”

“After.”

Mara brought the bell up and struck it against the wall.

The sound was sharp. The black recoiled. The healer cried out as if the note had cut it. The door shuddered, and a green line opened in the stone around its edge. For one breath, Mara saw through it: a circular room with six places cut into the floor, five crossed out. In the open place stood a girl with her back to them. Beside her was a smaller figure, too blurred to identify.

Then the sight snapped shut.

The bell had answered her. It had opened a view without opening the door. A flash of power, no more than a glimpse, but it made her fingers tighten around the brass.

The healer seized the bell. “You promised.”

“I promised to help close it.”

“You are opening.”

“I am learning.”

That was the truth, and the way she said it frightened her. She liked the feeling of the bell obeying. She liked that the healer had to stop her. She wanted the power to command the room, the door, and the black. She could hear the dose’s old promise in that desire, even though the bottle was empty.

The healer wrenched the bell from her hand and placed it back in the depression. “Not again.”

“Give me what you promised.”

The healer set two fingers against the silver-S cylinder through the pack. “Workroom.”

“You can take me there?”

“Show you.”

“And the power?”

“Snape made a way.”

The answer was all Mara needed to hear. She wanted to refuse the bargain, but she had already accepted it. She wanted to go back for the page, the ledger, the lost fragment, the button. She wanted to leave the castle. Most of all, she wanted to know what Snape had left and whether it could make the mark at her wrist obey her instead of the door.

“Take me to the Sixth Place,” she said. “Then the workroom.”

The healer studied her. “Sixth Place first.”

The room beyond the door made a sound like a breath sucked through teeth.

The bell rang once in its depression.

The round door opened inward.

Mara’s mark burned. The healer’s hand clamped around her wrist, but the pull came from the door itself. It seized her, dragging her forward. She dug her heels into the stone. The healer braced itself against the wall and caught her around the waist.

“Resist!” it shouted.

“I am!”

The Sixth Place unfolded beyond the threshold. It was not a chamber so much as a circle of stone that seemed to continue beyond sight. Six shallow circles had been cut into the floor. Five were crossed through with dark lines. The sixth remained open. At its center stood the girl from the glimpse, her hair pinned back, her clothes old-fashioned and damp at the hem.

Aveline.

Mara held against the pull. The space drew the air from her lungs. The open circle widened—not physically, but in her sight—until it seemed large enough to swallow the room around it. She felt the mark at her wrist tug toward it. The black lines under her skin answered the three grooves in the floor.

At the far edge of the circles, another figure stood. A small one. A child, perhaps. Its face was turned away. Aveline’s body stood between it and the open circle.

Mara tried to take one step back.

The door pulled her forward.

The healer dug its heels into the stone. “She chose the place. Not the child.”

“Who is it?” Mara shouted.

The healer’s grip slipped. “Final record.”

“Tell me now!”

“Not mine to say.”

The child turned its head. The face was hidden in shadow, but Mara recognized the shape of the hair, the small curve of the shoulder, the way it held one hand closed. Her mother had kept one photograph from Connecticut: a little girl sitting on a porch step beside Mara’s mother, years before Mara was born. Her mother had never explained who the girl was. Mara had once asked, and her mother had said, “Someone we lost.”

The blurred figure in the Sixth Place wore the same small silver thimble on a cord around its neck—the one from the family tin. The old heirloom, passed from one unseen hand to another.

Mara stopped fighting for one terrible instant.

The pull seized that instant and used it.

The healer shouted her name. Its fingers tore away from her wrist. Mara’s boots slid over the threshold, scraping against stone. She reached for the doorframe with her good hand. Her fingers closed around the edge, but the mark burned hot and the black line under her skin lit up. The doorframe became slick as wet glass.

She was pulled into the Sixth Place.

Behind her, the healer remained outside the threshold, one hand extended. It could not follow. The door began to close between them.

The last thing Mara saw was its face—not stitched now, but human, exhausted, and afraid.

Then the door shut.

The six circles lay silent around her. The five crossed-out places were empty. Aveline stood within the open one, still turned toward the child. The black had gathered around her feet, but did not cross the circle’s edge.

Mara pushed herself up. Her knees had struck stone. The pack had fallen beside her. The silver-S cylinder remained inside. She checked the strap, the clasp, her knife. All were there. The family page was gone; it had fallen in the records room. The bell was gone too, left outside in the depression that had opened the door.

The cost of abandoning it had been losing it anyway—and discovering it could still answer without her.

Aveline turned her head. Her face was young, pale, and tired. She looked at Mara as if she had been waiting a long time, but not for her specifically.

“You brought the name back,” Aveline said.

“I didn’t know.”

“No one does, at first.”

“Who is the child?”

Aveline glanced toward the shadowed figure. “The one I left behind.”

Mara took a step toward them. The open circle brightened. Her wrist mark tightened, and the black under her skin crawled toward her palm.

“What did you do?”

“I chose.”

“Did you save them?”

Aveline did not answer.

Mara understood that silence as the answer and hated her for it. She also understood that she could not ask the question and expect a clean reply. The child’s face remained hidden. The silver thimble glinted once, then vanished beneath the shadow.

A door appeared beyond the six circles. It was older than the castle stone around it, a narrow wooden door bound in iron. On its surface was a silver S.

Snape’s mark.

Mara’s want rose again, immediate and fierce. There was the legacy she had come for. The workroom. The power. The promise that she could make the castle’s rules bend. She stepped toward it.

Aveline caught her sleeve. Her fingers were cold, but solid.

“Not yet.”

Mara pulled free. “I’ve heard that before.”

“Then listen this time.”

The wooden door opened a fraction. Warm light spilled through the gap. The smell of old parchment and sharp herbs drifted into the Sixth Place. Somewhere beyond it, glass clinked softly. A table lamp turned on. A chair scraped back.

A man’s voice spoke from the workroom.

“Miss Hallowell.”

Mara stopped.

It was not quite Snape’s voice. She had never heard Snape speak. Still, the cadence carried the dry impatience of his notes, the same clipped certainty. The speaker knew her name and spoke it as if she had been expected.

“Come in,” the voice said. “I can teach you to control it.”

Aveline’s hand tightened on her sleeve.

Mara stared at the narrow opening. She could feel the magic behind it—not as a glow or a route but as pressure, rich and close. Something in the room answered the mark on her wrist. The power she wanted was not a metaphor. It was there, waiting, and it knew the shape of her hunger.

The child in the open circle lifted its closed hand.

A second silver thimble shone in its palm.

Mara took one step toward the workroom.

Aveline whispered, “That is not what he left.”

The light in the doorway went out.

From the darkness beyond came the sound of a bell, ringing slowly, as if someone were counting the seconds before it called again.
