## Chapter Three: The House at the Edge

By midmorning the mist had thinned enough to show the shape of the land. The river bent south, while the old road climbed west toward a low shoulder of rock. Beyond it, the hills rose in dark tiers, and above the highest ridge a broken tower appeared and disappeared as cloud crossed it. Mara kept to the water at first. The road was exposed, and the note in the signal hut had warned her away from it after dark. Daylight did not make the road safe, but it made it possible to see what might be waiting.

The new button rested in her pocket. She had wrapped it in the strip of grey cloth the elf had left, and still she could feel its faint warmth through the fabric. She had no intention of letting it burn her again. Whenever she thought of throwing it away, she remembered the green pulse running upstream beneath the river and the creatures’ shrieks. Whatever the button was, it had changed something. It might have drawn danger toward her; it might have driven it off. She could not tell which possibility frightened her more.

She made a list in the notebook while she walked: food, water, shelter, route. Then, beneath it, she wrote: **Do not assume the object wants to help.**

The handwriting looked too neat. Her fingers were trembling.

A line of stones marked the old road’s lower edge. Their carved faces had been weathered almost smooth, but one still showed a shield and the head of a serpent. Mara stopped beside it. Someone had driven a narrow iron spike through the carving. The spike was dark with age, and a length of red thread had been knotted around it.

She did not touch it.

Something clicked from the hillside.

Mara turned. The slope above the road was empty, except for a few bent grasses and a scatter of black stones. She waited, listening. A second click answered from farther off, followed by a sound like a fingernail drawn along glass.

She resumed walking, more quickly.

The road emerged from the valley beside a ruined cottage. Its roof had caved in, and the front wall was split by a jagged crack. The doorway had been bricked up from the inside. On the outer stones, someone had painted a white circle and cut it through with three strokes. Beneath the mark, in faded red letters, were the words **KEEP THE HOUSE CLOSED**.

Mara’s first instinct was to go around it. Her second was to look for evidence. She had come this far because other people had left traces, and because she had spent too long accepting that certain doors were not meant for her.

The cottage stood just off the road, at the edge of a small stand of rowan trees. Its windows were boarded, but one board had come loose. Mara used the back of her knife to lift it. The wood came away with a sigh of damp fibres. Inside, the room was nearly black. She directed the phone’s weak beam through the gap and saw a table, two chairs, a stove, and a line of small shoes laid neatly against the far wall.

She froze.

The shoes were children’s shoes, perhaps six pairs, arranged toe to heel. Mud had dried in the seams. A red scarf lay folded on the table. On its edge, someone had embroidered a name in yellow thread: **ELSPETH**.

The neatness disturbed her more than the dust. Nothing in the cottage had been left in the confusion of flight. The chairs were pushed in. A chipped cup stood beside the stove, its handle turned outward, and a row of pegs by the door held no coats. It looked less like a home abandoned and more like a room prepared for people who had been expected to return.

Mara lowered the board. There was no reason to enter. She had no need of the shoes or the scarf. She could leave the house as she had found it and keep to the road.

A child whispered from inside, “You’re late.”

Mara stepped back so quickly her heel struck the stone wall. The cottage remained still. The voice had been soft, close to the floor, and utterly ordinary.

She tried to decide whether she had heard a memory, a person, or her own mind making a sound from the silence. She thought of the old voices in the night, the ones that had asked who kept the lights on. Those had been distant, almost woven into the wind. This had sounded as if someone stood behind the door.

“Hello?” she called.

No answer.

The red thread on the iron spike stirred, though there was no wind at the cottage wall. The strand lifted and pointed toward the bricked doorway. Mara backed away. The thread fell flat again.

She should have kept walking. Instead, she went to the front of the cottage. The door was not merely blocked; the bricks had been set from inside, rough mortar pushed into every seam. Someone had made a barrier while still in the room, then found another way out—or had never needed to leave by the door. The white circle painted across the stones had been crossed through three times. The mark resembled the sign at the railway shelter, but the strokes were more deliberate, each ending in a hooked notch.

Beside the cottage, half buried in nettles, a metal box had been fixed to the wall. Its lid bore a corroded label: **CIVIL DEFENCE—LOCAL INSTRUCTIONS**. Mara scraped away the moss with her glove. The box was locked, but the hinges had rusted loose. She prised one edge open with her knife and heard the lid crack. Inside, a bundle of paper had fused into a grey lump. Most of it crumbled when she touched it. One sheet, folded in waxed cloth, remained legible in fragments.

The paper was a list of names and ages, written in several hands. The first column gave a destination: **HOGWARTS—NORTH SERVICE ENTRANCE**. The second recorded the number of children in each household. Against the name Elspeth, someone had drawn a small square and written **six with her**. Below that, in darker ink, was a hurried instruction: **At the second bell, take the lower path. No lamps. Keep the children between adults. If separated, do not call names.**

The final line had been overwritten until the paper was nearly torn: **Do not use the school road after the red flare.**

Mara read it twice. The war had not turned this cottage into a refuge. It had made it a waiting point, a place where families gathered before an evacuation toward the castle. The children had been meant to go through the hidden routes with the rest of the survivors. The shoes could have been spare pairs, set out for a group expected to arrive wet and exhausted. Or they could have belonged to the six children on the list.

She tried to picture them: adults whispering over a map, hands fastening scarves, someone making a promise that the castle would be safer than the road. The arrangement of the shoes became unbearable in a new way. Each pair seemed to be waiting for its owner to come back and put it on.

A brittle newspaper clipping lay folded inside the box. Its headline had mostly vanished, but she could make out the words **EVACUATION ROUTE CLOSED** and a date from the final winter of the war. A paragraph beneath it described a “mass movement of minors and noncombatants” toward the northern refuge. The article blamed a landslide and a fire at the old school access road. Someone had underlined a sentence in pencil: **Authorities cannot confirm the number of children missing after the night transfer.**

The pencil line had been pressed so hard it had cut the paper.

Mara slipped the clipping into her notebook. It was evidence, but not the kind she had hoped to find. She had wanted clues to hidden passages, to Snape’s stores, to the practical remnants of magic. Instead, the place was giving her a history made of incomplete lists and things people had left ready for a return that never happened.

The cottage’s loose board tapped once against the window frame.

Mara turned toward it. The dark inside remained unchanged. She could see the shoes, the scarf, and the dull shape of the table. On the table, the red scarf had shifted. She was certain it had been folded lengthwise before. Now one end hung over the edge, the embroidered name turned toward the window.

**ELSPETH.**

Mara looked away. Names, the elf had said, had power—or perhaps the elf had warned her not to use them. She could not remember the exact words. She could remember the way its single eye had fixed on her when she almost spoke Snape’s name.

From inside the cottage came a small sound: not a footstep, not a knock. A breath drawn in through the nose, patient and expectant.

“You’re late,” the voice said again.

It might have been the wind entering through a crack in the chimney. It might have been a phrase repeated by the boards and stones, held there from the night the evacuation failed. The words came with the same faint rasp each time, the last syllable clipped in precisely the same place. Mara thought of an old recording catching in a damaged machine, repeating a fragment long after the speaker had gone. It might have been someone waiting in the room. She could find no evidence for any one explanation, and the lack of evidence did not make the cottage feel empty.

She did not answer. She did not say the name on the scarf. But she had come for evidence, and the list in the box could not tell her whether the shoes belonged to the missing children or had been left for them. Mara went to the doorway and tested the brickwork. The barrier was loose at one edge; several bricks shifted when she pressed them. She worked them free with her knife and hands, wincing at every scrape of stone. The opening was narrow, but she could squeeze through.

Inside, the air was close and cold. Her phone beam found the shoes first, then the red scarf on the table. The embroidered name faced her. She kept her gaze on the stitching only long enough to confirm it was the same one, then looked away. Nothing moved. The whisper did not return.

She examined the room without touching the shoes. Six pairs, carefully lined up. One pair was smaller than the others, with a cracked buckle and a patch sewn across the toe. Beside them lay a child-sized coat, its sleeves turned inside out as if someone had removed it in a hurry. The table bore shallow cuts—perhaps tally marks, perhaps a child's attempt to write letters with a knife. Near the stove, the plaster had blistered in a narrow oval. The cottage had been burned from within, but only a little: a controlled flame, or a failed attempt to destroy something quickly.

Mara found a strip of paper wedged beneath one table leg. Its ink had run, leaving only fragments: **...second bell... lower way... keep together...** She placed it beside the evacuation list in her notebook and photographed the room. She did not take the scarf or any of the children's things. The silence seemed to grow more attentive while she worked, as though the house registered each movement and filed it away.

At the threshold, she turned once more toward the dark room. The whisper did not speak again. That steadiness—its absence at the moment she listened for it—made the earlier words feel less like a visitor and more like a signal triggered by her arrival, a scrap of the past released by some condition she had unknowingly met.

She left through the gap in the bricks and replaced none of them. The cottage remained open by the width of her shoulders, no longer sealed, though she could not tell whether she had freed anything or merely given it another way out.
At the road, she found a narrow track leaving west through the trees. A rusted sign lay face down in the weeds. She turned it with her boot. Its enamel lettering had mostly peeled away, but the bottom line remained: **SCHOOL ACCESS — STAFF ONLY**.

Hogwarts was still several miles distant, if the map could be trusted. This track might lead to an outbuilding or a wartime evacuation route. It might also be one of the roads the warning meant. Mara returned to the notebook and checked her copied map. The hand-drawn line that crossed the river ended at a square marked with a small black symbol. She had assumed it represented a shelter. Now she saw that the square sat almost exactly where the cottage stood.

Beside it, in a different hand, someone had written: **THE HOUSE REMEMBERS WHO LEFT.**

Mara shut the notebook.

A bird rose from the rowan branches with a clatter of wings. The sound made her flinch. She was beginning to understand how little of her fear belonged to the thing she feared. Every noise had become a warning because the place had taught her that warnings were everywhere and explanations were scarce.

She followed the westward track.

The school-access sign had been planted beside a fork, though the smaller branch was almost swallowed by bracken. Its posts bore three neat axe cuts and a strip of faded blue cloth. Mara brushed the cloth with one finger. It tore like wet paper. Beyond the fork, the buried track dipped between banks of earth, too narrow for a cart and too straight to be a deer path. It could have been made for people moving quickly and quietly.

She checked the map again. The black square marking the cottage sat at the edge of a dotted line that ran beneath the hill toward the castle. The line ended at a hand-drawn X. In the margin, someone had written **not the main way**. Beneath it, in a cramped script, another hand had added: **If the children are late, wait for the second bell. Do not send anyone back.**

Mara read the words until the letters began to blur. She imagined the adults who had written them—tired, afraid, arguing over a route while the sound of fighting carried across the valley. They had made rules because rules were something to hold in the dark. Do not use lamps. Do not call names. Do not send anyone back. All the instructions were practical, but each one admitted the same possibility: someone would be lost.

A small tin whistle lay in the mud under the sign. Its mouthpiece was dented, and its chain had snapped. Mara picked it up, wiped the grit away, then put it in her pocket. She told herself it was evidence, not a keepsake. Still, she could not shake the sense that taking it was another kind of answer to the whisper in the cottage.

The trees grew closer together. Rowan berries, shrivelled and black, clung to the branches like beads. The path was crossed by roots, and in places the soil had been churned by something heavy. She found prints in a patch of mud: narrow, deep impressions with three blunt points at the front. They could have belonged to a large bird. The stride was too long for the injured hippogriff she had seen.

She moved on without kneeling to measure them.

A low stone wall ran beside the track. Its stones had been fitted without mortar, and small scraps of cloth were wedged between them: a sleeve button, a strip of red wool, the corner of a handkerchief embroidered with a single letter. Each scrap faced the same direction, toward the castle. It was not a trail left by accident. Someone had marked the way, perhaps for a group moving by moonlight, perhaps for children too frightened to remember a route.

At one point the wall dipped, and Mara saw a shallow hollow beyond it. Six small stones had been set in a circle. Their tops were blackened, though there was no sign of a fire. In the centre lay a cracked glass marble, green and cloudy with age. She did not cross the wall. The marble caught the weak light and briefly seemed to contain a moving fleck, like a tiny eye turning to follow her.

The old war voices came back to her—not as words this time but as the impression of a crowd far away, many people speaking softly at once. The sound rose when she faced the hollow and faded when she turned toward the path. She could not tell whether it came from the trees, the wind, or from the pressure building behind her ears. She walked faster until the murmur dwindled.

At the top of the rise, the path opened onto a terrace of broken stone. Below, the valley spread out in layers of mist and ruined wall. For the first time, she saw Hogwarts clearly—not the whole castle, but enough to understand its scale. Towers leaned at angles that seemed impossible. One had split down its centre. The central keep remained, black against the low cloud, its windows empty and its roofline jagged. The lake lay beyond it, flat and silver. No reflection of the castle appeared on its surface.

Mara stood with both hands gripping the strap of her rucksack.

The castle did not look like a place that had been abandoned. It looked like a place that had survived an attack and was still waiting for the next one.

She opened the notebook to the page with Snape’s name. Her list seemed childish against the view: potions rooms, office, Slytherin spaces. She remembered the boy from the corridor vision, watching her from the green-lit passage. She had imagined that finding his belongings would be like uncovering a set of tools. A wand, a book, a cache of ingredients: useful things, proof that magic could be acquired if a person searched carefully enough.

But the castle had been full of people. Children had walked out through hidden routes. Some had never been found. Whatever Snape left behind had been left in a place where the dead and the living might still claim it.

A thin ribbon of smoke rose from somewhere below the terrace.

Mara crouched and looked through the stones. There was a small cluster of buildings near the outer wall, one with its chimney intact. Smoke emerged from it in a narrow, straight thread, scarcely disturbed by the wind.

She was not alone.

A grey shape moved between the buildings. Then another. They were too small to be people, but one carried something long and pale over its shoulder. Mara raised her binoculars. The figure stopped and turned its head toward the terrace. She lowered the glasses at once, though she could not explain why.

A voice spoke from behind her.

“Wrong side.”

Mara spun around. The elf stood among the rowans. It was the one-eyed creature from the shepherd’s hut—or she thought it was. Its shape was wrong in ways she could not immediately name. One shoulder rose nearly to its ear, while the other sloped low beneath a coat pieced from different sleeves. Its arms were too long, elbows bending outward like the joints of a spider. One hand rested against a tree trunk, fingers splayed; there were seven of them, each thin as a root and ending in a blunt, dark nail. The other hand was tucked against its chest, where the ribs made a small cage beneath the skin.

Its face was not the wizened, doll-like face Mara had expected from stories and illustrations. The skin had been pulled taut over a narrow skull, then gathered in folds along one cheek. A single eye sat low and off-centre, glossy and pale, with a cloudy film drawn across the top of it. Where the other eye should have been, a seam of puckered flesh ran from brow to jaw. Its mouth opened sideways, not down, and the teeth inside were tiny and flat, like worn grains of rice. A brass key hung from its neck, tied with cord that had bitten into the skin.

Mara had thought of house-elves as servants: small, quick, and somehow comic in their clothes. Nothing about this creature was comic. It looked not like an elf dressed in scraps, but like something that had been forced to keep wearing the remains of a house after the house had ceased to be a home.

“Which side is right?” Mara asked.

The elf's jaw twitched. A click came from its throat, then another. “Leave-side.”

“I’m trying to reach the castle.”

It shook its head once. A strained breath scraped through its teeth. “All… try.”

“I’m looking for something that belonged to—” She stopped herself before the name escaped.

The elf’s visible eye narrowed. “His… hole.”

Mara’s hand went to the button through her pocket. “What does that mean?”

“Not button.” The elf touched the brass key at its throat, then recoiled as if the metal had burned it. “Door. No—” Its mouth worked soundlessly. It pressed both hands to its chest, fighting for breath. “Mouth.”

“You mean this thing is a mouth?”

The elf gave a sharp, frustrated click. Its head jerked toward the river, then back to her. “Down. You… put down. River told.”

“I didn’t understand what it was.”

“Still… don’t.” The words came out thin and wet. It tried to say more, but only a choking rattle followed; one long arm wrapped across its throat, and the single eye squeezed shut.

The elf looked toward the smoking buildings. A small figure had appeared at the far end of the terrace below. It stood with its arms hanging at its sides, facing uphill. Its head was wrapped in cloth. It did not move.

The elf made a low, rapid series of clicks. The figure below turned away.

“Who is that?” Mara whispered.

“Not house.”

“What is it, then?”

The elf’s jaw worked. “House learned to walk.”

A gust moved through the trees. The smoke below bent sharply toward the castle, though the wind touched Mara’s face from the opposite direction.

The elf seized her sleeve. Its fingers were cold and hard as twigs. A tremor ran through its long arm. “No road. No bell. No name.”

“I need to get inside.”

“Inside… gets.”

“Everything here seems to want that.”

The elf made a dry, clicking sound. For a moment, something almost like pity crossed its face. “Learning.” It released her and pointed to a narrow stairway descending beside the terrace. “Under wall. Water black—stop. Clear—don’t.”

“That makes no sense.”

“Good.” It backed into the rowans, one hand pressed against its throat. “Sense… finds.”

“Wait. What do you know about Sn—”

The elf’s face tightened. Mara swallowed the name. “About the man I’m looking for?”

The elf tried to answer. Its mouth opened sideways; no words came, only a thin wheeze. It tapped the brass key once with a crooked nail, then pointed toward the castle. “Dead… hungry.”

It vanished among the trees.

Mara remained on the terrace until the cold had soaked through her knees. Below, the wrapped figure had disappeared. The smoking chimney still drew a straight dark line into the sky.

She checked the button. Its warmth had faded. In the cloth around it, a new mark had appeared: three small dark circles arranged in a line, as if the brass had pressed through the fabric without touching it.

She held the cloth up to the grey daylight. The circles were not stains; they had no colour, only a shallow dent in the weave. They were spaced exactly like the three strokes on the cottage’s white mark and the three grooves in the hippogriff’s wound. Mara’s stomach tightened. The repeated pattern might be coincidence, or a warning, or a sign that the same thing had touched every place she had passed.

She tried to remember the elf’s words exactly. *When water runs black, stop. When it runs clear, don’t.* It had not said what to do when the water was brown. Perhaps the distinction mattered only underground. Perhaps it had given her a riddle because a direct instruction would have been dangerous. She was learning that nearly every piece of advice here came with a trapdoor hidden inside it.

Mara could turn back. The railway was behind her, the station perhaps a day’s walk away. She could find a village, a telephone, a reason to tell herself the journey had been a mistake. The choice was real; she felt it as clearly as the straps cutting her shoulders.

She thought of the list in the cottage: six children with Elspeth, the second bell, no lamps, no names. The people who had planned that evacuation had believed there would be a safe way through. They had trusted the castle because it had been a refuge before the war made it a target. Now the route lay open beneath broken stone, and no one remained to tell her whether it had ever worked.

She thought of the signal hut’s warning, the old newspaper, the elf’s key. She thought of Snape—not the man she had built from books and fragments, not the severe figure whose reputation had survived his life, but a person who had known the castle from within and might have hidden something where only the desperate would look. She wanted what he had left because she wanted power. She wanted power because she had spent too much of her life watching other people decide what she could endure, what she could know, where she was allowed to go.

The thought did not make her braver. It made her ashamed, and then angry at the shame. She could not undo the reason she had come. She could only decide what she did with it.

Then she looked again at the broken towers.

She had not crossed an ocean to become sensible at the edge of the thing she wanted.

Before descending, she made a small mark on the terrace stones with a piece of chalk, an arrow pointing back toward the rowans. If the stairway turned, split, or took her in a circle, she wanted a sign she had made herself. She tied the loose end of her scarf around a projecting stone, then checked the knot twice. The cloth looked absurdly bright against the grey ruin. She left it anyway.

At the mouth of the stair, the air smelled of wet iron and extinguished candles. The sound of the wind above dwindled as she went down. She counted steps under her breath, then stopped when she remembered the warning not to use names. Numbers were not names, she told herself. The distinction comforted her for perhaps four steps.

The stairway beneath the wall was narrow and slick. At its foot, a shallow runnel carried water between the stones. Mara watched it for a long moment. The stream was brown, not black, not clear. She did not know whether the elf had meant the colour, the clarity, or something that had to be seen rather than described. She tested the edge with the tip of a stick. The water curled around it, carrying a thin skin of oil that broke into green and violet bands.

From somewhere beneath the wall came a muted metallic sound: a bell struck once, far away. Mara stiffened. The cottage instruction returned to her—at the second bell, take the lower path. She waited, counting heartbeats. No second sound came. Perhaps the note had been meant for the children decades ago. Perhaps something below had heard her arrive.

She stepped over the runnel and continued into the shadow beneath Hogwarts.
