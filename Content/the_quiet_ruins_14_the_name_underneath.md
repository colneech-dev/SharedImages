# The Quiet Ruins

## Chapter Fourteen: The Name Underneath

The door took her by the shoulders.

Mara braced both hands against the frame, boots skidding over the stone. She tried to hold the threshold, tried to wrench herself back toward the corridor, but the pull had no weight she could fight—only certainty. The bell was gone. She had left it behind. Still, the dark knew her name.

The door slammed shut behind her.

She struck the floor on one knee. Pain flashed through her injured palm, and the torch rolled from her hand, spinning a crooked ring of light across the stone. For several breaths she could only listen: to her own ragged breathing, to the small clatter of the torch settling, to the silence that followed as if the room had been waiting for her to arrive.

The room had no ceiling.

Mara could see the dark above her, though she knew there should have been stone there. It stretched upward without distance, a depth of black that swallowed the torchlight before it could find an edge. The six circles were cut into the floor around her, each one wide enough for a child to sit inside. Their grooves had been filled with something that shone like wet ink.

At the centre stood the healer.

He wore the face she had seen before: long, exhausted, almost kind. His sleeves were rolled to the elbow. There was a dark stain on one cuff. A narrow glass vial rested between his fingers, and inside it something pale drifted slowly from end to end.

“You came back,” he said.

“I didn’t come here.”

“No.” He looked at the circles. “You were brought.”

The answer settled over her like the cold after a fever. Mara turned toward the way she had entered. There was no doorway. Only a wall of old stone, smooth as a sealed tomb. She put her palm against it. The black beneath her skin—the dose, the thing the dose had let into her—stirred, eager to show her a route. For a moment, a thin green line appeared under the stone.

Then it vanished.

Mara’s hand dropped. The cut in her palm had opened again. Blood welled along the crease and ran toward her wrist.

The healer watched her. “You can make the room answer.”

“You said that before.”

“I said you could learn.”

“You said you could give me control.”

“I can.”

A faint movement stirred in the dark above. The healer did not look up. Mara thought she heard a breath, then understood it was the sound of her own lungs trying to keep pace with fear.

The vial in his hand held the same cloudy substance she had swallowed. The second dose had shown her paths hidden in stone and turned the castle’s silence into a language she could almost understand. It had also made the black less frightening. Worse: it had made it seem useful.

She hated how much she wanted to take the vial.

“What do you want?” she asked.

“To be allowed to finish what was interrupted.”

“By the children?”

“By fear.”

The healer’s face changed. It did not distort; the features simply grew more exact, as if something behind them had remembered how to wear a person. The lines beside his mouth deepened. His eyes seemed too dark to reflect the torch.

“I wanted to cure them,” he said. “The black had entered the walls. It was in the water, the mortar, the cracks between one room and the next. The dose let them see what the castle hid. I thought sight would give them a way out.”

“And instead?”

“It gave the thing a way in.”

The honesty unsettled her more than a denial would have.

“What were you trying to call?”

“Something that could take the black away.”

“Did you know what would answer?”

“No.” He looked down at the vial. “I wanted to be right badly enough to stop asking.”

The words might have been a confession. They might have been bait. Mara could no longer tell the difference. The entity had learned the shape of regret; it could speak in the voice of remorse as easily as it had spoken in her mother’s.

“You’re not him,” she said.

“Not anymore.”

The torch guttered.

For an instant, she saw another figure behind the healer: a woman in a plain dress, one hand lifted as though to shield someone smaller. Her face was blurred by the dark. A silver thimble glimmered at her finger.

Mara took it from her pocket. Ruth’s thimble. The metal was cold, but as she held it up, the figure in the room sharpened.

The woman lowered her hand.

“Aveline?” Mara asked.

The healer turned his head toward the figure. “She chose.”

“Chose what?”

“To be the door.”

The woman’s outline flickered. A pale scar crossed one cheek. Mara had seen the mark in the records, in a photograph so damaged it had looked like a scratch in the paper.

“You were the one she left,” Mara said.

The woman’s eyes fixed on her. Her mouth moved, but no sound came.

The six circles caught the torchlight one by one. In the first, Mara saw a child crouched with both hands over his ears. In the second, a girl with a bandage around her head. In the third, two small shoes side by side. A fourth circle held only a dark stain. The fifth reflected the healer. The sixth reflected Mara.

The figures appeared and disappeared with the light. Not ghosts, she thought. Not exactly. The room had kept their shapes, or the entity had learned them from whatever had happened here.

“How many children?” she asked.

“Six were brought below,” the healer said. “Three received the dose. They could see the paths through the black. They could see the way out.”

“But the thing could see them.”

“Yes.”

“And Aveline stayed.”

“She held the opening while the others crossed.”

The woman’s face trembled. Mara thought she saw a second figure standing just beyond her, half hidden in the dark: a girl, or a younger woman, holding a hand out toward Aveline.

“Ruth,” Mara whispered.

The name moved through the room. The circles answered with a low vibration. The black in their grooves shivered.

Aveline’s form became clearer, and with it the younger figure. Ruth looked no older than the children in the circles, but the room had worn the years from her in strange ways. Her hair hung in a braid. Her eyes were fixed on Aveline, full of a question that had lasted too long to remain a question.

“You left her,” Mara said.

Aveline nodded once.

“Why?”

“To save the others.” The words were faint, as if she had not spoken in years. “The door was closing. I could hold it, or follow.”

“And you chose to hold it.”

Aveline looked at Ruth. “I thought there would be time.”

“What would you have lost if you’d taken her with you?” Mara asked. “Not what the others would have lost. You.”

Aveline’s gaze stayed on Ruth. “The opening. The others’ chance to cross. And the person I thought I had to be.”

Ruth’s expression did not soften. “You could have asked me what I was willing to lose.”

Ruth did not look away.

The silence that followed was not forgiveness. It was worse: the truth left untouched.

Ruth’s fingers curled at her sides. “You told me to wait.”

Aveline’s eyes closed. “I know.”

“You said you would come back.”

“I thought I could.”

“You heard the door close.”

“I did.”

The admission passed through the room without echo. Aveline did not reach for Ruth. Mara saw the effort in that restraint, the way Aveline held herself as if any gesture could become another demand.

Ruth looked down at the thimble. “I kept it because you said it would help me mend the hem. I thought you would need it when you came back.”

Aveline’s face crumpled, but no tears fell. “I should have told you the truth.”

“You should have taken me with you.”

“Yes.”

The answer did not repair anything. It changed nothing in the room, yet the black in the circle nearest Ruth drew back by a finger’s width, as if the words had loosened something the silence had held tight.

Mara lowered the thimble. “And my family?”

The healer lifted the vial. “Your family kept the line.”

“What line?”

The floor’s circles brightened. Between them, narrow seams of silver light appeared, joining the grooves into a pattern beneath the stone. A seventh line ran from the wall behind the last circle to the centre of the room, where it joined the others. Mara saw it now—not with her eyes alone. She felt it in the old ache of her wrist, in the blood beating under the cut, in the way the room’s attention tightened when she stood near the wall.

The line ended at her.

Not her body. Her name.

“Hallowell,” she said.

The healer smiled with the wrong mouth. “A fixed line. A name entered in the record so the threshold could be held shut.”

Mara’s stomach clenched. “My family did this?”

“Your family was used.” Aveline’s voice came from the dark. “The name was written down. Passed on. Remembered.”

“I didn’t agree.”

“No,” she said. “You were born into the answer.”

The words made the offer in the healer’s hand flare with sudden appeal. If the line had been forced on her, then perhaps taking power from it was not theft. Perhaps she could use what had been done to her. She could make the castle open paths instead of closing them. She could learn the language in the walls, turn the black aside, never again be at anyone’s mercy.

The healer held out the vial.

“Take it,” he said. “The line is already yours. Let it be useful.”

The offer found the fear she had been trying not to name. She was afraid the entity would strip away the power she had begun to gain—the strength that had carried her through the castle, the courage that had let her keep moving when every sensible part of her wanted to run. She feared being left with nothing but the old helplessness, at someone else’s mercy again.

Mara stared at the pale drift in the glass. She remembered the hidden path through the infirmary, the rush of knowing where to step. She remembered the black recoiling when she pressed the serpent button, and the fierce satisfaction of making it move. The feeling had not been ordinary courage. It had been command. The castle had seemed to hesitate before her.

She wanted that again.

Her fingers twitched toward the vial.

Then she saw the circles as they had been: six children put in six places, each one turned into a measure, a use, a chance to make something work. The healer’s regret had not prevented him from repeating the choice. It had only taught him better words for it.

“Give me the vial,” Mara said.

The healer’s expression softened. “You understand.”

He held it out. The glass caught the torchlight, and the pale substance inside gathered into a bead, rolling up the inner wall against gravity. Mara did not move toward it. The healer’s free hand opened, palm up, in a gesture that might have been an offer or a demand.

“Not to drink,” she said. “You want me to hold the line.”

“I want you to see what you can do with it.”

The circles flared. One of the child-shapes in the first circle jerked upright. Its hands fell from its ears. The darkness poured over its shoulders, and the small figure turned its blank face toward Mara.

She stepped back. The circle’s black groove rippled. The shape lurched after her, but could not cross the silver seam.

“Stop it,” Mara said.

“I can’t.”

“You made this.”

“I made the dose. I opened a way. The thing in the room is not mine.” He watched her carefully. “But you can make it stop.”

The child-shape struck the invisible boundary. A crack of light snapped through the circle. Mara’s pulse jumped. She could feel the route beneath the floor, a pressure running from the wall through the centre and branching toward each ring. She knew—without knowing how—that if she pulled at it, the dark would obey.

The thought thrilled her.

She raised her bleeding hand. The shadow recoiled from the circle’s edge.

For a single, intoxicating moment, she felt the room yield. The black withdrew from the silver seam. The child-shape fell back, and the circle went quiet.

Then the other five rings answered. Their grooves filled with lightless movement. Something pressed against each boundary from below. The force that had obeyed her in one place had carried her attention through the whole pattern, and now the room knew exactly where she was standing.

Pain lanced from Mara’s palm up her arm. She dropped her hand.

The healer lowered the vial. “You felt it.”

Mara clutched her wrist. “You used me.”

“I showed you what was already there.”

“You knew it would spread.”

“I knew you would want to stop it.” His voice softened again. “You could learn to hold all six. You could keep the black from reaching anyone else.”

The six circles throbbed, each pulse drawing a little more warmth from the room. The child-shapes had vanished. In their place, thin black tendrils slid over the grooves, searching for a seam.

Mara forced herself to look away from the healer’s offered vial. “What happens if I take it?”

“You stop being helpless.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

“No,” he said. “It is the one you came here wanting.”

“I understand you want me to choose it.”

“I want you to choose what you need.”

“You want the line to hold.”

“I want the door open.”

The room gave a single, deep shudder. Dust fell from the impossible dark above. The circles brightened until Mara could see the marks cut into their edges: names, most worn away, some scratched out. One name remained clear beside the sixth circle: RUTH.

The silver thimble warmed in Mara’s hand.

The false workroom appeared behind the healer. A door opened in the far wall, and beyond it lay a narrow room with green bottles on shelves, clean parchment on a desk, a brass lamp burning without flame. Snape’s handwriting covered a sheet at the centre of the desk. She could not read the words, but she knew the shape of them—the sharp angles, the impatient strokes. The room looked like everything she had imagined when she first came north: proof, instruction, the beginning of power.

A voice, low and dry, came from beyond the open door.

“Access is not mastery.”

Snape’s warning. Or the entity’s imitation of it.

Mara did not step through.

The workroom light spilled over the floor and stopped at the line of black between her and the doorway. The healer watched her choose not to cross.

“You came all this way for what he left,” he said.

“I did.”

“You wanted his magic.”

“I wanted what I thought it would let me do.”

“And now?”

“Now I want this to end.”

“Those are not always different things.”

Mara looked again at the silver lines in the floor. The seventh line joined the wall to the centre, and the centre to her. At the edge of the circle, the seam met the stone where the black had kept its distance.

The wall behind the last circle looked different from the others.

She crouched, keeping the knife ready, and brushed grit from the lowest block. A metal corner showed beneath the dust. A shallow recess had been cut into the stone and covered with a thin plate of tarnished brass. No handle, no keyhole—only a worn silver S.

Snape.

The empty cylinder in her pocket seemed to grow heavier. She took it out. Its seal was broken; the note had been read, and the paper burned. She turned it in her hand and found a narrow notch at its base. The notch matched a raised catch beneath the S on the brass plate.

Mara pressed the cylinder’s edge into the catch.

For a moment, nothing happened. Then the plate clicked inward.

The black along the floor’s edge shivered.

She pulled the plate free. Inside lay a packet wrapped in oilskin, no larger than her palm. Its string was tight and brittle. Mara glanced at the healer.

“You came prepared,” she said.

His face shifted in the dark. “He came late.”

“Maybe he knew better than to come.”

She loosened the string.

The first sheet was a diagram of the Sixth Place: six circles, a narrow passage, the door, and a line drawn from the wall to the room’s centre. Snape had written measurements and dates in a cramped, severe hand. Several lines were crossed out so forcefully the nib had torn the paper.

A folded scrap slid from between the pages and landed near the black seam.

Mara lunged for it. The dark licked over the stone where her fingers had been a heartbeat earlier. She snatched the scrap back, scraping her knuckles against the floor.

It was a rubbing of the circle nearest the wall. Snape had marked a tiny break in its outline and drawn an arrow toward the recess. Beside it he had written: **A gap is not an exit. It is where the line can be cut.**

On the next page, a sentence was underlined twice:

> **The family is not the lock. The line is. Do not mistake inheritance for consent.**

Mara read it again. It did not tell her what her family had done or why the room remembered them. It named the danger without offering a way around it.

At the bottom of the page, Snape’s handwriting changed. The strokes were heavier, the ink pressed deep into the fibres.

> **There is no safe use of the Sixth Place. There are only uses whose cost has not yet arrived.**

She folded the sheet and put it inside her coat.

The healer watched her. “He could have given you the power to command it.”

Mara looked at the break in Snape’s rubbing. “He left a record of what he tried.”

“He failed.”

“Maybe.”

“You think a map will save you?”

“No.” She rose, clutching the diagram. “I think it tells me where to cut.”

A faint scrape travelled through the wall. Somewhere beyond it, metal touched stone: one low, distant note. Mara flinched. Her hand went to the empty place where the bell had been. She had left it behind in the shaft, wrapped and silent. She had thought leaving it meant refusing the thing that wanted to follow her.

Snape’s notes offered no answer. The bell had already given her one warning of its own: **If it is heard, do not answer it.** Beside the diagram, Snape had added no rule, only a blunt mark through the line leading from the wall to the centre. He had tried to break the connection here—or had at least found where it ran.

Mara looked from the mark to the silver seam in the floor. The bell’s distant note faded, leaving the question of who had made it unanswered.

The healer took a step toward her. “You have what you came for. The record. The path. The power. You can stop being a name someone else wrote down.”

Mara felt the offer settle in the part of her that had always kept count: the times she had been moved, left, outnumbered, told to wait. She had crossed an ocean and a country to find something that could make the world answer when she spoke.

She still wanted it.

The want did not vanish because she understood it. It remained in her like hunger.

“I won’t become the next fixed point,” she said.

The healer’s face emptied of kindness. “Then the line remains.”

“Not if I cut it.”

Aveline looked at the broken mark on Snape’s diagram. “You cannot cut it with a knife.”

Mara glanced at the blade in her hand. “What can?”

“Your name.”

The answer was not a spell. It was a cost.

The silver lines in the floor brightened. The seventh line tightened from the wall to the centre, and from the centre toward Mara’s wrist. Beneath her skin, the dose pulsed. The dark in her veins—whether real or imagined—stretched toward the line as if it recognized its own path.

The healer’s hand closed around the vial. “You can still take the offer.”

The false workroom waited behind him, its warm lamp and quiet promise. Mara heard paper rustle inside. A page turning. Snape, or the thing that wanted her to follow, waiting for her to come close enough to mistake invitation for permission.

She turned away from it.

At the wall, the gap in the circle was small: no more than the width of her thumb. The black avoided it. Mara pressed Snape’s diagram against the stone, aligning the marks. The line in the drawing ran from the wall to the centre of the room, then toward the place where she stood.

She understood the geometry. She did not understand the magic.

Perhaps that was enough.

She pressed her already bleeding palm to the gap in the circle. The silver line beneath the floor answered, brightening from the wall to the centre, then drawing taut toward her wrist. The cut stung. Mara held her hand there and felt the room pull at her name.

The room lurched. The healer cried out—not in pain, but fury. The black surged from the grooves, racing toward the centre. Aveline stepped between it and Ruth. Ruth reached for her, and this time Aveline did not turn away.

Mara’s name rose in her throat. She could feel the room waiting for it, ready to use it as a knot. If she spoke Hallowell, the line would tighten. If she let the name stand as it had been recorded, the Sixth Place would keep its hold.

She could not unmake the past. She could refuse to let it decide what she did next.

“Ruth Vane,” Mara said.

The silver thimble burned cold against her fingers.

The name struck the room harder than a shout. The sixth circle filled with light. Ruth turned from Aveline toward Mara. The line from the wall faltered.

“Ruth Vane,” Mara repeated. “You were left here. You were not the lock.”

Ruth’s outline trembled. For the first time, she looked away from Aveline—not in anger, but to see the room that had held her. The circle beneath her cracked from edge to edge.

The healer lunged for Mara. Aveline caught his arm. Her fingers passed through his sleeve, but the gesture stopped him. The face he wore split for a moment, showing something beneath: not a man, not a skull, but a depth with no features at all.

“You cannot release what you do not understand,” it said.

“I understand enough.”

Mara drew the knife across the silver filament. The blade scraped against something she could not see. The filament snapped.

The room gave a sound like a bell struck underwater. The note passed through the floor and climbed the walls. Somewhere above them, stone answered with a long, grinding groan. The six circles flashed—not silver now, but a hard, exhausted white—and the black surged inward, folding toward the centre as if the grooves had become a drain. The false workroom went dark.

The healer staggered. The vial slipped from his fingers and broke against the floor. Its cloudy contents spread in a thin shining pool. “It was made from the water under the west stair,” he said, as if the confession had been shaken loose with the glass. “I boiled it with powdered moonstone and the black scraped from the patients’ lungs. I thought I could make the sickness visible.”

The black in the pool crawled toward Mara’s bleeding hand.

Aveline pulled Ruth against her. For a breath, the two women stood together—not forgiven, not whole, but no longer divided by the room’s demand.

Then the line from the wall broke.

The castle shifted around them. A crack opened across the ceilingless dark; grit and small stones fell from nowhere. The floor tilted, and the black in the circles changed direction, retreating from the broken seam in narrow, twitching streams. A deep report rolled through the walls, followed by the slam of distant doors. Dust clouded the torchlight. Mara caught herself against the wall as the room lurched again.

Every circle went dark.

Mara fell to one knee. The knife clattered away. Her wrist bled freely. The silver thimble rolled across the stone and stopped at Ruth’s feet.

The healer’s body came apart at the edges. His coat dissolved into a scatter of grey ash. His face remained longest, its expression not hatred now but astonishment.

“I was trying to make it stop,” he said.

“I know.”

“Do you?”

Then he was gone.

The room did not collapse. It loosened.

The impossible ceiling lowered into darkness; the six circles became shallow marks in the floor. The sudden quiet rang in Mara’s ears. She tried to stand and nearly fell. Her wounded wrist had gone numb, and the blood on her palm had dried in a dark crust. She pressed the heel of her other hand against the cut, breathing through the pain until the room stopped tilting.

The diagram lay beside her. The paper had split along its fold, and one edge was scorched black. She reached for it, but the sheet dragged against the stone as if caught beneath something. For one frightened instant she thought the line had caught it. Then the paper came free. The silver ink on the drawing had faded to grey.

Somewhere above, stone shifted against stone. A narrow door appeared in the wall where Mara had entered—or where she had first been brought. Its outline glimmered with a weak green light.

Aveline stooped and picked up the thimble. She held it toward Ruth. Ruth did not take it at first. Then she closed her fingers around the silver.

“Can you leave?” Mara asked.

Aveline looked toward the door. “I can try.”

Ruth stood beside her, one hand still wrapped around the thimble. Her shape wavered, thin as smoke in wind. Mara could not tell whether she was free, or whether the room had merely lost its grip for a moment.

“Come with me,” Mara said.

Aveline studied her. “You cannot carry us.”

“I can walk.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“No,” Mara said. “It isn’t.”

The door opened a hand’s width. Beyond it lay a sloping passage, broken stone and darkness. No false green light. No neat route drawn for her. Just a way out, if it was one.

Mara retrieved the knife and wrapped her injured wrist in a strip torn from her sleeve. She kept Snape’s diagram and the family page against her chest. The cylinder was empty. The bell was gone. The serpent button was missing, lost somewhere in the castle’s passages. The room had taken its price, though she did not yet know how much.

At the threshold, she looked back once.

The six circles held no figures. The healer was gone. The black had withdrawn from the stone, leaving a damp sheen in the grooves. The place looked abandoned again, but Mara did not mistake emptiness for safety.

She stepped through the door.

The passage climbed toward the ruined heart of Hogwarts. Aveline followed, then Ruth, whose outline flickered whenever the torch flame bent. The stone pressed close around them. Once, Mara heard a scrape behind the wall. Once, a whisper spoke her surname in a voice that might have belonged to no one.

She did not answer.

The passage narrowed at a collapsed arch. Broken blocks filled it from floor to shoulder height, leaving a gap no wider than Mara’s body. She set the torch down and tried the nearest stone. It shifted under her hand, grinding against the block beneath it.

She unfolded Snape’s diagram against the wall. The route ended at this arch, marked with a small cross and two short strokes. At first the marks looked like another measurement. Then she noticed the tiny arrow beside them, pointing down and left. The pencilled note was nearly rubbed away: **LOW BLOCK IS A WEDGE. LIFT THE STONE ABOVE BEFORE YOU PULL.**

“Don’t,” Aveline said.

Mara froze. A thread of black shone in the crack under the stone, thin as a root. It flexed toward her fingers.

The dose stirred in her blood, offering the old green route. She could almost see a path through the rubble, a way to pull the stones aside without touching them. The idea came with the familiar promise of ease—and the memory of the six circles answering when she had tried to command them.

She withdrew her hand.

“Can we go around?” she asked.

Aveline looked back down the passage. The darkness behind them had begun to fill with a pale, shifting mist. “Not now.”

Mara braced her boot against the lowest block and tested it from the side, where the black could not reach. The diagram’s arrow had shown her what she had missed: the low block was not the obstruction but the wedge holding the upper stones in place. She found the smaller piece lodged above it, eased it loose first, and lowered it without letting it fall. Only then did she lever the wedge sideways. The stone rocked once and slid free. Dust filled her mouth. The passage groaned.

Ruth stood close behind Aveline, one hand closed around the thimble. She did not flicker while the stone moved. Mara could not tell whether that meant the thimble anchored her or whether she was simply watching.

The smaller block came free. Mara passed it back. Aveline took it, though her fingers barely seemed to touch the stone. Together they cleared a gap wide enough for Mara to turn sideways and squeeze through. The torn sleeve at her wrist caught on a jagged edge. She pulled free, leaving a strip of cloth behind.

On the other side, something breathed in the wall.

Mara lifted the torch. The flame leaned toward a hairline crack in the stone. A small pale hand pressed against it from within, fingers splayed. It withdrew before she could speak.

She kept moving.

At the top of the passage, the castle opened around her. The corridor was familiar and changed: fallen plaster, a staircase split down its centre, pale daylight coming through a roof broken by the war. Dust lay over the stones in a thin grey skin. Beyond a high window, the grounds rolled north into fog.

Mara leaned against the wall until the shaking in her legs eased.

The castle smelled of wet stone, old smoke, and the bitter green of crushed leaves. Somewhere far above, a draught moved through a broken window. The torch flame bent toward it, then straightened. Ordinary things, she told herself. A damaged roof. A change in the air. The body’s need to make a pattern out of fear.

Her wrist throbbed beneath the makeshift bandage. When she loosened the cloth to check the cut, a thin silver line lay across the skin, no deeper than a thread’s impression. She rubbed it with her thumb. It did not fade. The cut would heal; the silver line would remain, a scar left by the severed connection.

She wrapped it again.

Aveline stood near the window, staring out at the grounds. Ruth remained close, the thimble enclosed in her hand. In the daylight their outlines were less distinct. Aveline’s shadow reached the floor. Ruth’s did not. When Mara looked away and back, Ruth’s face had changed—not younger or older, but briefly her own, no longer fixed in the question she had carried.

Mara wanted to ask if that meant she was free. She did not. She had spent too much of the night demanding answers from people who had been made into answers already.

She had come looking for Snape’s power. She had found a warning, a failed map, and proof that the castle could preserve a name long after the people who bore it were gone. It was not enough to answer what Snape had truly left behind. His workroom remained sealed somewhere beneath the collapsed passage, or perhaps it had never been where she thought it was.

The answer would have to wait.

Mara pushed away from the wall and started north.

A bell rang after she had crossed the outer grounds.

One clear note drifted from behind her—or seemed to. The ruined castle stood dark against the sky. Mara stopped. Aveline stopped with her. Ruth’s shape flickered almost out of sight, then steadied.

Mara did not turn around.

The note thinned into the mist. For a moment there was only the wind moving through the broken towers.

Then another tone sounded somewhere ahead, or far to the side. Lower, more distant, and not quite the same. It might have been an answering bell. It might have been the first note carried strangely through the ruins.

Mara’s injured hand closed around the empty cylinder in her pocket. The metal was cold. Ahead, the northern road disappeared into fog.

A sting crossed the inside of her wrist. She stopped and pulled the bandage back. The permanent silver scar remained unchanged. Beneath it, black gathered into a pinprick, then spread into three tiny grooves—like a mark pressed from the other side of the skin. This second mark faded when she rubbed it, but the skin stayed cold.

From somewhere behind her, too far away for a voice to carry, came the healer’s whisper: “You can still hear me.”

Mara wrapped the wrist again. She could not tell whether the words had crossed the distance, or whether fear had learned to speak in his voice. Either way, the mark was real, and so was the road ahead. She had lost the certainty that leaving meant being free. She had not lost the strength to choose her next step.

She kept walking.
