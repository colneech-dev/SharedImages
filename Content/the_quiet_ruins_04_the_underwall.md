## Chapter Four: The Underwall

The passage narrowed after the first bend. Mara had to turn her shoulders to keep the rucksack from scraping both sides, and the ceiling sank until she walked with her head bowed. The stones were slick with a film that shone faintly under her phone light. Here and there, iron staples had been driven into the wall, their ends furred with rust. They might once have held cables or lanterns. One still held a curl of blackened wire, twisted around the stone as if something had tried to pull it free.

She kept one hand against the wall and the other on the strap of her pack. The stream ran somewhere below her boots, no longer visible but audible as a thin, irregular trickle. Every few steps it seemed to move away from her, then return beside her again. The passage did not descend so much as bend inward, following the slope beneath the outer wall.

Mara stopped and looked back. The entrance was a grey wedge at the top of the stairs. Her chalk arrow remained visible on the terrace above, small but distinct. The strip of scarf at the projecting stone moved once in the wind. She could still go back. The sight of that ordinary scrap of fabric was almost enough to make the decision for her.

Then the bell sounded again.

One low strike travelled through the stones. It was not loud, but she felt it in her teeth. The stream trembled, and the sound of water vanished for a moment. Mara waited. She remembered the cottage instruction: second bell, take the lower path. This was the second bell, if the first had been the sound from the terrace. The instruction had been written for children in wartime, in a passage built to lead people somewhere. That did not mean it was meant for her.

There were two routes. The stair continued ahead and sloped down. A smaller opening, almost hidden behind a fall of stones, led to the left. She had passed it on the way in without noticing. A metal sign had been fixed above it, but its lettering was obscured by mineral deposits. She wiped it with her sleeve.

**LOWER TRANSFER—KEEP MOVING.**

The sign had a small arrow pointing into the left-hand opening.

Mara’s pulse beat hard in her throat. She made herself inspect the passage before choosing. There was a draught from the left, cool and carrying a smell like wet leaves. The main tunnel smelled of old iron and candle smoke. She could hear nothing in either direction. The elf had said black water meant stop, clear water meant don’t. The runnel was out of sight now. The wartime note said not to send anyone back. She could not tell whether she was following an instruction or letting the place direct her.

She took the lower route.

The opening scraped her shoulders. Beyond it, the corridor widened and levelled. Its walls were brick rather than stone, laid in hurried uneven courses. Some bricks had been marked with white chalk: short strokes in groups of five, then a space, then another set. Mara counted them as she passed. Thirty-five. Then a blank stretch. Then eleven. The marks might have been a tally of people or steps between turns. She did not touch them.

The smell changed. Beneath the dampness was smoke, old and sour, with a sharper scent of burned hair. Mara stopped. Her phone beam picked out a soot smear across the ceiling and the blackened remains of a wooden frame. The passage had been scorched here. On the floor, a narrow line of glassy slag ran from one wall to the other, glossy as frozen water.

She crouched. A small brass buckle lay partly embedded in the slag. It was too small for a belt, perhaps from a child’s shoe. The metal had melted at one edge. Mara felt the urge to pick it up, to add it to the growing inventory of things she was collecting as if objects could make the past intelligible. She stopped herself. The buckle had survived better than the person who wore it might have.

A faint sound came from ahead: a shuffle, then a whisper of cloth against brick.

Mara turned off her phone light.

The dark pressed down around her. The sound came again. A slow drag, followed by a pause. She held her breath, and in the pause she heard another noise—something breathing, but not close enough to locate. She eased the knife from her belt. The small blade was ridiculous against whatever might live under Hogwarts, yet the weight of it in her hand steadied her.

“Who’s there?” she said.

Her voice travelled ahead and came back flattened. No answer followed.

The scrape continued, now moving away. Mara waited until it faded, then switched her light back on. The glassy trail across the floor reflected the beam in a narrow, wavering line. For a moment, she thought she saw a shape standing at the far end of the passage. It vanished when she blinked.

She went on.

The corridor ended at a door of dark oak reinforced with iron strips. A circular plate had been fixed at eye level, bearing the same three-stroke sign she had seen on the cottage. This mark was scratched deep into the metal, not painted. Below it, someone had cut a small square, perhaps where a lock had once been. The keyhole was too narrow for the brass key around the elf’s neck, if she had ever been foolish enough to think it might be meant for this.

Mara pushed the door. It did not move.

She searched the frame for a handle and found a ring on the left side, cold and slick. She pulled. The door shifted half an inch and stopped. Something on the other side held it closed.

“Hello?”

The word seemed to disappear into the wood.

She leaned her shoulder against it and tried again. The door gave slightly, then a weight pressed back from within. Mara stumbled away. A low vibration passed through the iron bands and into her palm. It felt less like a person pushing and more like the door itself had tightened.

She remembered the button in her pocket. She unwrapped the grey cloth carefully. The brass serpent caught the phone light, its coiled body polished by wear. The three dark circles had not appeared on the button; they remained impressed in the cloth. She held the token near the scratched sign.

The door answered with a soft, single click.

Mara pulled her hand back. The click had come from inside the wood, close to the square cut into the plate. She looked at the serpent button. Its mouth was open, a small black notch between the fangs. The elf had called it a door, then a mouth. She had thought the phrase was a warning about what it could open. Now she wondered whether it meant the object was itself an opening, or something that had been made to speak to another opening.

She did not press it against the door. Not yet.

Instead she examined the iron strips. They were secured with square-headed nails, each one darkened with age. At the lower edge, a thin line of pale dust had collected in the gap. It lay undisturbed except for one small track: three parallel grooves, running from beneath the door toward the wall.

Mara backed away.

The mark matched the wounds on the hippogriff. The same grooves had appeared in the cloth. Whatever had made them might be on the other side—or might have passed through this place long ago. She imagined the creature with the wrapped head, standing below the terrace. She imagined its long pale burden. She tried not to imagine what it had carried.

A whisper came from behind the door.

“Not yet.”

It was not the cottage voice. This one was lower, dry, and interrupted by static-like crackling. It sounded as if spoken through a wall from a great distance. Mara stepped close enough to hear, but not close enough to touch the wood.

“Who said that?”

Nothing.

She waited for a second phrase, a clue, a name. The passage gave her only the small run of water somewhere below. If the voice had been a warning, she had no way to know whether it had come from a person, an echo, or the structure of the tunnel itself.

Mara put the button away and tried the door again. It stayed firm. The sign gave no hint of a latch, and the square cut in the plate was too small to reach through. She could force it, but the door looked old and the passage beyond seemed more dangerous than the passage she occupied. The lower transfer route had brought her here; perhaps it continued elsewhere.

She moved along the wall. Behind a broken section of brick, she found a recess no wider than a cupboard. Inside lay an empty oilskin pouch and a folded strip of canvas. The canvas had been used as a map. Most of its ink was gone, but a line marked the outer wall and a second line crossed it at an angle. The two met at a small drawing of a serpent. Beneath it was written **S.S.—stores, not sanctuary**.

Mara’s attention sharpened. Those initials could have meant anyone. But she knew what she wanted them to mean. The map showed a narrow route running from the underwall passage toward a place labelled **old stores**. The notation had been crossed out in red, then rewritten in another hand: **sealed after breach**.

She folded the canvas and placed it in her notebook. It was the first evidence that might lead directly to Snape’s old spaces, but it was also a warning that the route had been sealed after something got through. Mara could not tell whether the breach had been caused by the war, by a creature, or by the castle’s own defences failing.

There was a second scrap in the recess, a list of supplies in a precise, narrow hand. Most entries were ordinary: salt, bandages, lamp oil, dried nettle. At the bottom, written in a different ink, was a single line: **Do not store names with the ingredients.**

Mara read it twice. The wording did not make sense, but she remembered the elf’s warning. No name. It might have been practical advice about some magical process. It might have been superstition. She left the scrap where it was and stepped away from the recess.

A steady knocking began somewhere above her.

Three knocks, a pause, then three more. The sound travelled through the ceiling, too measured to be loose stone. Mara looked up. Dust sifted down around her, catching the phone beam like grey snow.

She moved quickly toward the end of the passage, where the map suggested a junction. The knocks followed. At the junction, she found two narrow tunnels and one broad archway blocked by fallen masonry. The narrow tunnel to the right sloped sharply upward. The one to the left ran level beside the stream. Water gleamed in the left-hand channel, but it was not transparent; its surface looked black, as if the light were being absorbed.

Mara stopped. Black water meant stop.

The knocking above ceased.

She did not move. In the glossy darkness of the runnel, her reflection appeared upside down. Her face looked pale and distorted, the phone beam making her eyes seem empty. Then something behind her reflection moved across the water.

Mara spun around. The tunnel behind was empty.

A cold thread of air touched the back of her neck. She kept still until it passed. The water remained black, its surface unbroken. She understood the instruction as a command, not a riddle: do not take the passage beside the black stream. She turned to the upward tunnel.

The climb was steep and slippery. Twice she had to use both hands to haul herself over fallen stone. The tunnel was older here, cut through bedrock rather than built in brick. Strange mineral veins crossed the walls like pale roots. They brightened faintly when the button in her pocket warmed, then dimmed when she moved away.

At the top, the passage opened into a low chamber. A wooden ladder rose through a square hatch in the ceiling. The hatch was shut, but a thin line of daylight showed around its edges. Mara set down her rucksack and listened. Above it, she heard no voices or movement. She pushed against the hatch with both hands. It lifted a fraction, then stopped against something heavy.

She climbed two rungs and tested it again. A shower of grit fell through the gap. On the underside of the hatch, someone had carved words into the wood: **IF YOU HEAR THE BELL, DO NOT OPEN.**

The warning made her let go.

The second bell had sounded below. If this was the lower transfer route, the hatch might lead to a shelter or store room. But whoever had carved the message had placed it where a person climbing from beneath would see it. It was not a general warning. It was written for someone who had already come this far and was considering going up.

Mara lowered herself from the ladder. The chamber was barely large enough to stand in. A cracked basin sat in one corner, its tap long gone. Beside it, a line of muddy footprints led from the ladder to a small door in the stone. The prints were human-shaped, though the toes were unusually long. They went to the door and stopped. None returned.

She followed the footprints with her light. At the threshold, the stone had been scratched by nails or claws. Three long grooves cut across the door, angled downward. The marks were fresh enough that pale grit still lay at their base.

The hippogriff’s wound came back to her with such force that she could almost smell the blood on its feathers. She remembered its furious eye, the beating wings, its desperate attempts to keep her away. The injuries had not been random. Something had caught it, held it, and raked those lines into its flank.

She did not touch the door.

A whisper came from behind it, nearly too faint to hear.

“Help.”

Mara’s hand tightened around the knife. The voice sounded human, but that meant nothing here. She backed from the door, keeping the light on it. A second whisper followed, separated from the first by a long pause.

“Please.”

The words were thin, exhausted, and so ordinary that her instincts urged her forward. She imagined a survivor trapped in a room beneath the castle, someone who had been there since the war. Then she remembered the elf: inside gets you. The phrase had sounded like broken English, but the fear in it had been unmistakable.

“Who are you?” Mara asked.

The answer came at once. “Elspeth.”

Mara’s stomach turned cold. The name from the scarf. The name from the evacuation list. The cottage echo had said only *You’re late*, but the rules at the cottage had warned against calling names. Had the voice answered her? No. She had asked who it was. That was enough.

She stepped back another pace.

The door shuddered once from the other side. A scraping sound ran across it, then stopped at the three fresh grooves.

Mara did not run. She forced herself to watch the door until the scrape faded. The voice did not repeat the name. The silence afterwards was not relief. It felt like something holding its breath.

She turned away and found a low stone bench beneath the ladder. A metal cylinder had been tucked under it, wrapped in waxed cloth. She drew it out carefully. It was a small case, sealed at both ends with dark wax. One side bore a silver initial, **S**. The wax carried a faint herbal scent, sharp and bitter. This could be the first real piece of Snape’s trail, or a decoy planted by someone who knew what Mara hoped to find.

She did not open it. The chamber was too confined, the door too near. She slid the cylinder into the padded pocket inside her rucksack and fastened the flap. As she did, the serpent button grew warm enough to sting through the cloth. The black runnel somewhere below began to gurgle.

Mara looked at the ladder. The hatch remained shut. She looked at the door with the claw marks. Beneath it, a thin line of water had appeared, dark as ink. It crept across the floor toward her boot.

She pulled her foot away. The water stopped.

For several seconds, nothing moved. Then the line withdrew beneath the door, as though drawn back by a mouth.

Mara forced herself to breathe slowly. She had come beneath the wall expecting an entryway. Instead she had found a sealed door, a voice with a missing child’s name, and a clue bearing Snape’s initial. The tunnel had not welcomed her. It had tested what she would answer.

She left the chamber by the upward passage, not the black-water route. At the junction, the broad archway blocked by rubble gave a low, settling groan. A stone rolled free and struck the floor. Behind the rubble, something tapped three times.

Mara did not investigate. She climbed the slope toward the hatch, pausing below it to listen. The warning remained carved into the wood: **IF YOU HEAR THE BELL, DO NOT OPEN.**

No bell sounded.

She pushed the hatch. It shifted a finger’s width, then jammed. Dust sifted down through the gap. Above, stone scraped against stone, followed by a muffled thud. The hatch settled back into place.

Mara climbed down. The ladder shook under her weight. She had no intention of forcing the hatch and bringing the ceiling down on herself. Whatever lay above could wait; the tunnel behind her was the only route she knew, and even that had already changed once.

She returned to the junction. The three taps came again from behind the rubble. This time they were followed by a soft dragging sound, as if something were moving along the far side of the blocked arch. Mara backed away, careful not to turn her back on the stones.

The passage she had entered by was gone.

Not sealed by a fresh collapse: the narrow opening had become a blank stretch of brick, damp and unbroken, with the chalk tally marks continuing across it. Mara ran her fingers over the mortar. It was cold, smooth, and perfectly set. She knew she had come through there. She could still picture the cramped turn, the scrape against her pack, the chalk marks disappearing into the dark. The passage had been there less than an hour ago.

She struck the wall once with the flat of her knife. The sound came back hollow from somewhere behind it.

A single word breathed through the brick: “Not yet.”

Mara jerked away. The voice had the same dry crackle as before, as if the words had travelled through dust and stone for years. No name. No explanation. Only that small refusal, spoken with a familiarity that made her feel expected.

She tried the other route, the steep tunnel she had climbed earlier. It curved back on itself and brought her to the same junction from the opposite side. The black-water runnel still flowed there, dark and glassy. She stopped before it, remembering the elf’s warning. On its surface she saw a reflection of the tunnel behind her—but in the reflection, the wall where the entrance should have been stood open.

A shape was crawling through it.

Mara spun around. Nothing occupied the passage. When she looked back at the water, the reflection showed only her own face, pale and strained.

She had to get away from the junction. She chose the narrow crawlspace beside the fallen arch, where the stones had shifted enough to leave a gap scarcely wider than her shoulders. The opening sloped downward. She pushed her rucksack ahead of her and wriggled after it, stone pressing into her ribs. Something brushed the sole of her boot from behind.

She kicked hard. A brittle chittering answered in the dark.

Mara scrambled forward. The gap pinched shut around her hips. For a terrible moment she could neither back out nor move ahead. Her phone light showed a scatter of pale things in the crack beside her face—small bones, each one neatly split down the middle. Something thin and jointed reached between them, feeling along the rock toward her cheek.

She shoved with both legs. The stone scraped her coat and tore a seam. The reaching limb snapped back, but not before one hooked tip caught the fabric at her shoulder. Mara twisted, yanked herself free, and slid out onto the far side in a heap of dust and grit.

The rucksack snagged. She pulled once. It held fast. From inside the gap came a wet clicking sound, then the slow, deliberate tug of the strap being drawn inward.

Mara cut the strap with her knife.

The bag came free suddenly, and she fell backward, striking her elbow on the floor. The strap remained caught between the rocks, pulled taut into the darkness. Something gave a short, furious hiss from within the crack. She did not wait to see it emerge.

The crawlspace ended in a low chamber where the wall had split diagonally. Through the fissure she saw another room beyond: shelves of dark wood, a narrow worktable, and a scatter of glass vessels glinting faintly in the beam of her phone. The air that came through the crack smelled bitter and herbal, like crushed roots and old medicine.

On the far wall, almost hidden behind a fallen beam, a silver serpent had been painted above a narrow door. Beneath it, a brass nameplate caught the light. She could make out only the last letters: **…PE**.

Mara moved closer. The chamber beyond might have been a storeroom, or a workroom. A blackened bottle lay on its side near the threshold. On the worktable rested a book, its cover secured with a strap. She could not read the writing on it, but she recognized the narrow, severe hand from the notes she had copied before the journey.

Snape’s.

The recognition sent a jolt through her so sharp that for a moment she forgot the dark, the creature in the gap, and the shifting tunnel. There it was: not a legend or a name in a notebook, but the remains of a place he might have used. A real trace, tantalizingly near.

Then the ceiling shifted.

A slab of stone dropped between Mara and the fissure, striking the floor with a crack that shook dust from every surface. The gap narrowed to a thin seam. The book and the table vanished behind falling rubble. A second crash followed, deeper in the chamber. The wall groaned as if something enormous were settling on top of it.

Mara threw herself backward. The fissure sealed under a cascade of stones. The serpent painting disappeared. The last thing she saw was a brief flash of the brass nameplate, then nothing but jagged rock and powdered mortar.

She pressed her palm to the new wall. It was solid. Her fingertips came away grey. On the other side, something rolled across the floor and stopped.

The faintest whisper slipped through the rubble. “Not yet.”

It might have been the same voice. It might have been the tunnel repeating its earlier sound. Mara could not decide whether the words referred to the book, the passage, or her. She knew only that whatever she had glimpsed was no longer reachable.

A scrape came from behind her.

At the end of the low chamber, a narrow crack opened between two stones. Something pale pushed through, first a hooked forelimb, then a folded head with no visible eyes. Its skin was almost translucent, veined with dark threads. It unfolded itself with the slow, insectile care of a thing too large for the gap, and its claws made three parallel lines in the dust.

Mara backed away. The creature’s head turned toward her, though it had no face she could see. It made the same wet clicking sound that had come from the crawlspace.

She ran.

The chamber offered two exits. The passage to the left was half blocked by a tilted slab. The right-hand opening was lower but wide enough for her, and the air coming from it smelled of cold water. She took the right, ducking beneath a broken lintel. The creature struck the stone behind her. The impact shuddered through the tunnel and sent chips scattering over her back.

The passage ran downhill and ended at a blank wall.

Mara pressed both hands against it, searching for a seam. The creature’s claws rasped behind her, closer now. She found a narrow vertical gap at floor level and dropped to her knees. The opening was barely large enough for her shoulders. She pushed the rucksack through first, then crawled after it, scraping skin from her knuckles. On the other side, the gap widened into a low drainage run where black water lapped at the stones.

She did not step into it. She pulled herself along the dry edge, keeping one hand above the water and the other on the wall. The creature struck the gap behind her. Its forelimb slid through, clawing at the stone. The hooked tip scraped along her boot and tore a strip from the heel.

Mara scrambled farther along the ledge. The black water beneath her moved against the slope, running uphill. She kept her eyes on the wall and did not look down again.

At last, the channel opened into a small dry pocket. The tunnel beyond was blocked by a collapse: a wall of broken stone, timber and twisted metal. A narrow wedge of grey light shone through near the top, too high and too small to climb through. The air carried a faint smell of grass. Beyond that sealed breach lay the world outside, and beyond the other direction lay the castle.

She tried the rubble. A few stones shifted, but when she pulled one free, the rest settled with a grinding rumble. She stopped at once. Somewhere above, fragments continued to fall, one after another, until the passage went quiet.

Mara sat back on her heels. Her hands were bleeding. Dust coated her tongue. The torn shoulder of her coat hung loose, and the cut rucksack strap dangled beside her. She had entered the tunnel expecting it to lead in one direction. Now the castle had folded the way behind her, the creature had found her, and the trail to Snape had appeared only long enough to be buried again.

A dim glow pulsed through a crack in the stones to her left. She leaned toward it. On the far side was a corridor lined with green-black tiles. A narrow doorway stood at its end, half hidden by a fallen arch. The view lasted only a moment before the light faded.

She could not reach it from here. The collapse between them was too deep, and the surrounding stone shifted at the smallest touch. But the corridor had been inside the castle. The glimpse of the serpent, the shelves and the handwriting had not been a trick of her imagination. Snape had been here. Or someone had arranged the room to make her believe he had.

The creature clicked again behind her, somewhere in the hidden crawlspaces. The sound travelled through the stone, answered by a series of faint taps from farther away. The tunnels seemed to be full of small movements, each one stopping when she listened.

Mara gathered her things and tested the way back. The route she had used was now blocked by the fallen slab. She followed the dry channel until it forked, then took the higher branch. It ended at a crack barely wider than her arm. She squeezed through sideways, rucksack dragged behind her by its surviving strap. The stone caught at her ribs and held. Panic rose hot in her chest. She forced herself to exhale, turned her shoulders, and slid free with a gasp.

The tunnel beyond was familiar only in fragments. A rusted staple. A line of white chalk. The remains of the scorched frame. She recognized the route, but the marks had changed: the chalk tallies now ran in the opposite direction, and a fifth stroke had been added to a group she was certain had held only four. She did not try to count them again.

When she reached the junction, the water was no longer black. It ran clear, so clear the bottom looked close enough to touch. The elf’s warning came back: when it runs clear, don’t. Mara stopped before the channel and did not cross. She searched the wall until she found a shallow shelf above the water, then climbed over it, keeping her body pressed to the stone.

On the other side, she saw the dark oak door again. A thin crack had opened between its edge and the frame. The serpent button warmed through its wrapping. From behind the door came a soft whisper, blurred and crackling, as though it were being played from a damaged recording buried somewhere in the wood.

“Not yet.”

Mara did not answer. The words offered no name, no explanation, no promise. Their slow repetition made the silence around them feel occupied.

She followed the tunnel back toward the entrance, but the route did not return her to the first stair. It ended instead at a collapsed arch where the stones left a low opening into a cramped, sloping pocket. She lowered herself through. On the far side, the air changed: less damp, faintly scented with rain and dead leaves. The passage rose for several yards and stopped at a final curtain of rubble.

Through a hand-sized gap, she could see daylight and the underside of the outer wall. She was close to the entrance she had chosen, but the collapse had filled the way back. Mara pressed her cheek near the opening and listened. No wind reached her. No birds. Only the faint ticking sound of the tunnel settling around her.

She could turn back into the dark and try another route. She could keep searching for a way in, following the serpent marks toward Snape’s lost room. Or she could force a path through the last loose stones and return to the terrace while she still knew where she was.

She was afraid. The fear was not abstract now: it had claws, weight, and the memory of its touch against her boot. She could feel it in the tremor of her hands. She wanted the open air so badly that she could almost taste it.

But she had seen the workroom. She had seen the book and the nameplate. Somewhere beyond the collapse were objects that had belonged to a man who had understood magic as discipline, leverage, survival. The thought of what they might teach her pulled at her with a force she hated and wanted all the same.

Mara held the serpent button in her palm. Its small mouth was a dark notch. She remembered the elf’s broken warning, and the voice behind the door that had told her not yet. Neither had told her to leave.

The narrow gap before her led back toward daylight. Behind her, the tunnel branched into deeper darkness. Mara stood at the broken threshold, unable to choose between the fear that had kept her alive and the power she had crossed an ocean to find.
