## Chapter Twelve: The Open Door

“**Mara Hallowell.**”

The voice came through the fog as if the stairwell had learned to speak with a mouth full of water.

Mara stopped. The bell in her pack gave a faint, answering tremor—not a ring, exactly, but the pressure of one remembered in metal. Behind her, the ghost’s footsteps had ceased. Ahead, the fog thickened over the descending steps, pale and low, crawling upward against the current of cold air.

“Who are you?” she called.

The fog did not answer. Something moved within it, a shape gathering itself out of folds of darkness. First the shoulders, then the narrow head. A strip of white cloth crossed its chest. A small badge glinted there: a bowl with a line through it.

The healer from the lower room.

Its mouth had been stitched shut with black thread. The stitches pulled at the skin when it tilted its head, though no sound came from it. It was not the ghost that had said, You brought it back. The ghost had been a woman with a translucent face and an old-fashioned collar, her expression full of grief too tired to become anger. This figure was solid enough to leave wet marks on the stone.

Mara drew the knife.

The healer lifted one hand, palm out. Its fingers were stained dark to the second knuckle. Then it pointed down the stair and made a beckoning motion.

“You want the bell.”

Its head tipped, almost a nod.

“You want me to ring it.”

This time it nodded clearly.

Mara’s fingers tightened on the knife. “What comes when I do?”

The healer’s sewn mouth twitched. The black stitches parted by a fraction. A sound like a breath pushed through a keyhole escaped it. The bell answered with another shiver, and deep below them something struck stone once.

Not a ring. A knock.

The healer’s hand opened wider. A dark line ran from its wrist to its palm, like ink beneath the skin. It beckoned again.

Mara looked at the stairs. She had seen what the first dose had done. She had felt the hidden routes open in her sight, the castle’s seams lit as if someone had drawn them in pale chalk. She had also felt the dose strip away a hesitation she had once considered part of herself. The thought of using the last drop made her mouth water.

She hated that.

The sealed silver-S cylinder pressed against her ribs through the pack. Snape’s object. The reason she had crossed an ocean, walked through a dead landscape, and entered a castle that kept saying her name. She had promised herself she would find his workroom and take whatever remained. The healer might be a danger, but it might also know the way.

“Lead,” she said.

The healer turned and descended.

Mara followed.

They went down through a narrow service stair, past doors that had been bricked shut from the other side. Behind one, something scraped once along the wall, then fell silent. Mara kept the knife ready and the bell’s cloth wrapping tied. The healer did not look back, but its steps slowed whenever she lagged. It seemed to know exactly how far she was behind.

At the bottom, the stair emptied into a corridor with a low ceiling. The air smelled of boiled linen, damp wool, and the bitter herbal scent that had followed the Snape cylinder since the underwall. A set of brass hooks ran along one wall. Most were empty. On one hung a cracked enamel basin. Its surface was crazed with fine fractures, and a brown tide mark circled the inside.

A painted sign had fallen face down. Mara turned it over with the toe of her boot.

**INFIRMARY—LOWER ACCESS**

The healer waited beside a door with no handle. A narrow opening ran from the floor to Mara’s knee, too small for a person. The door itself was scored with shallow parallel marks, as if something had dragged its nails across it from the other side.

She had been avoiding the infirmary since the first time the castle had offered it to her. A room that pretended to be a way forward was not necessarily a way forward. But behind the door, something clinked—glass against glass. Then came a quiet, familiar sound: a quill scratching over paper.

Mara’s skin tightened.

The healer tapped the door twice. The bell in her pack trembled. A seam of pale light appeared in the wall beside the door, no wider than a hairline. It had not been there a moment ago.

The first dose—or what remained of it—had taught her to see the routes hidden beneath the visible ones. The second had sharpened that sight until every crack seemed to lead somewhere. She knew she could take the passage along the seam. She could avoid the infirmary proper, whatever waited behind the scratched door, and still reach the space beyond.

She could also turn around.

Behind her lay the stair, the service court, and the long route through the lower stores. She imagined retracing it with the healer following at a polite distance. She imagined the bell ringing in her pack, or the black reaching the stair before she found the service court. The castle had already made retreat into another kind of gamble.

The healer pointed at the seam. Then it pointed at the bell.

“You need me to open something,” Mara said. “Not the door. The other thing.”

Its head lowered. The black lines under its skin shifted, branching toward its throat.

“You can’t pass the threshold.”

The healer jerked its chin toward the scratched door. Its hands curled and uncurled.

“You want to summon whatever’s on the other side.”

The stitches parted again. A thin hiss came out, and the bell answered with a soft hum.

Mara looked at the door. She had no idea what lay beyond it. The healer might be trying to release a prisoner, call an ally, or bring something into the castle. The word *summon* belonged to spells and stories, but the bell had already made its own proof. It responded to sound. It had brought something nearer.

She could refuse to help. She could also learn what the bell did while she still had a choice about how to use it.

That was how she dressed up the want.

A damp smell rose from the seam, carrying an undertone of soap and metal. It reached into a memory she had spent years keeping folded small.

The memory was not of magic. It was a Connecticut winter, the kind that turned the gutters into black teeth. Mara had been eleven. Her mother had taken the two of them to a third-floor room over a laundromat in a town whose name Mara could no longer remember without looking at a map. The landlord had promised heat. The radiators clanged but stayed cold, and the pipes froze the first week.

Her mother had boiled water on a hot plate. The room smelled of damp coats, cheap detergent, and the pennies she kept in a mug for the payphone downstairs. Mara remembered sitting on the floor in two sweaters, holding a towel around her shoulders while her mother tried to make the window close. There had been a crack in the sash. Wind pushed through it in short, steady breaths.

“Just until Friday,” her mother had said.

Mara had believed her. Then Friday came and went. The landlord stopped answering. Her mother made calls from the laundromat and came back with the same careful voice, the same promise of a better place. Mara watched her fold their clothes into a borrowed suitcase and learned that a promise was not a wall. It did not keep the cold out. It did not make the next door open.

That night, she had taken a screwdriver from a drawer and pried loose the window latch. Not because she could fix it. She wanted to see how it had been made. She wanted to know where the metal caught, what held it, and which part would move if she pushed hard enough. When her mother found her, she was sitting on the sill with the latch in her palm.

“You could have fallen,” her mother said.

“I didn’t.”

Mara still remembered the look on her mother’s face: fear, then relief, then something like anger at the relief. For years, Mara had mistaken that look for proof that she needed to become the person who knew how everything worked. If she could understand the latch, she would not have to ask anyone to open the window. If she had the right tool, she would not have to wait for Friday.

Now, in the ruined castle, she understood the same thing and chose it anyway.

She reached into the pack and took out the bottle. One drop remained. The glass was cold. The healer watched the bottle, then her face.

“You know what this does,” Mara said.

It touched its own throat, where the black veins gathered under the jaw, and shook its head.

The gesture might have meant *no*. It might have meant *not to me*. It might have been a lie.

Mara tipped the bottle. The last drop fell onto her tongue.

It tasted of burnt mint and old coins. Her mouth went numb, then painfully sensitive. The door’s scratches sharpened. The pale seam became a narrow corridor of greenish light, bending behind the wall and curving toward a space that was not on the visible floor plan. Beyond it she saw the outlines of beds, a cabinet, a hanging screen—and six circles arranged around an empty place.

Her pulse steadied. The fear did not disappear. It seemed to step aside, embarrassed to be in her way.

The healer’s hands rose, asking for the bell.

“No,” Mara said. “You don’t touch it.”

It stiffened.

She enjoyed that. The realization came with a small, bright spark of satisfaction. She enjoyed the way the creature had to wait. She enjoyed knowing it needed her more than she needed it.

The thought should have frightened her. Instead, she smiled.

Mara untied the cloth and took out Aveline’s bell. The brass was tarnished around the rim, and the little tag tied to its handle still bore the stamped name. She held it in one hand and the serpent button in the other. Its green enamel glowed faintly, reacting to the black thread in the healer’s mouth.

A scrap of paper protruded from the button’s wrapping. The note she had found before the descent: **If the sixth door is open, do not ring the bell.**

The instruction was plain. The door was not open yet, she told herself. The seam was only a path. The warning could mean something else. She had spent too long being obedient to messages left by people who were not there to explain them.

“Show me where to ring,” she said.

The healer pointed to the narrow seam. Then, with two fingers, it drew a small circle in the air.

Mara looked again at the hidden route. The six circles hovered at the edge of her sight. Five were crossed through. The sixth was open.

The warning returned, sharp and unmistakable.

She should have put the bell away. Instead, she took her knife and tapped its flat side against the brass once.

A clear note rang through the corridor.

The seam widened.

A breath of warm air flowed through it, carrying the scent of linen, soap, and something sweetly rotten. Inside the hidden passage, a sixth circle appeared. A person stood in it, motionless, their face turned away.

The healer lunged for the bell.

Mara struck it again.

The second note was lower. The floor shivered. Somewhere behind the infirmary door, glass broke. The figure in the sixth circle turned its head.

The stitched mouth of the healer split at one corner. Black thread snapped. The creature made a sound—part groan, part laughter—and shoved both hands toward the seam. It wanted the passage open. It wanted whatever stood in the sixth circle to hear the bell.

Mara finally understood the shape of its desire. It was not asking to be freed. It wanted to summon something through her.

She could still stop.

She rang the bell a third time.

The hidden route opened wide enough to admit a person. The black surged out.

It spilled along the floor, not like liquid but like a shadow that had become too heavy to stay on the wall. It came for Mara’s boots. The healer recoiled, then turned toward the seam as if it had heard a beloved voice. A whisper rolled through the corridor, too low to make out.

The brass cylinder under the infirmary bed grew suddenly hot inside her pack.

Mara stumbled backward. The bell slipped in her hand. The black reached her boot and climbed over the leather, cold as river mud. She felt it press against her ankle, searching for a name.

She pulled the serpent button from her other hand and pressed it into the black.

Green light flared.

The dark recoiled from the button in a hard, rippling wave. It did not vanish. It pulled back, gathering itself in the corridor corners, leaving the stone bare between Mara and the seam. The light formed a thin outline on the floor—an arc, then a straight line, then a narrow path that ran through the wall.

For one instant, Mara could see every route the black had taken. It had flowed from the infirmary door, but not through it. It had come from a room hidden behind the wall, where the six circles were carved into a stone floor. The path led there and farther: down through the castle’s foundations, toward a deeper chamber marked with three grooves.

The button had not merely pushed the black away. It had shown her where the black belonged.

Then it burned her.

Pain shot through her palm. The button’s enamel cracked beneath her thumb. She let go with a cry. It struck the stones and rolled into a dark corner, its green light guttering out. A blister rose across the center of her hand, already splitting at the edges. The new sight vanished with the light.

The cost was immediate. Her left hand shook; her fingers would not close around the bell. She had lost the button’s protection, perhaps for good. But she had seen a route no map could show.

The healer swung toward her. Its remaining stitches tightened, pulling the mouth into a terrible, wet smile. The black withdrew from its feet and flowed toward the hidden passage. It did not touch the creature.

“You used me,” Mara said.

The healer’s head cocked.

She felt the second dose inside her, like a cold seed. Her burned palm throbbed. The thing had caused it. The thing had brought the black to her. She could have backed away. She could have shut the seam.

Instead she stepped forward and drove the bell’s rim into the healer’s face.

The brass struck its cheekbone. The healer reeled into the wall. Its badge tore loose and clattered to the floor. Mara hit it again, harder, using the bell as a weapon. The stitches across its mouth split. A black strand snapped free and whipped her cheek, leaving a line of cold that stung like a cut.

The healer fell to one knee.

“Don’t,” it rasped through its ruined mouth.

Mara froze. The word had been human. Its voice was hoarse, thin, and very afraid.

For a moment she saw a person under the black: an old face, exhausted eyes, a healer’s hands. She could have helped it. She could have asked what it had done and what it feared. The question rose and died before reaching her tongue.

“You should have said that before you tried to get me killed.”

She kicked the badge away. It skittered under a cabinet. The healer looked up at her, and Mara saw recognition in its eyes—not of her face, but of the choice she had made.

Behind the scratched infirmary door, the quill began to write again.

Mara turned toward the seam. The sixth circle was still open. The person within it had vanished. A line of words now marked the far wall, pale as chalk.

**SHE LEFT SOMEONE BEHIND.**

Mara read the sentence twice. Then she remembered the tag she carried, the one that said **SHE DID NOT LEAVE ME**. The two messages did not fit together. They could describe different people, or different meanings of *left*. They could be a lie. Aveline might have stayed to protect someone, or chosen one person over another. The word *sacrifice* came to Mara unbidden, ugly and simple.

The record cylinder burned against her side again.

She could not leave it unopened. Not after the bell had answered. Not after the healer had tried to summon something through her. Not after Aveline’s name had appeared in a room that should not exist.

The hidden passage led around the infirmary door, avoiding the main chamber. Mara entered it despite the healer’s low, warning sound. The corridor was narrow, barely wide enough for her shoulders. Her injured hand hung uselessly. She held the bell in her right and kept the knife ready in the left as best she could.

The route opened into a cramped room lined with iron-framed beds. Their mattresses had rotted into dark hollows. A cracked mirror hung above a basin. The room looked like an infirmary that had been folded and hidden inside the wall. At its far end stood a cabinet with a door hanging open. A brass cylinder lay beneath the nearest bed, where she had glimpsed it before.

It was not the silver-S cylinder. This one was light, dull, and ribbed around its middle. A narrow slot ran along the top. A little label remained: **RECORD—SIXTH PLACE**.

Mara took it out. It had been made to hold a strip of punched brass, like a music box. She pulled the side lever. A tiny crank emerged. The cylinder clicked once, then began to turn.

A voice came from it, thin with age but unmistakably alive.

“If this is being played, the door is open. I am Aveline Vane. I chose to stay.”

Mara’s breath caught.

The record clicked, slowed, and continued.

“They told us the sixth place was empty. It was not. There was someone there, though no one could agree on a name. The healer said the dose would make us able to see it. The bell made it hear us. We were told not to ring after the second measure. We did.”

A scratch of static interrupted the voice. The cylinder turned on.

“The healer wanted it to come through. He said it could take the black away. He was wrong, or he lied. The black was already part of him. He had been keeping it in the patients and calling that treatment.”

Mara glanced toward the passage. The healer had not followed. She could hear it breathing outside, each breath wet and uneven.

Aveline’s voice came again, quieter.

“I left someone behind. I did not leave them alone. If the door opens, do not believe the first voice that calls. It will use the voice you miss most.”

The cylinder clicked three times. A final sentence emerged, badly warped.

“Tell them I chose—”

The mechanism stopped.

Mara stared at the cylinder. The recording had offered answers and opened new wounds. Aveline had chosen to stay. Someone had remained with her—or had been the price of her choice. The black was not an invading thing alone. It had been cultivated, fed, treated as a cure. The healer’s desire to summon something might have begun as a desperate effort to call help. Or the record might have been made by someone who wanted Mara to believe that.

She turned the crank backward. Nothing happened.

A crack ran along the ribbed metal. The record had nearly exhausted itself. She slid the cylinder into her pack beside Snape’s sealed one.

The note she had found earlier was tucked in the same pocket, folded around the serpent button’s cloth. She unfolded it with her good hand. Its words had not changed: **If the sixth door is open, do not ring the bell.**

She had opened it. She had rung.

The space beyond the room shifted. The six circles reappeared, faintly visible through the wall. Five crossed out. One open. At the center of the open circle, something moved.

Mara’s mother’s voice came from the far side of the room.

“Just until Friday, Mara.”

Not quite her mother. The cadence was close, the vowel in *Friday* softened in the same way, but the voice was too clear and too young. It had taken one memory and copied the surface of it. The sentence that followed was not one her mother had ever said.

“Come home.”

Mara’s hand tightened around the bell. For one dizzy instant she was eleven again, cold at the window, wanting someone to promise that the next room would be warm. She wanted the voice to be her mother. She wanted to answer.

The record’s warning held her still.

Do not believe the first voice that calls. It will use the voice you miss most.

“Not my mother,” Mara said.

The voice laughed softly. The sound was not her mother’s at all.

A narrow shelf stood beneath the mirror. On it, half-hidden under a folded strip of linen, lay a smaller object: a blackened wand fragment, no longer than Mara’s palm. A silver S had been scratched into one end. Her pulse quickened. A piece of Snape’s work, perhaps. A key, perhaps. Something he had meant to keep.

She reached for it.

The healer appeared in the passage behind her. It had recovered enough to stand. Its face was wet with black blood. It raised one hand toward the shelf, then shook its head violently.

Mara took the fragment.

The bell rang once in her hand, though she had not moved it.

The open circle brightened. A voice breathed her full name from within it.

The black surged through the narrow room.

Mara fled toward the passage with the wand fragment in her good hand. She heard the healer cry out, then the soft rush of the black behind her. The hidden path she had seen with the button was gone, but she could still feel the route in memory: an angle through the wall, a turn at the broken basin, a low arch. She followed it blindly.

At the arch, her burned hand struck the stone. Pain flashed white. The fragment slipped from her grasp and clattered down into the dark behind her.

She kept going.

The choice was made in a heartbeat: the Snape piece or the exit. Her hand reached back once, fingers spread, but the black touched the floor where it had fallen. She pulled away.

She had lost the fragment.

The passage spat her out beside the scratched infirmary door. The healer was nowhere in sight. The black pooled under the threshold, moving in slow, deliberate folds. The door itself had opened an inch. Inside, rows of beds receded farther than the castle could contain. A pale shape stood at the end of them, its head tilted as if listening.

Mara backed away.

The silver-S cylinder remained in her pack. The real Snape object. She could still open it. She could not put off the reason she had come any longer.

She took the cylinder out and examined its sealed cap. The silver S was not a decoration. It formed part of a narrow catch, hidden beneath the lip. Her injured fingers failed to grip it. She set the cylinder on the stone, braced it with her knee, and used the knife’s tip to lever the catch.

The cap released with a soft click.

Inside lay a rolled strip of dark paper and a folded note, both dry despite the damp. Mara pulled out the note first. The handwriting was precise, slanted, and impatient. She did not know Snape’s hand, but she had seen enough old labels in the stores to recognize its severity.

**If this has reached you, the lower work is no longer contained. Do not mistake access for mastery. The bell is a call, not a key. The dose is an eye, not a gift. If you have used both, then you have already made yourself part of the mechanism.**

Mara read it once, then again.

Below, in smaller writing, Snape had added:

**The sixth place was never meant to be occupied by a living person. I could not close it without destroying the record. I chose the record. That was my error.**

Her mouth went dry. Snape had known about the sixth place. He had been there, or close enough to understand what it could do. He had chosen to preserve evidence rather than end the danger.

The remaining text was on the strip inside the cylinder. Mara unrolled it carefully.

**The name beneath the name is not Aveline. Find the first record, not the last. If the door speaks in a familiar voice, answer nothing. If it offers you what you came for, leave it unopened.**

At the bottom, a final line had been written so faintly she nearly missed it.

**I have heard it say my name.**

The cylinder contained no spell, no recipe, no promise of power. It contained a warning—and evidence that Snape had stood where she stood, feared the same voice, and failed to close the door. It made the thing she most wanted feel closer than ever.

Mara folded the note and returned it to the cylinder. She had wanted Snape’s power, a tool that would let her stop asking permission. Instead he had left her a record of his failure and a direction toward another record.

The bell hummed.

The black under the door trembled. It drew itself into a thin line, then an arrow pointing toward the corridor she had just escaped. The path shown by the button, or a counterfeit of it.

The healer’s voice came from beyond the door, muffled and pleading.

“Help me close it.”

Mara did not answer. The warning on the note was plain enough. She still wanted to know what lay beyond the sixth place. She wanted to learn what Snape had found, why he had chosen the record, and what name lay beneath Aveline’s. She wanted it so much that the desire felt like another dose working through her.

She shoved the cylinder into her pack and backed toward the corridor.

The serpent button was gone. Her palm was blistered. The brass record had told her Aveline chose to stay, but not whom she stayed for. The bell had summoned something, and the healer wanted to use her to summon it again. She had gained a route through the hidden structure and lost the one object that might have shielded her from the black.

Behind the infirmary door, the beds creaked in unison.

A voice from the hidden room spoke in a tone almost like her mother’s, but not quite. It sounded older, patient, and close enough to make Mara’s throat ache.

“Friday has come.”

The bell gave one low hum.

At the far end of the corridor, the pool began turning black.
